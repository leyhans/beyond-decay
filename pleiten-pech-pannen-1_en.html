Bankruptcies, Bad Luck and Blunders (1): The Money That Doesn't Arrive

What the European Court of Auditors found on Horizon 2020, the Innovation Fund and the Innovation Council

What has the EU achieved with its billions for innovation? The European Court of Auditors has looked. The first instalment of our series.

I. Who looks

In “Two Trillion Without a Course” we described how Brussels is fighting over the size of the next EU budget while hardly anyone asks what the money is to be spent on. The counter-question is obvious: what became of the money the EU has spent on innovation in recent years?

There is one body that checks this systematically: the European Court of Auditors in Luxembourg. It has no power to forbid anything. It can only look and write down what it finds. What it wrote down in 2026 about innovation funding is the material of this instalment. It concerns three instruments with three different tasks: a research programme, a climate fund and a venture capital fund. They fail in three different ways, but according to the same pattern.

II. Seventeen billion for industrial leadership

From 2014 to 2020 the research programme Horizon 2020 ran. One part of it was called “Industrial Leadership”. It was meant to ensure that European research turns into products with which European companies earn money. Around 17 billion euros were available for this, a good fifth of the whole programme.

On 15 October 2026 the Court of Auditors presented its special report 24/2026 on it. Its verdict: the money was not sufficiently geared towards commercialisation. Almost 80 percent went into six key technologies, which matched the political guidelines. But about half went not to companies but to universities and research institutions. Non-profit participants had their costs in innovation projects reimbursed at 100 percent, companies at 70 percent. This rule still applies today in the successor programme Horizon Europe. Neither Horizon 2020 nor Horizon Europe asks, when awarding funds, how capable of innovation an applicant is. And the Commission did not track whether the funded companies actually made products from their research. In the auditors' view, its own 2024 assessment may have turned out too positive because of the assumptions and methods behind it.

Imagine a sports club that pays for training for years, accounts precisely for training hours, but never checks whether its players ever win a match. And that reimburses the amateurs' travel costs in full but the professionals' only at seventy percent. That is roughly what the funding looks like that was meant to secure Europe's industrial leadership.

III. Forty billion that do not flow out

The Innovation Fund was set up in 2020. It is meant to carry demonstration plants for clean technology across the so-called valley of death, the stretch between a working trial and the first large plant. It is fed from the sale of 530 million emission allowances. At a CO₂ price of 75 euros per tonne that is around 40 billion euros up to 2030.

In March 2026 the Court of Auditors presented its special report 11/2026. The title alone is a verdict: “High potential, but slow progress and little impact on emissions reduction”. At the end of June 2025, almost five years after the start, around 12.3 billion euros were available. 331.8 million had been paid out, 2.7 percent of that and less than one percent of the expected total funds. Of 228 selected projects, 40 had been cancelled again, 20 withdrawn before signing, 20 terminated later. 16 were in operation. About one project in five fails before it even runs.

And what the running ones achieve: by December 2024 the projects should have saved 0.59 million tonnes of CO₂. Around 5 percent of that was achieved. It is as if the weather forecast promised twenty sunny days and one came. The auditors name a reason that goes deeper than any delay: applicants calculate their future emissions reduction themselves, purely theoretically, and this calculation decides two of five selection criteria. Whoever calculates more optimistically has better chances. In addition, the Commission repeatedly adjusted calls and funds to new political priorities without structured analysis, and industry at times showed little interest.

What this looks like in detail is shown by the largest single project. The Swedish company Stegra is building a steelworks in Boden that is to run on hydrogen instead of coke. The Innovation Fund supports it with 250 million euros. Production was to begin in 2025. In autumn 2025 Stegra needed around ten billion kronor more; in spring 2026 a consortium around the Wallenberg family rescued the project with 1.4 billion euros. At the end of September 2026 Stegra reported that completion would again cost significantly more than assumed in June, and replaced its founding chief executive. Stegra is also a portfolio company of InnoEnergy, a company funded by the European Institute of Innovation and Technology, the EIT. We return to that in the third instalment.

In fairness one must say: that little has been paid out is also due to a deliberate rule. The fund only pays once a project has reached milestones, that is, once the financing is in place, the plant is running and the savings are proven. That protects the money. The auditors consider the selection proper and the plants they visited technically successful, and the Commission points out that most of the funded plants have not yet started operating at all. But that is exactly the point: a fund meant to help across the valley of death hardly gets across itself.

IV. The money that comes too late

The European Innovation Council, the EIC, was supposed to be the opposite of programmes that examine applications for years. Since 2021 it is meant to finance young technology companies directly, including with equity, with around 10 billion euros over seven years.

Then departments of the Commission quarrelled over who should manage the equity fund. Dozens of companies that had been promised an investment waited up to about a year for their money. According to reports, some were on the brink of bankruptcy while waiting for the money that was supposed to save them from bankruptcy. The MEP Christian Ehler threatened to cut the programme's funding. The then chair of the EIC Board, Mark Ferguson, called the situation “unacceptable” and admitted that founders had waited “far too long” for their money. In July 2023 the Commission reported that of 268 approved investments, 147 had been signed.

In fairness one must say here too: the backlog has since been cleared. According to its own figures, the fund has invested in more than 360 companies since 2020 and attracted around 3.5 euros of private money for every euro of its own. But venture capital lives on speed. An investor who needs a year for a signature is not an investor for a young company but a risk.

V. The pattern

Three instruments, three different ways of failing. With Horizon 2020 the money arrived, but not where products are made. With the Innovation Fund the money is there but does not come out, and what comes out achieves a fraction of what was promised. With the EIC the money came, but too late.

What all three have in common is that what gets measured is what is applied for, approved and accounted for, not what comes of it. In January 2026 the economist Daniel Gros named one reason for this in a study for the European Parliament: the programmes are co-designed by those who are already there, by industry associations, large corporations and research institutions. They want improvement, not upheaval.

And what follows for the next budget? The Innovation Fund is to be extended for seven years from 2028. According to the Commission's plans, the EIC is to get three times as much money. Alongside, the new Competitiveness Fund is to be created. That is not wrong if one learns from the audit reports. So far it looks more as if the same programmes are getting more money before anyone has shown what the previous money achieved.
