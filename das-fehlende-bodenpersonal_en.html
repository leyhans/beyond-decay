The Missing Ground Staff

Celibacy and a Church that would rather have empty rectories than married priests

There is no commandment of Jesus and no continuous justification for the compulsory celibacy of Catholic priests. It is a church law, in its present form barely a hundred years old, and Rome suspends it whenever it wishes. That it still applies while the rectories empty says nothing about faith. It says something about an estate that protects itself.

I. A law of 1917

Ask where celibacy comes from and you usually get one of two answers. The pious one: Jesus lived unmarried, and whoever follows him at the altar does the same. The enlightened one: the Second Lateran Council introduced celibacy in 1139. Both fall short, and it is not an enemy of the Church who says so, but a priest.

Hubert Wolf was ordained in 1985, served as a parish priest in the diocese of Rottenburg-Stuttgart and has taught church history in Münster since 1999. In 2003 he received the Leibniz Prize; he has worked in the Vatican archives for decades. His finding: the rule that a Catholic priest of the Latin Church must be unmarried appears in this general form only in the Code of Canon Law of 1917. Only there is ordination not just an impediment to marriage, but marriage also an impediment to ordination.

The New Testament knows nothing of this. The Bible says nothing about Jesus’ marital status. Peter, whom the Church lists as the first pope, was married; Jesus healed his mother-in-law. Paul lived unmarried and wished that all were like him, but he called celibacy a gift of grace given only to some. And the Pastoral Epistles require the leader of a congregation to be “the husband of one wife”: if a man does not know how to manage his own household, how can he care for God’s church? Whether this forbids polygamy, forbids remarriage or requires marriage is disputed among theologians. What is not disputed is that the letters assume a married candidate as the normal case.

II. The changing reasons

Between the New Testament and 1917 there is not one justification but several. Each carried celibacy for a time, until the next one replaced it.

Purity. In the fourth century the shared meal of the congregation became a cultic sacrifice, and in the ancient view whoever offers a sacrifice must be pure. The Synod of Elvira in southern Spain, some time between 300 and 306, therefore required continence of married clerics in its canon 33. They remained married; they were only not to sleep with their wives. As long as Mass was celebrated only on Sundays, this meant, as Wolf puts it pointedly: not on Sundays. When it was celebrated daily, it meant: never.

The East did not follow. The Council in Trullo in Constantinople in 692 stated expressly in its canon 13 that priests and deacons live with their wives, and rebuked the Roman custom of forbidding this at ordination. It required continence only for the times of service at the altar. Two orders, then, centuries before the split between East and West, and both within the same Church.

The monastic ideal. The high regard for asceticism that entered Christianity under the influence of the Stoics was at first the business of monks, who were leaving the congregations precisely because life there seemed too comfortable. Leading a parish and monastic life were long considered incompatible. Sulpicius Severus, the biographer of Martin of Tours, reports that Martin himself complained his miraculous power had grown weaker since he became a bishop. With Gregory VII a monk ascended the papal throne in 1073, and with monks who were by now priests themselves, their way of life entered the secular clergy.

Inheritance. The Second Lateran Council of 1139 declared in canon 7 that a union entered into by an ordained man would not be recognised as a marriage. Canon 21 excluded the sons of priests from service at the altar unless they entered a monastery. The background was down to earth. There was no church tax; a parish lived from its land, and the priest farmed it. A legitimate son would have inherited farm and benefice. Where there are no children entitled to inherit, no church property is lost.

Practice followed the law only grudgingly. Wolf reports that in the prince-bishopric of Münster in the sixteenth century nearly 60 per cent of parish priests lived in marriage-like relationships. For a fee to the prince-bishop, wife and son could be legitimised, and the son succeeded his father in office and benefice, often for generations. The law was meant to protect church property; the dispensation brought the bishop money. How the Church holds its money together we showed with Peter’s Pence.

Demarcation. After the Reformation the unmarried priest became the mark that distinguished the Catholic from the Protestant parsonage, and after the shock of the French Revolution the core of a new image of the priest. Jean-Marie Vianney (1786–1859), the Curé of Ars, put it in a formula: “Oh, how great is the priest! … God obeys him. He speaks two words, and at his word our Lord comes down from heaven … After God, the priest is everything.”

Purity, the monastic ideal, inheritance, demarcation, elevation: no teaching authority today speaks of the cultic impurity of marriage, the parish farms are gone, and the denominations pray together. Rome now justifies celibacy spiritually: as a gift, as conformity to Christ, as freedom for undivided service. These reasons speak for the man who freely chooses to remain unmarried. They do not speak for a law that imposes it on all. Paul already called it a gift for some.

III. The counter-test

No pope can suspend a divine commandment. A church law he can. Which of the two celibacy is, Rome has answered itself.

In 1951 Bishop Albert Stohr of Mainz ordained Rudolf Goethe, a former Protestant pastor and a married convert, as a Catholic priest. Pius XII had dispensed him from the impediment of marriage. Telling is the condition attached at the time: Goethe was to live with his wife, but “as brother and sister”. Even the exception still carried the purity rule of Elvira within it. Since then Rome has regularly ordained married Protestant and Anglican clergy when they convert. In the Eastern Catholic Churches that recognise the pope, married priests are the normal case anyway.

The Second Vatican Council drew the lesson. Its decree on priests states that perfect continence is fitting for the priesthood but “not demanded by the very nature of the priesthood”. The reason given is the practice of the Eastern Churches. At this point doctrine and history agree.

Remarkable is whom Rome dispenses: the pastor who comes from another church, not the Catholic father of a family who has lived all his life in his own parish. What is granted to the convert is denied to the faithful.

IV. The filter

There remains the objection that celibacy has a spiritual value that speaks for itself. That may hold for the man who chooses it freely. But a compulsory law also works where no one is thinking of spiritual things: in selection.

Whoever wants to become a priest must renounce marriage and family. That keeps away above all the men who want both. And it attracts men for whom the renunciation is easy, including those for whom it is convenient. The MHG study commissioned by the German bishops in 2018 describes among the accused clerics a “regressive-immature” type who sees in celibacy the mistaken opportunity “not to have to deal adequately with the formation of his own sexual identity”. Its director, the psychiatrist Harald Dreßing, put the hypothesis this way: “Celibacy acts like a magnet on personally and sexually immature people who may also deny their homosexuality.” And the study found a counter-test in its own files: deacons, who are not bound to celibacy, were accused considerably less often than priests.

Precision is needed here, because this is where things are readily confused. Most victims of abuse in the Church were boys. It does not follow that homosexual men are more likely to abuse. The MHG study states: “Homosexuality is not a risk factor for sexual abuse.” The American John Jay Report of 2011 came to the same conclusion and names the reason for the high share of boys: they were more accessible to the perpetrators. When the Church in the United States admitted girls as altar servers, the share of female victims there rose in the late 1990s.

The risk factor, then, is not the orientation but its denial, and in an institution that forbids it and at the same time attracts it. The study speaks of the “complex interplay of sexual immaturity and warded-off and denied homosexual inclinations in an ambivalent, in part openly homophobic environment”. Celibacy is not the cause of abuse. It pre-sorts who enters this environment.

In December 2025 Pope Leo XIV wrote in his letter “A Fidelity that Creates the Future”: “Only priests and religious who are humanly mature and spiritually grounded” can take on the commitment to celibacy. That is right. It is at the same time an admission that the law presupposes a maturity which it cannot produce and whose absence it attracts. How this pope separates abuse, as a “human problem”, from the institution we examined in “A Human Problem”.

V. The second counter-test

Is celibacy, then, to blame for the Church no longer finding new priests? Caution is needed here, for the numbers do not speak only for this thesis.

The Protestant churches have no celibacy. Their pastors may marry, and for decades women have been able to become pastors too. Nevertheless, in five years the number of students of Protestant theology in Germany fell by about 34.7 per cent, of Catholic theology by 29.4 per cent. Measured by students, the Protestants are losing their new generation even faster than the Catholics. And in the religious orders, which also have hardly any young members left, everyone takes a vow of chastity, whatever applies to secular priests.

Celibacy, accordingly, is not the main cause of the shortage. The main cause is that fewer and fewer people want to spend their lives in a church. Abolishing celibacy will not save the Church.

But celibacy aggravates the shortage at the most sensitive point. For there is a group Rome could have without any effort: married men who have proven themselves in work, family and parish and would be willing. They are called viri probati, proven men. The Church has them already. It simply does not ordain them.

VI. Empty rectories

In October 2019 the Amazon Synod in Rome voted on the proposal to ordain proven married men as priests in the remote regions of Amazonia. The paragraph received 128 votes in favour and 41 against, about three quarters. It was the most contested of the whole document and still had the required two-thirds majority. In areas where a congregation goes months without Mass, it was meant to help. In his letter Querida Amazonia of February 2020, Pope Francis did not mention the proposal. Wolf openly criticised him for this at the end of 2022.

Leo XIV, who lived for a long time in Peru, took up the thread in August 2025 in a telegram to the bishops of Amazonia: the Eucharist must be accessible everywhere. In his letter of December 2025 he worries at length about the shortage of priests and proposes what should be done: better pastoral care, outreach to young men, shared living against the loneliness of priests, more cooperation with lay people. Access to ordination he does not mention. At the ordination of priests in St Peter’s in April 2026 he praised celibacy, which, like the love of spouses, must be “preserved and constantly renewed”.

In Belgium they are further along. Johan Bonny, bishop of Antwerp, announced in a pastoral letter in March 2026 that he would make every effort to ordain married men as priests by 2028. His diocese, which had almost 1,500 active priests until the 1960s, has by his account fewer than 70 priests under the age of 70, and about two thirds of them come from abroad. The number of unmarried men who want to become priests has “fallen to a level barely above zero”. Several married men are already in training. No approval from Rome has become known so far.

That describes the situation. The Church chooses neither reform nor an open admission of decline. It chooses a third way: it administers the decline. Parishes are merged until one priest is responsible for many church towers, and the gaps are filled by priests from Africa, Asia and Eastern Europe. The Church would rather bring in priests from other continents than ordain the married men of its own congregations. That is resignation that looks like a solution.

VII. The ground staff

Why does Rome hold on to a law for which there is no longer any biblical, dogmatic or practical justification? In his book on celibacy Wolf concedes one point to its defenders: with the end of compulsory celibacy, the clerical system as a whole could come up for negotiation, with its disdain for “lay people” and for women. He considers that a good thing. Rome fears it.

This fear, we conclude, is the real reason. A married priest lives like the people in the pews. He has in-laws, debts, teenage children. He is one of them who holds an office. The celibate priest of the Curé of Ars is something else: a man at whose word God comes down from heaven, and therefore one who is not contradicted. Celibacy secures the distance between altar and pew. The clerical estate lives on that distance, and it also lives on the fact that it polices itself.

In German, popular speech calls priests God’s ground staff. That staff is missing because the Church wants it so. It could have had it: fathers of families, deacons, parish leaders who stand ready. It prefers empty rectories to a rectory with a pram at the door. A Church that decides this way is not defending the faith. It is defending an estate.
