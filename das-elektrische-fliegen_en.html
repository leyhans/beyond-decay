Electric Flight – A New Line of Business

The proof of concept – and who scales now?

The first proof that electric flight works came from a small company in Slovenia. Two German companies failed at the proof itself, with around two billion euros. Whoever has the battery or the customer will scale, and that is not Europe.

I. Two first flights

On 12 August 2026, the largest battery-electric aircraft ever flown took off in Plattsburgh in the north of New York State. Heart Aerospace's X1 stayed in the air for 27 minutes, climbed to 335 metres, and its powertrain delivered more than one megawatt. On 14 November, the CE25A of the state-owned aircraft maker Comac, a regional aircraft for 19 passengers, flew with a crew for the first time in Binzhou in China's Shandong province. The flight lasted 16 minutes and 38 seconds and covered about 60 kilometres.

In its commentary “China fliegt vor” of 26 November 2026 on the Chinese flight, the F.A.Z. wrote that in the West electric airliners were “at best in the development stage”. That is not true. The larger aircraft flew three months earlier, in America. It was built by a company founded in Gothenburg in 2018 that moved to Los Angeles in April 2025.

The first proof, however, came neither from America nor from China. In June 2020 the European Union Aviation Safety Agency EASA granted full type certification to the Velis Electro of the Slovenian company Pipistrel, the first electric aircraft in the world to receive it. It is a two-seat trainer, nothing spectacular, but it flies with a certificate and it is sold.

Technically, then, the proof of concept has been delivered, from the small trainer to the 19-seat regional aircraft. Economically it has not: there is still no electric airliner anywhere carrying paying passengers on a scheduled route. The open question is no longer whether it flies, but who will turn it into a business.

II. Failed before the proof

Germany had two companies that wanted to conquer electric flight. Lilium, from Wessling near Munich, raised around 1.5 billion euros. Volocopter, from Bruchsal, raised more than 500 million dollars and lost 146 million euros in 2023 alone. Both failed before they had shown that their aircraft could do everyday work.

The Lilium Jet was due to fly with a pilot for the first time in early 2025. It never did. Even the name was misleading. A jet burns fuel and expels hot gas. The Lilium Jet had 30 electrically driven ducted fans, that is, fans in a tube. Volocopter wanted to carry passengers at the 2024 Paris Olympics and had to settle for demonstration flights. Both aircraft were essentially large drones that never entered scheduled service.

It was not the money. Two billion euros is more than many of the companies flying today have had. Both started with the hardest task: flying people vertically through cities. That requires the strictest certification and leaves the least margin on weight. Whoever takes off vertically has to carry the entire weight on a stream of air. A large rotor does this by moving a lot of air slowly, like a helicopter. Small fans have to accelerate a little air very fast, and that costs many times the energy. Lilium wanted to build a helicopter out of hair dryers. How politics accompanied such projects we described in “Das Modell und die Maschine” (in German).

III. What the drone is in China

Anyone wanting to defend Volocopter can point to China. There EHang received certification for its EH216-S passenger drone in October 2023 and has delivered more than 400 of them. Six were initially cleared for commercial operation. A flight costs 299 yuan, and the flights are sightseeing trips over Guangzhou and Hefei. Even where the large drone is allowed, it is an attraction, not a means of transport.

Comac also fitted ducted fans to the CE25A, ten of them, five on each wing. The difference lies in what they are for. On the CE25A the wing carries the aircraft, as on any ordinary aeroplane, and the fans only push. That is what they are good for. Comac did not rescue Lilium's idea. Comac left out the part that could not work.

IV. Starting with the easy part

The American company Beta Technologies took the opposite route. Its CX300 takes off and lands on ordinary runways and in tests has carried six people at more than 240 kilometres per hour over more than 560 kilometres. Beta wants to fly cargo and medical transport from airport to airport first, then passengers, and only at the very end vertical take-off aircraft with passengers. On the side, the company sells its electric motors to other manufacturers and has orders worth a billion dollars on its books. High fuel prices help it, because they push operators towards electricity.

This is not a question of courage. Whoever starts with the easy part earns money sooner, gathers flight hours and learns certification on a case that can be solved. Whoever starts with the hardest part needs investors' money until they lose patience.

V. Who scales

Several companies have by now proved that electric flight works. Whoever scales will have one of two things: the battery or a patient customer. The battery determines how far an electric aircraft can go. The long-standing target for commercial operation is about 500 watt-hours per kilogram.

China has the battery. Comac, CATL, the world's largest battery maker, and Shanghai Jiao Tong University founded a joint company for electric aircraft in 2023 with 600 million yuan in registered capital. America has the customers. Heart's production aircraft has commitments from United Airlines, Air Canada and JSX, and Beta starts where cargo and ambulance flights are waiting for cheaper propulsion.

Europe had the companies, but neither the battery nor the customer. What is left of them has been sold. Pipistrel, the company with the first certificate, was bought in 2022 by the American group Textron, which also owns Cessna, Beechcraft and Bell, for around 235 million dollars. Lilium's patents were bought at auction by the American company Archer for 21 million dollars. Volocopter went to the Chinese group Wanfeng for 10 million euros, against a book value of around 42 million. Heart left of its own accord. And Northvolt, the battery factory that was supposed to make Europe independent, belongs after its insolvency to the American company Lyten. We described the same pattern for solar cells in “Europe Does Not Scale”. What a state can achieve as a customer is set out in “Der eingebaute Großkunde” (in German).

VI. Not just the money

The usual complaint is that Europe lacks capital. Lilium and Volocopter did not lack it. What they lacked was the right order: first what can be proved, then what can be sold, and last what impresses. And they lacked honesty about their own technology, which begins with a name like “Jet”. Pipistrel did it right and built the easy thing first. It will still scale in America, because nobody in Europe was willing to finance the second step after the first.

The F.A.Z. asks what Europe's answer to China is. It has already been given: one sale to America, two insolvencies and a move to Los Angeles.
