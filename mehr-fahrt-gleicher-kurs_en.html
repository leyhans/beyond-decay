More Speed, Same Course

The finance minister, the 2027 budget and a tanker heading for the cliffs

A finance minister is supposed to steer a state whose engine is losing power, with an apparatus of officials and the financial markets as his tools. Arms and debt can increase the speed, but they do not change the course.

I. The Man at the Till

Imagine someone who wakes up in the morning knowing that this year he must borrow more than two hundred billion euros that he does not have, for a country whose industry has been shrinking for eight years, at interest rates he does not set. He has a ministry, a debt agency in Frankfurt and thousands of civil servants. He has no say over whether a plant closes in Stuttgart, whether a tariff comes from Washington or whether a war in the Persian Gulf drives up the price of oil. Yet he is expected to make the accounts balance at the end of the year.

Nobody takes on such a task voluntarily without great hubris or great naivety. Either one believes one can tame the chaos, or one does not see what one is supposed to tame. This is not a statement about a particular person. It is a statement about the office as it stands today.

The man who currently holds this office is Lars Klingbeil. He is also Vice Chancellor and chairman of the SPD. On 27 November 2026 the Bundestag passed his budget for 2027 by 317 votes to 260. It allows the federal government to spend around 556 billion euros in the core budget, more than ever before.

II. The Figures

For 2027 the federal government plans new borrowing totalling 210.46 billion euros. That is the second highest in the history of the Federal Republic; only in the Covid year 2021 was it higher, at 215.4 billion. In the core budget it is almost 123 billion; the rest comes from the special funds for the Bundeswehr and for infrastructure and climate neutrality. The defence budget alone amounts to 109.75 billion euros, a third more than in 2026. With almost 30 billion from the Bundeswehr special fund, the total is nearly 140 billion, more than at any time since the end of the Cold War.

What debt costs becomes visible only with a delay. In 2027 the federal government pays around 42 billion euros in interest; by its own planning this is to reach 80.7 billion in 2030, twice as much as today. And even with all the new loans, the financial plan for 2028 to 2030 still lacks a combined 109 billion euros for which there is no cover. The reserve of almost four billion euros left for 2028 is not money in an account but an old authorisation to take on further loans.

How the apparatus works can be seen at the Finance Agency. In 2026 it raises a total of 525.5 billion euros on the credit markets. Of this, 309 billion serve only to repay old debt falling due this year. The state borrows money to pay back borrowed money, week after week, in auctions whose price is set by investors. A ten-year federal bond cost around 3.5 per cent in interest in October.

III. The Tanker

Picture the country as a fully laden tanker. The government stands on the bridge and watches the speedometer. It shows slight headway: the federal government expects growth of 1.3 per cent for 2026, 1.1 per cent for 2027 and only 0.6 per cent for 2028. The economics minister says: “We have left the trough behind us.”

Down in the engine room things look different. Industry is the ship's engine, and it has been losing power for years. From the beginning of 2018 to March 2026, industrial production fell by 15.67 per cent. In mechanical engineering the production index dropped from 109.3 to 87.5 points. The machine tool industry, from which all others buy their machines, is 35 per cent below its 2018 level after adjusting for prices. Compared with its neighbours, this is not a fate that has befallen all of Europe: from 2015 to 2025 Germany's industry lost almost eleven per cent. Over the same period Poland grew by 54 per cent, Switzerland by 36 and Austria by 23 per cent.

At the same time the ship takes more cargo on board every year: the debt. That the speedometer still shows headway is due to precisely this cargo. The federal government itself writes that growth is carried mainly by exports and by state spending on defence and infrastructure. The debt pushes a ship whose engine is getting weaker. That holds for a while, but it changes nothing about where the ship is heading. And carrying the cargo costs more every year.

IV. More Throttle, Same Rudder

Klingbeil has defended the debt with two images. “You cannot defend yourself against Putin with a balanced budget,” he said in the summer. And in the budget debate in September: “Defending yourself without new debt is like flying to the moon without a rocket.”

The first image is right. That the “black zero” of the balanced budget was not a sign of strength but the consumption of substance, we described in “The Black Zero”. The second image is more revealing than intended. A rocket burns its fuel within minutes. Whether it arrives depends not only on the amount of fuel but on the trajectory, calculated beforehand and, where necessary, corrected along the way.

Where the fuel goes can be looked up. For the period from September 2025 to December 2026 the Bundeswehr planned contracts worth 83 billion euros. The largest share, 44 per cent, goes to the navy, for frigates, submarines and supply ships. In the budget debate it was pointed out that the navy is ordering two tankers at 450 million euros each, while Britain and Norway buy comparable ships for 140 to 210 million.

A widespread impression is that the money goes mainly to America. For the new contracts this is not true: on this list, around 6.8 billion euros go to American manufacturers, about eight per cent. The large purchases such as the 35 F-35 fighter jets for around ten billion euros are older; a further 15 aircraft for 2.5 billion are planned. With them, little more than building the airbase is left for German industry. But by far the greater part of the money goes to European and above all German manufacturers. The problem is not where things are bought, but what.

The Kiel Institute for the World Economy has analysed German orders since 2020. Before the war in Ukraine, 21 per cent went into new technology: autonomous vehicles, drones, networked air defence, computer-assisted command. Today it is 9 per cent. In absolute terms this spending has barely risen since 2020; all the additional money goes into equipment of known kinds. Poland has gone the opposite way and raised the share from 3 to 16 per cent. German planning postpones the claim to technological superiority to the years 2035 to 2039. The institute's president, Moritz Schularick, calls for robotics, drones and satellites instead of more crewed vehicles and more barracks.

To be fair: tanks and frigates are not useless. In Ukraine tanks are still in use, but they survive there only with protection against cheap drones. Germany is buying the equipment of the last war in large numbers, and the protection it needs in the next one only in small numbers. Why a euro spent on tanks yields at most fifty cents for the economy, and why Europe builds fourteen different main battle tanks where America has one, we calculated in “Münchhausen's Heirs” and “The Technological Zoo”.

For the ship this means: more throttle, same rudder. Whoever accelerates without changing direction only reaches the coast sooner.

V. What the Man at the Till Cannot Steer

Klingbeil inherited a gap of 34 billion euros in the 2027 budget and declared that he had closed it completely. The Tagesspiegel writes that this was achieved partly through savings and partly through creative budgeting. The opposition speaks of tricks. The budget accompanying act does the rest: the tax on spirits rises by a fifth in January 2027, a tax on sugary drinks is to follow in 2028, the federal government pays three billion euros less into the pension insurance in 2027, and the standard rates of social benefits stay at their end-2026 level until July 2028. Planned cuts to housing benefit, to maintenance advances and to the immediate supplement for children from low-income families were blocked by the SPD; new taxes on plastic packaging and crypto transactions were blocked by the CDU/CSU. All of these are adjustments at the till. His adviser Jens Südekum counters that, excluding defence and interest, spending in the 2027 core budget falls by 17 billion euros, and to the question of interest costs he replies: “What would the alternative be?”

The question is justified, and it goes to the heart of the matter. The finance minister can decide how much money is spent and where it comes from. Where the ship goes, he does not decide. That is decided by the plants that close or stay, the inventions that are made or not made, the permits that take months or years, and the electricity prices at which a factory can or cannot produce. Here the coalition did act shortly before the vote: the electricity tax falls to the European minimum for everyone in 2027. But only for one year, paid for with leeway on the renewable-energy (EEG) account; whether it stays is to be decided in the 2028 budget. A hand on the rudder that lets go by itself after twelve months. The finance minister sits at the till. The rudder lies elsewhere, and nobody looks after the rudder, because it belongs to no single ministry.

A farmer who takes out a loan for a new tractor can repay it, because with the tractor he harvests more. If he takes out the same loan for the village fête, the fête is lovely, but the harvest stays the same and the instalments come anyway. Not all the debt in the 2027 budget is a village fête. The bridges being replaced with the special fund are tractors, even if progress with them is far too slow, as we showed in “The Reminder Has Arrived”. But the largest and fastest-growing item is equipment that harvests nothing, and the part that could bring forth something new is where savings are made.

Hubris is believing that the till can replace the rudder. Naivety is not noticing that one is trying.

VI. Where the Rudder Lies

A change of course would not be expensive. It would consist of decisions that at first cost hardly any money but touch vested interests. Inventors and small firms would need a chance against the large companies in public contracts, as the state could provide as a “built-in customer”. An agency like SPRIND, described in “The German DARPA”, would need to be the model rather than the exception. Permits would need to take months instead of years. The dual vocational training system would need to be taken seriously as the foundation of industry, not merely as a source of apprentices for craft businesses, as we wrote in “What Comes After the Fat Years?”. And the state would need to be able to do again what it now buys from consultants; that was the subject of “A State That Knows Nothing and Can Do Nothing”.

None of this is in the 2027 budget, because none of it fits into a budget. These are decisions at the rudder, not at the till. As long as nobody makes them, all that is left to the man at the till is to take more cargo on board every year and point at the speedometer.
