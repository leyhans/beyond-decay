Three Examiners, Three Questions

Twice about strategy, once about the price

A robot maker that grew up in Europe is to be sold to Japan. Brussels asked whether competition would suffer. Beijing asked who would get the data. Only one of the two questions stopped the deal. An addendum to “The Gordian Knot”.

I. The Sale

ABB was formed in 1988 from the Swedish ASEA and the Swiss Brown Boveri. The group’s robotics division employs around 7,000 people and accounted for about seven per cent of revenue in 2024. It builds its robots in three plants: in Västerås for Europe, in Auburn Hills near Detroit for the Americas and in Shanghai for Asia. The Shanghai plant opened in December 2022; it cost 150 million dollars, and robots build robots there.

In April 2025 ABB announced that it would float the division on the stock exchange. In October 2025 things turned out differently. The Japanese group SoftBank offered 5.375 billion dollars for the whole of it, and ABB accepted. Closing was planned for the second half of 2026, subject to approvals in the European Union, in China and in the United States.

II. Three Examiners

Brussels finished first. On 13 February 2026 the notification reached the European Commission; on 11 March it cleared the deal, under the simplified procedure and without conditions. It examined what European law allows it to examine at this point: whether the merger impedes competition. According to the Commission’s finding it does not, because the two companies together have only a limited market position. The question was answered in just under four weeks.

Beijing took longer and asked something else. On 5 November 2026 ABB confirmed that the Chinese office for the security review of foreign investment had prohibited the acquisition of the robotics business in China. The authority has not published its reasons. According to reports by the Financial Times and Reuters, two things were at issue: the data that accumulate at customers in China and with which models of artificial intelligence can be trained, and the buyer. SoftBank is one of the major backers of OpenAI. It was the second large deal China prohibited this year. In April the American group Meta had to unwind its purchase of the start-up Manus.

Washington has not yet decided. But how robots are viewed there has been known since the summer. On 28 July the communications regulator FCC put foreign-built humanoid and four-legged robots on its list of barred equipment, together with the kind of inverters found in solar installations. New devices of this kind may no longer be imported. The reasoning: they could be controlled remotely and used for surveillance.

So three authorities have looked at robots. The American one asked who can switch the device off. The Chinese one asked who gets the data. The European one asked whether the price will rise.

III. The Division

China is the division’s largest market. ABB builds more than nine out of ten robots for Chinese customers in the country itself. About 30 per cent of the business depends on it.

According to a Reuters report of 20 November, ABB and SoftBank are trying to save the deal by carving out this part. It could be sold to a Chinese company or listed in China. If that fails, ABB is considering floating the entire division itself after all.

The German engineering weekly VDI nachrichten has asked the question that follows from this: after a division, who owns the knowledge that used to belong to one group? The control software, the experience from half a million installed robots, the processes from the Shanghai plant cannot be cut into 70 and 30 per cent like a plot of land. Whoever buys the Chinese part gets a robot maker with European technology and the Chinese market. Whoever buys the rest gets the technology without its largest market.

IV. The Case Before

It is not the first time. In 2016 the Chinese household appliance group Midea took over the Augsburg robot maker Kuka; since 2022 it has owned it entirely. Nobody prevented it at the time.

Ten years on, the record is mixed. The trade union IG Metall notes that the feared break-up and the relocation of the whole company to China have not happened. The Bavarian economics ministry calls Midea a reliable owner. At the same time some 500 jobs were cut in Augsburg, and in February the magazine Wirtschaftswoche used satellite images to show how capacity in China is growing.

Of the four large makers of industrial robots, two came from Europe. One has belonged to a Chinese group for ten years. The other is now being divided between a Japanese investor and a Chinese buyer not yet known.

V. What Follows

One can object that all this is in order. ABB is a private company and may sell what belongs to it. Japan is an ally, not an adversary. The Chinese prohibition is no model but an act of sealing off, and its reasons are known only at second hand. And the Commission did what the law requires of it: merger control examines competition and nothing else. Whether a foreign buyer affects security is decided in Europe by the individual states, and Switzerland is not even a member of the Union.

All of that is true, and it describes the problem. For the sale of its key industries Europe has a thorough procedure for the smallest question and none for the largest. Whether a price rises is examined. Who will build the robots standing in European factories in ten years, who owns their data and who can switch them off is examined by nobody at European level. The two other powers answer these questions, each in its own way and both ruthlessly.

In “From Both Shelves” we wrote that Europe buys its artificial intelligence in America and in China. With robots it goes one step further. Europe does not only buy from both shelves. It has placed its own goods on them. What becomes of the factories equipped with them is described in “What Remained of the Show”.

A disclosure belongs here: SoftBank is one of the largest backers of OpenAI, and OpenAI is a direct competitor of Anthropic, the company behind Claude.
