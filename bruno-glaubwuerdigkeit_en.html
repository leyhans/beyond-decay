The Case of Giordano Bruno and the Credibility of the Catholic Church

Four hundred and twenty-five years after the execution on the Campo de' Fiori. Whoever upholds the verdict in the year 2000 cannot retreat behind other times. That makes the question not historical but present.

The Church as the oldest living component of the megamachine — and the structure of an institutional lie that has never dirtied its own hands.

On 17 February 1600, an Ash Wednesday, a man was burned on the Campo de' Fiori in Rome whose tongue had first been clamped so that he could no longer address the crowd. He was fifty-two years old, his name was Filippo Bruno of Nola, as a Dominican friar he had taken the name Giordano, he had wandered through Europe for years — Geneva, Toulouse, Paris, Oxford, Wittenberg, Prague, Frankfurt — and wherever he arrived he had in the end broken with the authorities of the place. He spent eight years in the custody of the Roman Inquisition, seven of them under interrogation. The chief prosecutor, Cardinal Robert Bellarmine, later canonised, is said to have been troubled by his execution all his life. To this day there is dispute about the content of the charges, because central trial records have disappeared or are kept under lock and key by the Church.

Modern memory has canonised Bruno as a martyr of science. Since 1889 his monument has stood on the Campo de' Fiori in Rome, a dark, hooded monk looking out over the square, which has since become a market. The monument was erected by anticlericals, in anger at the papacy, which had just lost its Papal States. Since then the story has run along a particular track: Bruno burned because he taught the infinite number of inhabited worlds; the Church burned the scientist; the Enlightenment triumphed later all the same.

This story is not entirely wrong, but it misses the essential point. Bruno was not a scientist in the modern sense. He was a Renaissance philosopher, a hermetic thinker, a late Platonist with cabbalistic admixtures, a teacher of the art of memory, probably for a time a spy in English service. What brought him before the Inquisition was indeed also his doctrine of the infinity of worlds — but the central charges lay elsewhere. Bruno had disputed the Trinity. He had denied the divinity of Christ — Jesus had not been God, but, in one of the formulations of the charge, a skilful magician. He had rejected the virginity of Mary. He had declared transubstantiation impossible. He had rejected eternal damnation. He had taught the transmigration of souls between worlds.

These are the charges preserved in the Sommario del Processo, rediscovered in 1940. They concern what theology calls the Depositum Fidei — the Church’s treasury of faith, its dogmatic core. Bruno was not primarily burned because he took the stars to be distant suns. He was burned because he had disputed the dogmatic centre.

This precise state of the facts does not exonerate the Church. It aggravates the case. Whoever tortures a man for eight years and in the end burns him because he disputes the Trinity has a different problem from someone who burns him over a cosmology. The first is the defence of a doctrine by means of death. The second would be a confusion of theology and natural philosophy. The Church did the first, and in the year 2000 — four hundred years later — it expressly confirmed it.

I. The statement of 2000

On 17 February 2000, four hundred years after the execution, Cardinal Angelo Sodano, at the time Cardinal Secretary of State and the second man in the Vatican, issued a statement. He called the execution a sad episode and a terrible death. He expressed deep regret about the method. And then he added that the inquisitors had had the desire to serve the truth and to promote the common good, and had done everything possible to save his life.

In the same year Cardinal Paul Poupard, at the time President of the Pontifical Council for Culture, spoke at a Bruno symposium organised by the Jesuits. His words are precise and ought to be quoted: Bruno’s thought was incompatible with Christian thought. Therefore one cannot and should not speak of a rehabilitation, because in the case of Giordano Bruno there is no basis for such a hypothetical operation, as took place, for example, in the cases of Jan Hus and Galileo Galilei.

These two statements are not accidental. They are the official position of the Church to this day. What they say has a double structure. First: we regret the method. Second: we do not regret the verdict. This is no slip of wording — it is a carefully calibrated double statement. The method is surrendered because the method can be surrendered; the verdict is upheld because the verdict is bound up with the doctrine that is still defended today.

This puts the matter in a position where the usual historical distancing no longer works. It is not those were different times, we cannot judge that. The Church of 2000 judged the matter, and on the substance it judged as the Church of 1600 did. It only changed the form in which it judges. The verdict itself — Bruno was rightly convicted — stands. What falls away is the execution. What remains is the confirmation of the guilty verdict.

This is the point at which all evasions stop. The Church that speaks today is exactly the same Church that burned in 1600. It is not a different institution with the same name. It is the same institution with different means. Whoever does not see this has not read the point of the Sodano-Poupard statements.

II. Why Galileo, but not Bruno

In 1992 — after 359 years — Pope John Paul II declared in a formal address to the Pontifical Academy of Sciences that the theologians of the time had made an error in the Galileo case. The declaration was restrained, but it was a rehabilitation. Galileo, who was never burned but died under house arrest, had been acquitted after centuries.

Why could that happen, and why not in Bruno’s case? The answer Poupard gave in 2000 — Bruno’s thought was incompatible with Christian thought — is the honest answer, and it deserves analysis. It says that what Galileo asserted was compatible with today’s Catholic teaching, or could be made compatible; and that what Bruno asserted is not.

This answer is correct. Galileo asserted that the earth revolves around the sun. This statement touches the cosmology of the Bible — which in several passages presupposes a geocentric world view — but it does not touch the dogmatic core of the teaching. In the Galileo case the Bible became hermeneutically reinterpretable. The cosmology could be surrendered because it was not a dogmatic centre.

With Bruno it is different. Whoever acquits Bruno implicitly declares that in the doctrine of the Trinity, in Christology, in the doctrine of the Eucharist, in eschatology, several truths may stand alongside the Catholic one. Catholic theology cannot do that without giving itself up. The Trinity cannot be reinterpreted like the solar system. It stands at the centre of what the Church calls revealed truth.

That is the core of the problem — and it is not a problem between an old and a new Church. It is the condition of the institution’s continuity. Bruno cannot be rehabilitated because his heresies strike at what the Church still teaches today. The verdict of 1600 must be confirmed, even if the method is regretted — otherwise the identity of the institution falls.

III. The brachium saeculare — how to kill without lifting a hand

There is a feature of Catholic inquisitorial practice that is almost always passed over in modern memory, and it is central to understanding the institution. In its own self-understanding, the Church has never, in its entire history, executed anyone itself. It passed judgement. It handed the execution over to the brachium saeculare, the secular arm.

This construction is not harmless. Nor was it a necessity; it was a choice. The Church could have executed like other courts of its time. It did not, because canon law forbade clerics to shed blood — Ecclesia non sitit sanguinem, the Church does not thirst for blood. Instead the Inquisition convicted the heretic, excommunicated him, handed him over to the secular court and, in the formula of handover, expressly asked that the secular arm show mercy and spare the life of the condemned.

This request was legal code. It meant the opposite of what it said. A secular court that had treated an excommunicated heretic with mercy — that is, had not executed him — would itself have come under suspicion of heresy. The request for clemency was the formal demand for execution. Everyone knew it. The formula served solely to keep the Church’s hands clean while the pyre burned.

This is not a modern insinuation. It is the open statement of contemporary theorists of the Inquisition. Francisco Peña (1540-1612), the most important canonist commentator on the standard inquisitorial handbook, the Directorium Inquisitorum of Nicolaus Eymericus, expressly recorded in his commentary edition, published by 1578 at the latest, that the request for clemency was a pure formality — it served, in Peña’s own words, solely so that the inquisitors should not appear to consent to the shedding of blood and thereby fall into canonical irregularity, which would have had consequences for their ecclesiastical careers. Peña was no anti-Catholic critic. He was a member of staff of the Roman Inquisition. He explained the mechanism in an insider text written not for the public but for the inquisitors themselves.

In Bruno’s case this formula was applied. The verdict of 8 February 1600 handed him over to the secular authorities with the customary request that the punishment be carried out as mildly as possible and without the shedding of blood. What was carried out was burning alive. The formula alone would not have required it — hanging, too, would have been bloodless and would have preserved the canonical rule. That it was burning nonetheless did not follow from the wording of the handover formula but from a separate theological logic running in the background. Heretics were burned for three reasons that had nothing to do with the prohibition on shedding blood. First: the obliteration of the body. The Church taught bodily resurrection; a heretic was not to be able to rise again, his body had to be destroyed so completely that no relics remained that might later be venerated as a martyr’s relics. Second: the anticipation of hell. The burning was a didactic spectacle — the onlookers were to see what awaits the deviant in eternity. Third: the maximisation of agony and deterrence. Burning alive was the most protracted form of execution, visible from afar, loud, heavy with smell.

The executioners who carried it out knew this logic. There was the practice of strangling a repentant convict before the burning — this was considered an act of mercy that spared the longer agony. For unrepentant heretics this mercy was precisely not provided. For Bruno explicitly not — the bound tongue that prevented him from speaking to the crowd is the historical evidence that he was to go into the fire fully conscious. Someone who is strangled before the burning no longer needs a bound tongue. The executioner, too, knew what he had to do — and what he was precisely not to do. The architecture of the killing was arranged so that Church, governor and executioner each performed his part without any of them having to feel responsible for the whole. That is the perfection of outsourcing: even within the execution, responsibility was distributed so that nobody had to bear it.

The citra sanguinis effusionem formula was therefore not the reason for the burning. It was the legal camouflage that made a theologically motivated choice appear to be a mere canonical detail. The Church did not want the heretic merely dead — it wanted to see him burn, because the fire carried a theological content of its own. The handover formula concealed this choice in a request for leniency that every participant understood as its opposite.

Bruno was not burned by the Church — formally, at any rate. He was burned by the Roman governor after the Inquisition had handed him over to secular jurisdiction. The Church can claim with complete legal correctness that it killed nobody. It merely pronounced the verdict and dictated the theologically grounded manner of execution in a handover formula that seemed to say the opposite. The hands stayed clean. That applies to Bruno, it applies to the hundreds of thousands of others who were executed over the centuries — witches, heretics, Jews, people of other faiths — and it applies to all those who would still be executed if the Church still had the secular arm at its disposal.

In the Papal States themselves, however, even this legal outsourcing fell away. There the Pope was at once supreme spiritual shepherd and secular monarch. There was no independent secular arm to which the Inquisition could have handed heretics over. The executioner was on the papal payroll. The executions took place on the squares of the papal capital, Rome, in the name of the Pope as secular sovereign. The actors who in the morning, as inquisitors, handed the heretic over to the secular arm were in the afternoon, in another role, themselves the secular arm — as Cardinal Secretary of State, as governor of the city of Rome, as members of the judicial commission. The same persons in two robes. In the Papal States the brachium saeculare was not outsourcing but self-reference. The Church handed the heretic over to itself and had him executed by its own executioner on its own squares. In 1600 Bruno was burned on the Campo de' Fiori, which belonged to the city of Rome, which belonged to the Papal States, whose sovereign was the Pope, on whose behalf the Inquisition had passed judgement. A clearer self-reference could hardly be constructed.

This is not a historical detail. It is the architecture of the institutional lie. An institution that in its self-presentation never kills itself, but for centuries gives the orders that lead to death, can later claim that it never killed. It can attribute the burnings to the secular rulers. For the bishops and inquisitors involved, it reduces their guilt to participation and certifies in the year 2000: “…they had the desire to serve the truth and to promote the common good, and had done everything possible to save the life of Giordano Bruno.”

And at the end of the twentieth century, in its infinite goodness and wisdom, the Church can ask forgiveness in a perfidious staging of mea culpa for what “sons and daughters of the Church” had done — as if the hierarchy had not pronounced the verdicts. The Church as an institution is and remains holy, pure and innocent. The guilt is played down in an unbearable way and becomes the personal guilt of the inquisitors. It is interesting that in this assignment of guilt the “daughters” of the Church are, for once and expressly, named as well. What is their guilt supposed to have consisted of? There were no female inquisitors. There were no female bishops. There were no female popes. The hierarchy that pronounced the verdicts was exclusively male. Women appear in the history of the Inquisition in two roles: as servants of the inquisitors, who cooked, washed and kept silent — and as victims, above all in the witch hunts, in which the overwhelming majority of those executed were women. When the Church in the year 2000 speaks of “daughters” for whom forgiveness is to be asked, it ranks precisely these two groups among the accomplices — the marginally involved alongside the real victims. The witches become accomplices in their own burning. That is not an admission of guilt. It is a second burning, with words.

In the Papal States this self-presentation was a constructed fiction without any legal basis; in the other European territories it was a legally effective construction of outsourcing. In both cases the result was always the same: a dead man and an institution claiming it had done nothing.

IV. Adaptation to the changed balance of power

The only thing the Church lacks today is the possibility of having its verdicts carried out. That is the sober balance. The humanitarian conduct, the regret, the mea culpa appearances are not moral progress on the part of the institution. They are adaptation to a world in which the secular arms no longer respond to the bishops’ signal.

This observation is not cynical. It follows from a simple test. When did the Church stop burning heretics? In 1826 — the last official execution by the Spanish Inquisition, the teacher Cayetano Ripoll, hanged for deism in Valencia, because burning had by then come to be considered barbaric. Why did it stop? Not because in the course of the Enlightenment the Church had come to see that killing dissenters is wrong. It stopped because the liberal constitutions of the nineteenth century deprived the Church of the right to set the secular arm in motion. In the states in which it retained power — Spain, Portugal, parts of Italy — the Inquisition remained active for longer. In the states in which it lost power, it stopped.

The sharpest test is provided by the Papal States themselves. There the Church could kill for as long as it wished — it was the sovereign that paid the executioners. From 1796 to 1864 Giovanni Battista Bugatti, known in Rome as Mastro Titta, was in the service of the popes. In sixty-eight years he carried out four hundred and fourteen executions, on behalf of seven successive popes — Pius VI, Pius VII, Leo XII, Pius VIII, Gregory XVI, Pius IX. Executions with axe, rope, hammer and guillotine. On the Piazza del Popolo, the Piazza di Ponte, in provincial towns of the Papal States. The victims were robbers, murderers — and in the Restoration period after Napoleon above all political opponents: Carbonari, followers of Mazzini, Italian patriots who stood for a united, secular Italy and thus against the temporal rule of the Pope. Pius IX — the same Pope who in 1854 raised the Immaculate Conception and in 1870 papal infallibility to dogmas — sent Bugatti into retirement in 1864 with a monthly pension of thirty scudi. Bugatti’s successor carried on the work. The last execution in the Papal States took place on 9 July 1870 in Palestrina. Seven weeks later, on 20 September 1870, Italian troops broke through the Porta Pia and took Rome. That ended the Papal States. It also ended the papal practice of execution. Both on the same day, because the one was the precondition of the other.

This is not a story from the Middle Ages. These are dates from the late nineteenth century. In 1870 Bismarck was the founder of the German Reich, Lincoln had abolished slavery five years earlier, France had been publicly debating the death penalty for two years, Italy had united politically. While all of Europe was conducting the Enlightenment debates on penal reform, the Catholic Church went on beheading. It did not stop out of insight. It stopped because its executioner was taken away from it, together with the state in which he worked.

That is not reform out of insight. It is adaptation out of weakness. An institution that in five hundred years of systematic persecution never voluntarily renounced the means of killing — that gave it up only when the means was taken out of its hands — has no argument that it is mild today for reasons of its own ethical development. It is mild because it has to be. Today’s representatives of the Church are cut from the same cloth as their predecessors. They issue statements that articulate regret, and they do so in a world in which any other statement would isolate them immediately. What they do not do is what a truly transformed institution would have to do — break with the verdicts of its predecessors and declare them to be what they were: an injustice crying out to heaven. The Church would have to declare it as an institution and not hold individuals responsible for the injustice.

V. The Church as the oldest living component of the megamachine

In The Myth of the Machine Lewis Mumford described how the megamachine — the large-scale technical, hierarchical system aimed at the total mobilisation of people and resources — first became visible about five thousand years ago in the building of the Egyptian pyramids. It consisted of five constituent elements, which he called the pentagon of power: power, productivity, profit, prestige, publicity. It required a priesthood that legitimised the whole as cosmic order, a king who provided the head of command, a scribal bureau that ran the administration, an army that broke resistance, and a myth that brought those involved to submit voluntarily.

It is no accident that the Catholic Church is recognisable in this scheme. It is the oldest still-functioning variant of the priesthood element of the megamachine, in European adaptation. Since the late fourth century it has fulfilled the function of supplying the religious legitimation of a total claim to rule — first in symbiosis with the Roman emperorship, then with the Frankish, then with the European monarchies, then in competition with the emerging nation states, and today in a complex tangle with the global structures. It is a power in its own right, with its own diplomacy, its own bank, its own state, its own law. It is not one religion among others. It is a large-scale institutional structure in religious packaging.

In Das Ende der Megamaschine Fabian Scheidler described the history of the last five hundred years of this large-scale structure. He shows how the European megamachine developed its characteristic tyrannies in the early modern period — the economic tyranny of accumulation, the ideological tyranny of apocalypticism, the military tyranny of permanent war, the political tyranny of state power. In all four of these tyrannies the Catholic Church has played a constitutive role.

It was and is an economic great landowner and taker of interest — the latter under carefully chosen names, because according to biblical law (Deuteronomy 23:20-21) and several medieval councils — Lateran II in 1139, Vienne in 1311 — taking interest was considered a mortal sin. The Church itself did not give up the forbidden business; it renamed it. What was legally called census was economically interest. What went by the name of damnum emergens or lucrum cessans — compensation for lost profit — was likewise interest. What the Montes Pietatis, the Church’s pawnshops, collected as an administration fee was interest. And what could not be relabelled in this way was left to the Jews, whom the Church exempted from the prohibition on interest between fellow believers on the grounds that they were not Christians — and to whom at the same time it closed off most other ways of earning a living, thereby making them permanent foreign bodies in their surroundings. That is the economic variant of the same architecture that worked for killing in the brachium saeculare: the forbidden is outsourced, the hands stay clean, and afterwards one points at those who carried it out.

The Church’s most lucrative source of income, however, was not the relabelled interest business. It was the zealously stoked fear of hell and the unhindered access of priests to the dying in their anguish of soul. The theological stage for this was built in the High Middle Ages: the Fourth Lateran Council of 1215 made the doctrine of purgatory binding; alongside it stood the doctrine of eternal damnation, and alongside that the monopolistic administration of the sacraments, which were considered the only path to salvation. This set in motion a business model without historical parallel — and it ran on two levels.

On the first level the grieving bereaved paid. Whoever had a dead relative who was not to end up in hell could shorten the time spent in purgatory through Masses for the dead, through indulgences, through alms, through endowments. The Church was the sole administrator of these accounts in the hereafter. Whoever did not pay left his own father, his own mother, his own child behind in a state of pain of indefinite length — so, at least, ran the doctrine the bereaved had to believe. The trade in indulgences of the late Middle Ages, over which the Reformation was ignited, was merely the loudest form of this first business.

The more perfidious level lay at the deathbed itself. Here it was not the bereaved paying for the dead. Here the dying signed their assets over directly to the Church — and thereby withdrew them from their own living. The priest was legally and sacramentally privileged in the death chamber. He heard confession, gave the Eucharist, anointed the dying. In precisely this hour, when the fear of hell was greatest and the will weakest, wills were drawn up, bequests formulated, land signed over to the Church. Medievalists estimate that a considerable part of the Church’s landholdings in Europe — some say up to a third — derives from such deathbed bequests. That was not by chance. It was the result of a system in which the Church generated the doctrine (hell), provided the personnel (the priest in the death chamber), monopolised the sacraments (the only means of salvation) and handled the contract (the will). Four functions in one hand, at the most sensitive moment of a human life.

The point is that this business was not given up when the Church lost power. It is still in operation today, only less visible. The German dioceses, the Catholic orders, the Vatican, Caritas and other church-related organisations actively promote provision by will, run their own inheritance advisory services, hold training sessions for priests on dealing with the dying. What has changed is the language — the medieval fear of hell has become the late-modern concern for meaning at the end of life, for leaving behind a good cause, for spirituality in dying. What has not changed is the architecture. The priest or the pastoral carer still has privileged access to the death chamber. The institution still inherits through this access. Assets are still rewritten at the deathbed, at a moment when the bereaved are often not present and do not learn what is happening. The core business has merely changed its packaging.

This is not a side effect of a religious institution. It is structure. Through its doctrine the Church generates the fear that nobody can resolve alone; it monopolises the only way out; and it collects the assets that are rewritten under the pressure of this fear. In the Middle Ages the fear was hell, in the 21st century it is the emptiness of meaning at the end of life, but the mechanism is the same. It is the core business of the institution, since the High Middle Ages, without interruption.

It was the ideological source of the apocalyptic thinking without which the dynamic of conquest and annihilation of the modern era would have been unthinkable. It was a military co-organiser of the Crusades and of the European wars of religion. It was the political ally of practically all the absolutist monarchies of Europe.

Whoever removes the Catholic Church from the picture of the megamachine has not understood the picture. It is not an outsider critically accompanying the megamachine. It is its oldest living component. What in Mumford’s pentagon of power of the megamachine is grasped as the priesthood took, in Europe, the form of the Roman Church. It has filled this function without interruption from the late fourth century to the present day — from the Edict of Thessalonica, by which Nicene Christianity became the only permitted religion of the Roman Empire in 380, to the global tangle of the twenty-first century. The role of the Protestant and Calvinist religion as components of the megamachine is a separate story that would go beyond the scope of this essay.

From this classification follows something that becomes visible in the Bruno case. The Church’s inability to overturn the verdict of 1600 is not a character trait of one individual religious institution. It is a constitutive feature of every component of the megamachine that grounds its own legitimacy on a revealed or sanctified truth. Whoever administers the truth cannot admit errors in the truth without losing the licence to administer it. Whoever decrees death in the name of the truth cannot withdraw the death sentence without withdrawing the doctrine defined as truth.

VI. The institutional lie par excellence

What makes the Catholic Church a particularly clear case is the combination of the length of its history, the sharpness of its doctrine, and the refined architecture of the brachium saeculare, which enabled it to carry out the crime without formally laying a hand on anyone.

An institutional lie is not the lie of a person. A person who lies can recant. An institutional lie is a system of statements in which untruth is part of the structure. The person working in the institution does not lie personally; he repeats what the structure instructs him to say. The lie is thereby depersonalised, hard to attribute, and it becomes stable because it does not depend on the sincerity of individuals.

The Catholic Church is the institutional lie par excellence because it fulfils all the conditions of such a lie to the highest degree. It has a two-thousand-year tradition in which the lie has hardened. It has a doctrine that cannot be reformed without dissolving the institution itself. It has a legal architecture — the brachium saeculare — that enables it to organise the crime without formally appearing as the perpetrator. And it has a language capable of combining regret over the method with defence of the verdict without the combination becoming immediately visible as a contradiction.

The institutional crime par excellence is not the individual crime. It is the structural apparatus through which the crime becomes possible, is protected from prosecution by other authorities, and is afterwards brought into a form that leaves the institution undamaged in its immaculate self-image. The burning of Bruno is only one example. The same pattern shows itself today in the abuse cases: individual perpetrators act in their own interest, but their actions at the same time have structural causes that the Church does not tackle — and cannot tackle without calling itself into question as an institution. The structural mechanism that made the burning of 1600 possible, justified it for over four hundred years and today half-withdraws it without really overturning it is the structural, continuing crime. It is not only the crime of 1600. It is the crime of 2026, of which the crime of 1600 is only the historically most visible manifestation. The Church has manoeuvred itself into a position from which it can no longer find a way out — because, regardless of what it does, it is and remains questionable.

Whoever finds this hard to say has a reason. In Western culture the Church has enjoyed a special forbearance that has not been granted to other institutions with a comparable record. Whoever denounces state crimes — Stalinism, National Socialism, colonial history — is heard. Whoever denounces church crimes quickly comes under suspicion of hostility to religion, of anticlericalism, of one-sided cultural criticism. This protective formation is part of the institutional lie. It ensures that the question cannot be asked as it ought to be asked without those asking it being pushed into an ideological corner.

VII. The balance, in summary

Bruno is not the only one. He is the particularly well-documented individual case, because his trial can be reconstructed through the rediscovered Sommario and his statue on the Campo de' Fiori keeps the memory alive. But the diagnosis of this essay — crime without dirty hands, defence of doctrine at the price of human lives, half-withdrawn regret without overturning the verdict — applies to a much larger complex. A complete account would go beyond the scope of this essay. What follows is the summary list, in which each case is outlined in a few sentences and the structural point is named.

The witch burnings, 15th to 18th centuries. In the Malleus Maleficarum (1487) of the inquisitor Heinrich Kramer and in the papal bull Summis desiderantes affectibus (Innocent VIII, 1484), the Catholic Church laid the theoretical foundation for the European witch hunts. Estimates speak of forty to sixty thousand executed, the vast majority of them women. The Church has never officially apologised for these killings. The process is exactly the same as with Bruno: the verdict was pronounced, the hands were kept clean, the regret was absorbed centuries later in the general mea culpa gesture without a single case having been rehabilitated.

The Crusades, 11th to 13th centuries. Eight officially numbered Crusades, plus children’s crusades and Reconquista campaigns, with hundreds of thousands dead — Muslims, Jews, Orthodox Christians, heretics — and the Fourth Crusade, which sacked Constantinople in 1204 and thereby decisively weakened the Byzantine Empire. In 2001, under John Paul II, the Church issued a general apology to the Orthodox world, but it has never rejected the concept of the crusade as such, never withdrawn a single call to armed violence, never repaid the money from the plundered riches.

The wars against heretics, 12th to 14th centuries. The Albigensian Crusade (1209-1229) against the Cathars in southern France, with the annihilation of an entire culture and the killing of at least two hundred thousand people. The order Kill them all, God will know his own, attributed to the papal legate Arnaud Amaury at the storming of Béziers (1209), is possibly apocryphal, but what it describes — the indiscriminate killing of Catholics and Cathars, because separating them could not be expected of the soldiers — is historically established. No rehabilitation, no admission of guilt.

The persecution of the Jews in the Lateran IV complex. The Fourth Lateran Council of 1215 — the same one that made the doctrine of purgatory binding — also decreed the obligation for Jews to wear distinctive clothing, their exclusion from public office, their legal treatment as a special group. This created the theological and canonical template for seven centuries of anti-Jewish practice in Europe. In 1965, with the declaration Nostra aetate, the Church revised its attitude to Judaism. But it has not admitted that its own teaching between 1215 and 1965 was a constitutive element of European antisemitism, without which the dynamic of persecution of the modern era, including the Shoah, would have been unthinkable.

Colonial history, 16th to 20th centuries. With the papal bull Inter caetera (Alexander VI, 1493) the Church divided the non-European world between Spain and Portugal. With the encomienda doctrine and the theological dispute over the souls of the Indians (Valladolid 1550-1551) it supplied the justifying figures for the conquest of Latin America, which went hand in hand with millions of deaths among the indigenous population. In Africa and Asia it played analogous roles. In 1992 the Church accompanied the Columbus year with a mea culpa gesture, but it has never formally revoked Inter caetera.

The Vatican Bank, 20th century to the present. The Istituto per le Opere di Religione, founded in 1942, has for decades been entangled in money-laundering, Mafia and corruption affairs. The death of the banker Roberto Calvi under Blackfriars Bridge in London in 1982, the collapse of Banco Ambrosiano, the involvement of Archbishop Paul Marcinkus, the first-ever conviction of a cardinal — Angelo Becciu, sentenced in 2023 to five and a half years in prison for embezzlement, appeal pending — are not a series of individual cases but the practice, stable over decades, of a financial institution that evades regular supervision through its special statute.

Sexual abuse and its cover-up, 20th and 21st centuries. Hundreds of thousands of documented cases worldwide, in the USA, Ireland, Australia, Germany, Belgium, Chile, in most Catholic countries. The structural cover-up — the transfer of perpetrators, the intimidation of victims, the obligations of secrecy — has been shown to be systemic in several state inquiries (Ireland: Ryan Report 2009, Murphy Report 2009; Germany: MHG study 2018). The Church has paid compensation in individual dioceses, but it has not touched the system of the special status of priests that made the architecture of abuse possible in the first place — celibacy, hierarchical closure, special canonical jurisdiction, the seal of confession as protection for perpetrators. The finding is exactly the same as with Bruno: method regretted, structure defended.

These seven complexes are not exhaustive. They are a selection showing that the diagnosis of this essay does not rest on a single case. It applies to a pattern. In each of these cases we find the same architecture: the doctrine that legitimises the crime; the outsourcing of execution to third parties or concealment through institutional secrecy; the half-regret decades or centuries later without fundamental revision; and the sparing of the institution by a culture that attacks other major perpetrators harder than this one. The Church is not an institution that has made mistakes. It is an institution whose mechanics of error are the structural precondition of its existence.

VIII. What remains

It would be a misunderstanding to conclude from this diagnosis that the Catholic Church should be abolished by force or that its members should be despised. The overwhelming majority of its members are people who have nothing to do with the institutional structure described here. They live their faith for reasons that have nothing to do with Bruno, with the brachium saeculare or with the pentagon of power. The diagnosis concerns the structure, not the people who live within it.

But the diagnosis concerns nothing less than the structure either. It says that this structure cannot give an honest answer to a question that it itself raised — the question of the Bruno verdict. It says that the failure of the answer is not accidental but structural, and that the structural element in it is the actual crime. It says that today’s representatives of the institution are mild not for moral reasons but for lack of power. And it says that this institution is part of a larger construction, the megamachine, which has been at work for five thousand years and whose oldest living religious component is the Church.

The statue on the Campo de' Fiori, erected by anticlericals in the nineteenth century, is today less an anti-Catholic monument than a memorial to something more general. It stands for the possibility that an institution kills a human being in the name of a doctrine that, even four hundred and twenty-five years later, it is not prepared to relax on this question. It stands for the reminder that such institutions exist, that they cannot find their way out of this position by their own strength, and that the regret they express today is another form of the same crime.

And it stands for a question that is not carved into the statue but is contained in its substance: which of our present-day institutions will, four hundred years from now, find itself in the same position — having done something it ought to regret but cannot overturn, because the verdict is bound up with the core of its doctrine? Whoever takes this question seriously has understood the Bruno case without having to reduce it to the religious. And whoever answers honestly sees that the megamachine is not an old story but the form in which we live.

In its entire history the Church has never killed anyone itself. It passed judgement. It organised the burning without lighting the fire. It never dirtied its hands. The only thing it lacks today is the possibility of continuing the procedure. What it expresses in the way of regret is not transformation. It is adaptation to the changed situation.
