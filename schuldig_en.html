It Is Always the Others Who Are to Blame

On decisions that leave no trace.

I. Two words that do not mean the same thing

Blame looks backward. It seeks an author, measures his share and demands something in return — punishment, penance, restitution. It is a closed account, and once settled, the matter is held to be finished.

Responsibility looks forward. It seeks not an author but a remit, and it ends not with a payment but with a change of conduct.

The distinction is not academic. The word blame carries a religious imprint that almost everyone has encountered in some form, and it provokes one of two reactions: defence or contrition. Both end the conversation. The title of this text is therefore the sentence we are examining, not the category in which we think.

One further point at the outset: this is not about confessions. A country that has long wrestled with its past demonstrates how quickly an admission becomes a practised form — and a practised form achieves the same as an accusation: it ends the reflection. Whoever has confessed is done. Whoever has accused others is done too. Both are closing formulas.

II. The case

A proposal for which there was no precedent: a radially movable axial bearing in which the balls run not in grooves but on flat surfaces. In this way an axial offset can be accommodated without adding a further element.

The solution appeared in no textbook. That was precisely the objection — unspoken, but effective. The usual reservations came: from the responsible design engineer, from his colleagues, from the head of the design office. Arguments one knows, which nobody has to substantiate because they appeal to established practice.

Then came the sentence this text is about:

We can try that on the next machine, when we are not under such time pressure as now.

And the proposer allowed himself to be put off.

It was built by the textbook. On the next machine as well, for by then the proposer had been sidelined. The machine was built twice. It was not stiff enough.

III. What became of it

Ever since, the process for which that machine was built has been regarded as interesting but not stiff enough in practice.

That verdict is misattributed. What was tested was not the process but one particular U-axis. The finding applies to that solution. To this day it is attributed to the invention as a whole.

And because the alternative was never built, there is nothing to set against it. The verdict is irrefutable — not because it is correct, but because the counter-evidence never came into existence. Whether the unconventional solution would have been stiff has to this day never been investigated.

The consequence reaches far. Of a process consisting of three spindles, only the reduced two-spindle version has become the state of the art. Half the invention.

IV. Postponement as the perfect decision

We can try that on the next machine is not a refusal. That is its strength.

Against a no one can argue. It has an author, a justification, a date — it can be attacked, overruled, revised. Against a later one can do nothing, for formally one has been given one’s due. The proposal has not been rejected. It has been noted.

Nobody has to justify anything. Nobody carries anything. And the matter is settled all the same.

What this produces is an asymmetry in the records, and it governs everything that follows.

The solution that was built exists. It is drawn, manufactured, described in a trade article and documented in a patent application. A body of records, dated and attributed.

The proposal does not exist. No minutes, no file note, no record of the postponement. For forty years it was to be found in no archive anywhere.

Actions leave traces. Postponements do not. Every reckoning, every retrospective works with traces. So it captures the one and passes over the other — not out of leniency, but because its method entails it.

V. The sentence, forty years on

Last year the original solution was reconstructed and committed to paper. The design engineer of that time looked at the drawings and said:

Didn’t we want to do it that way back then?

He was not lying. He remembered, and in the direction in which one remembers.

The proposal had survived, because it had been right. The rejection had vanished, because it could no longer be sustained. And into the gap that opened, the word we had moved.

This is the everyday form of the heroic account, and it is the most unassailable, because it sounds generous. It claims no glory for any one person. It turns what one man proposed against all the others into a joint undertaking that circumstances prevented.

And it could not be refuted. The reconstruction proves that the solution was possible — not that it was proposed at the time. That is precisely why the sentence could be spoken with nobody able to contradict it.

VI. Three degrees that must be kept apart

Not every failure to act is attributable. Three cases must be distinguished.

One did not see it. That is not responsibility, that is misfortune.

One saw it and could not prevail. The standing was insufficient, the resistance too broad, the moment wrong. This is the tragic case, and it is more common than posterity assumes. Insight and the power to act are two different things.

One saw it, could have acted, and did not. Only this case is fully attributable.

In the case described, the shares are distributed and must be told apart. Allowing oneself to be put off where one could have pressed belongs to the one who allowed it. The sidelining that followed belongs to others. Whoever throws both together is engaged in self-accusation — which is merely the next form of the account, with the sign reversed.

VII. Why the account hardens with age

People attribute successes to themselves and failures to circumstance. This is well documented and holds for almost everyone.

Less often considered is that the tendency increases with age, for a reason that has nothing to do with character. As long as a life is running, an honest reckoning can be of use; one can do it differently next time. Where no next time is to be expected, revision only costs. It takes away the account and returns nothing.

The account then becomes the last remaining holding. Whoever touches it is not attacking an argument but the only thing still there.

This explains why such conversations almost never succeed. It does not make the account any truer.

VIII. Structure and actors

It is more comfortable to write about wiring than about people. Wiring does not answer back, is not offended, and need not be named.

And it is true enough: where a structure forces an outcome, the individual is interchangeable. A head of design under deadline pressure does what he is there for. A design engineer who appeals to established practice follows his training.

But a structure does not act. It makes certain decisions likely — it does not make them inevitable. That is why in every structure there are the few who do otherwise, and those few refute the excuse of all the rest. If the structure really compelled, then all criticism of it would be pointless too, for it would equally compel one not to change it.

The structure explains why many do the same thing. The actor explains why some do not. Without structure, every error becomes a defect of character. Without actors, nobody can be addressed any longer — and then one may as well stop writing.

IX. What remains

No more than two questions, and they are addressed to nobody in particular.

The first: Where did I see, could have acted, and did nothing? It is uncomfortable, because there is nothing to look up. One has to remember rather than consult.

The second is addressed to anyone with something to propose, and it is practical: What will be in the records if I do not put it there?

Nothing. A proposal that is not on file never took place. A postponement that nobody minutes is not a decision but merely time passing. And in forty years there will be a solution that was built, with an article and a patent application — and a Didn’t we want to do it that way back then? to which there is nothing to reply.
