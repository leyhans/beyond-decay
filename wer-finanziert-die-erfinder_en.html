Who Finances the Inventors?

What the Landesbanken have learned since Nord/GI, and what they have not

Addendum to: The Silicon Valley Built on Sand

“The Silicon Valley Built on Sand” ends with a question: who finances the inventors? The answer back then was: nobody. Forty years later it has improved, but not thanks to the Landesbanken, the German regional state banks, and not for every inventor.

I. What the Landesbanken financed instead

While Nord/GI was demanding interest from an inventor after three months, the Landesbanken were getting to know risk in another field. According to a compilation by the former member of the Bundestag Gerhard Schick, rescuing German banks has cost at least 68 billion euros since 2007, more than 30 billion of it for Landesbanken. The rescue of HSH Nordbank, which had overreached itself with ship loans, cost around 11 billion euros; that of BayernLB, after losses on American credit securities and on Hypo Alpe Adria, around 10 billion. WestLB was wound up in 2012. Nord/LB itself had to be rescued in 2019 with 3.6 billion euros, likewise because of bad ship loans.

Why the public banks in particular was examined in 2009 by the economists Harald Hau and Marcel Thum. The Landesbanken then held 21 per cent of the total assets of the German banking market but accounted for 43 per cent of all write-downs in the financial crisis. Hau and Thum analysed the biographies of 593 supervisory board members. In the public banks they found markedly less experience in finance and management, and the less of it a supervisory board had, the higher the losses.

One can sum it up like this: the Landesbanken were bold where they understood nothing, with credit securities from America, container ships and a bank in the Balkans. They were cautious where they could have understood something, with the inventor in their own region.

II. Nord/LB today

Nord/LB is healthy again. In 2025 it achieved its best result since 2015, and in the first half of 2026 it earned 370 million euros before taxes. Its focus, according to its own presentation for investors, is wind and solar parks and infrastructure, with a record 5 billion euros in 2025. On top of that come the large companies in Lower Saxony and its business with the savings banks.

We found no unit for venture capital or for founders. In NORD Holding, which grew out of Nord/GI and today takes over established medium-sized companies, Nord/LB still holds 15 per cent.

To be fair: a wind park is progress too. But it comes with expert reports, purchase agreements and collateral. An inventor comes with an idea and a patent.

III. Who is involved at all

Among the large Landesbanken, only LBBW in Stuttgart has run a genuine venture capital subsidiary, since 1998. It has made around 90 investments, mostly up to about three million euros, in young companies in software, medicine and financial technology, and it usually invests together with others rather than leading. Helaba in Frankfurt invests through Helaba Digital in young companies that, by its own account, are meant to complement its own business model. That is strategy for the bank, not support for inventors.

BayernLB calls itself a “progress financier” in its current advertising. With the former Tagesschau newsreader Jan Hofer it declares that much of what we cling to today will soon be fit for a museum: “Later always makes earlier look old.” What it actually finances, the campaign does not say. Young technology companies in Bavaria are financed through the state development bank LfA and its subsidiary Bayern Kapital, not through the Landesbank.

IV. What the market has learned

What Nord/GI did back then, a “participation” with running interest, exists today under an honest name. It is called venture debt: loans to young companies, at interest of 10 to 14 per cent a year. The difference lies in the order. Venture debt is available only to those who have already raised equity from venture capitalists, and then usually 20 to 50 per cent of that last round. Very early companies without professional investors, according to the industry’s own guides, rarely meet the requirements.

So the market has learned that interest may come only once the equity is in place. A gardener waters first and harvests later. Nord/GI wanted to harvest before anything was sown.

Others have since taken over the early phase. The state High-Tech Gründerfonds gives young technology companies up to one million euros, and the development banks of the federal states add to that. At the universities, founders’ centres have arisen that did not exist in 1985. UnternehmerTUM in Munich, founded in 2002 by Susanne Klatten, has been named Europe’s best start-up hub by the Financial Times three times in a row and, by its own account, produces more than 140 scalable companies every year.

V. The answer, forty years later

Our conclusion: the question “Who finances the inventors?” has a better answer today than in 1985, but only for a certain kind of inventor. Whoever comes from a university, has a team, presents a business plan and promises rapid growth finds programmes, funds and mentors. Whoever works alone, without a university behind them, on a machine that takes years before it can be sold, finds nothing at the Landesbanken and little elsewhere.

The Landesbanken have learned from their losses to be more cautious. They have not learned to understand inventors. The house on sand is gone. In its place they have not built a house for inventors.

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo (Anthropic) — Nuremberg, 11 November 2026.
