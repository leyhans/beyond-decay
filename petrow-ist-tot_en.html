Who Saves the World – Petrov Is Dead

The nuclear satellite and the abolished minute

Addendum to: Where Is Petrov? · COSMOS

In 1983 a single officer saved the world because he had a few minutes and distrusted his instrument. A nuclear warhead on a satellite takes both away from him.

I. The report

According to information leaked to the press, Russia could very soon put a nuclear warhead into space, not on a rocket for immediate detonation but on a satellite lying in wait. This was reported by the Frankfurter Allgemeine Sonntagszeitung on 27 November. Chancellor Merz called the news “shocking”.

Only the timing is new. The US government announced back in 2024 that Russia was working on such a satellite. At the centre is Kosmos 2553, a satellite Russia placed in an otherwise unused orbit in February 2022. In April 2026 the commander of the US Space Force, General Stephen Whiting, warned of a “space Pearl Harbor”.

What such an explosion does has been known since 1962. In the American test “Starfish Prime” a 1.4-megaton bomb exploded at an altitude of 400 kilometres. Street lights failed over Hawaii and several satellites were destroyed, among them the first television satellite in history. At that time low Earth orbit was almost empty; today tens of thousands of satellites circle there. According to an estimate by the German Institute for International and Security Affairs (SWP), even a relatively weak explosion would knock out five to ten per cent of them immediately and the rest gradually.

What is remarkable is what the experts in the same article say themselves. Ralph Thiele, former chief of staff at the NATO Defense College, considers the threat “possibly just a bluff”, because the bomb would also destroy Russia's own satellites. Retired Lieutenant General Friedrich Ploeger sees in it “typical Russian propaganda behaviour” aimed “above all at the population in Germany”. And the German government's spokesman said: “I did not speak of concern.”

II. The abolished minute

In “Where Is Petrov?” we described in March how Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Petrov, on the night of 26 September 1983, did not pass on a missile alarm from the Soviet early-warning system because he distrusted it, and how the system reprimanded him for it instead of fostering people like him. “Petrov’s Failure and Eichmann’s Reliability” dealt with the contrast between the one who doubts and the one who reliably carries out orders. Both texts are about people: about military and administration weeding out the Petrov type. Petrov died in May 2017. The nuclear satellite goes one step further. It also removes the technical preconditions for doubt.

For his doubt Petrov needed two things: a few minutes and an early-warning system whose report he could check. The bomb on the satellite takes both away. In 1962 the rocket carrying the bomb needed almost fourteen minutes to reach its target altitude, enough time to see where the blow was coming from. On a satellite the bomb can be detonated in a fraction of a second. “They can be detonated without warning time,” says former US Brigadier General Bruce McClintock. And the explosion is meant precisely to blind the early-warning satellites a Petrov would have to rely on.

On the other side stands the sentence with which Ploeger is quoted in the F.A.S.: “The moment a Russian atomic bomb explodes in space would be the moment of the nuclear counterstrike. Then the silos open and the intercontinental missiles launch.” This is the protocol Petrov broke, only this time announced as a plan before anything has happened. In 1983 Ulrich Horstmann wrote of a “secret agreement” to put an end to ourselves. The nuclear satellite is the machine that abolishes doubt, and the announced counterstrike is its counterpart on our own side.

III. Where our own thesis does not hold

In “COSMOS” we wrote in March that the Kessler syndrome is the strongest deterrent in history: whoever destroys in orbit destroys their own satellites too. Thiele relies on the same argument when he speaks of a bluff.

But it only holds between opponents who have equally much to lose. According to the counts of the astronomer Jonathan McDowell, at the end of October 2026 the United States had around 13,000 working satellites in space, China around 1,470, Europe around 1,340 and Russia around 370. Whoever has least in orbit loses least by its destruction. Against them it deters most weakly. We therefore have to qualify our thesis: the deterrent of a destroyed orbit works between those who have a lot in space, and hardly against the one who has little.

The most likely brake on Russia would be China, whose large satellite networks are growing fast and which would lose a great deal in an explosion. McClintock is counting on this too when he proposes building diplomatic pressure with China's help.

IV. Who saves the world?

Petrov is dead, and the technology at issue here is built so that a second Petrov would no longer get the chance. What can save the world then is only what is decided beforehand. That includes not declaring today that an explosion in space, however grave, is the trigger for nuclear war. Whoever announces now that the silos will then open has abolished doubt before there is any occasion for it.

The minute Petrov had no longer exists in space. It has to be built into the procedures while there is still time. Why the species tends to build such machines against itself at all we described in “The Beast”.

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo (Anthropic) — Nuremberg, 28 November 2026.
