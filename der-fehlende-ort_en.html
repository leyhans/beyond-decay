The Missing Place of Nightmares

A documentation centre that has been decided and is not being built

Addendum to: Bird Droppings or Catastrophe?

“Bird Droppings or Catastrophe?” ended with the sentence that whoever wants to prevent new barbarism must place the victims at the centre and give them a name. For the victims of the German occupation of Europe, the Bundestag decided on a place for exactly that. Six years later it does not exist.

I. The decision

On 9 October 2020 the Bundestag decided to create a documentation centre “Second World War and German Occupation Rule in Europe”. In the centre of Berlin it is to show what the Germans did in the countries they occupied. Some 230 million people in what are today 27 European states lived under that occupation.

In spring 2022 the German Historical Museum presented a detailed proposal. In October 2023 the Bundestag called on the government to set up a foundation and to equip it with money and staff so that the centre can be built.

Since then a small unit has been working in the museum, living from year to year. In 2025 it showed an exhibition on the first exhibitions about Nazi occupation, and since May 2026 it has been hosting a series of talks. A foundation with its own budget, a site and a timetable do not exist. In May 2025 the Minister of State for Culture, Wolfram Weimer, said the centre “is to” be built in a central location. Nothing of it has become visible since. The government does not explain why, and we impute no intention to it.

II. National nightmares

The Bulgarian political scientist Ivan Krastev recently summed up the reason for such a centre in one sentence: Europeans share common dreams, but their nightmares are purely national.

In Germany, apart from the extermination camps, people know a few place names from the occupation. Oradour, where the Waffen-SS murdered almost 650 people in June 1944. Lidice, Distomo, Kalavryta, the Ardeatine Caves. Remembrance gathers in these names. What the occupation was in everyday life they do not tell: forced labour, shootings, looting, hunger, rape, lawlessness. In Norway and in Greece, in Poland, in Belarus and in Ukraine, in France and in the former Yugoslavia, almost every family has such a story. Each nation remembers its own. A place where one sees them all together is missing, and it is missing precisely in the country they are all about.

III. The strongest army

In May 2025 Chancellor Merz announced that the Bundeswehr should become the strongest conventional army in Europe. In February 2026 he repeated it in Munich. Many neighbours welcome this today because they seek protection from Russia. The historian Joachim von Puttkamer reminds readers in the F.A.Z. that German soldiers play the leading role in their nightmares.

A country that asks its neighbours to trust its army should know what that army did to them. And it should show that it knows. That is what the centre was meant for.

IV. Deciding and paying

In March 2025 the Bundestag loosened the debt brake so that defence spending above a certain threshold no longer falls under it. Since then hundreds of billions of euros have been at stake for tanks, ammunition and vehicles, as we described in “Industrial Subsidy in Camouflage”. For a foundation that would build a memorial centre, the budget decision has been missing for three years.

That is the pattern. Remembrance as a decision costs nothing. Remembrance as a place costs money, land and staff, and it does so permanently. The Bundestag took the decision with a broad majority. Nobody is paying for the place.

V. What is missing

In “Bird Droppings or Catastrophe?” we wrote that history is a store of components that are all still there. One of them is the distance between the one who decides and the one who suffers. The centre was meant to shorten that distance by showing the Germans what their grandfathers did in Europe, place by place, family by family.

The army is being built. The place where one learns what Europe was once afraid of is missing.
