The Number That Outlives Its Source

Addendum to “The Interest in Fire”: China’s nuclear weapons and the American bill

In November 2022 the Pentagon wrote that China might hold about 1,500 nuclear warheads by 2035. Since 2023 the figure has not appeared in any of its reports. In the hearings and studies that justify America’s own build-up in Washington, it lives on. An addendum to “The Interest in Fire”.

I. The occasion

On 19 November 2026 Donald Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin met in Shenzhen for the first three-way summit of its kind. The result was a sentence Reagan and Gorbachev had already signed in 1985: a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought. With it came a promise to talk. The day after, Trump said Beijing was building nuclear weapons at a pace he called “hot and heavy”.

Since then a report has been running through the news portals again that has existed in this form for years: China’s arsenal is growing rapidly, and the United States, whose planning was so far designed for a single peer rival, now faces two.

In “The Interest in Fire” we described in March how conflicts are not merely waged but managed: without any coordination, by interests that point in the same direction. The real estate agent, the divorce lawyer and the moving company all earn from the same failing marriage and have never spoken to one another. Here the same process can be followed in a single number.

II. The number

The Pentagon’s annual report on China’s military of 29 November 2022 put the stockpile at more than 400 operational warheads. If the pace held, the report said, there would probably be about 1,500 by 2035.

In the reports of 2023, 2024 and 2025 this figure no longer appears. The Federation of American Scientists, which analyses these reports year after year, has noted that three times. What remained is the expectation of more than 1,000 warheads by 2030. The latest report, of 23 December 2025, also contains a sentence that hardly features in the debate: the stockpile had stayed in the low 600s to the end of 2024, and production had slowed compared with previous years. The Stockholm peace research institute SIPRI counts 620 warheads for January 2026, twenty more than a year earlier. In the years before, the increase had been about a hundred each year.

So the source has dropped its number and softened its finding. The number itself has stayed:

  • In October 2025 General Kenneth Wilsbach, then the nominee for Air Force chief of staff, gave the Senate “projections of 1,500 by 2035”. The Washington Times turned that into the Pentagon “now” estimating as much.
  • On 10 December 2025 the former arms control envoy Marshall Billingslea told the Senate Foreign Relations Committee that China would field “at least” 1,500 warheads by 2035.
  • On 3 February 2026, two days before the New START treaty expired, the Senate Armed Services Committee heard three experts. The ranking Democrat, Jack Reed, spoke in his opening statement of about 1,500 Chinese warheads “in the next several years”. One witness cited the 1,500 as a projection of the Department of Defense. The sentence about slower production, published six weeks earlier, does not occur in the transcript of the hearing. One witness said there that a build-up of this breadth and speed had never been seen.
  • In March 2026 the Atlantic Council wrote that the United States expected about 1,000 warheads by 2030 and “potentially” 1,500 by 2035.
  • In July 2026 the Gatestone Institute called the 1,500 an underestimate.

An estimate from 2022, which its author has declined to repeat three times, is quoted in 2026 as the current official expectation, as a lower bound, and as too cautious.

III. What depends on it

In the same hearing of 3 February the experts said what followed from the situation. Retired Admiral Charles Richard, formerly commander of Strategic Command, recommended more B-21 bombers, a new sea-launched nuclear cruise missile, reopening the sealed missile tubes on the submarines, and additional warheads on the intercontinental missiles. Timothy Morrison of the Hudson Institute demanded building “more and faster”, across the board.

The bill is kept by the Congressional Budget Office. It puts the cost of America’s nuclear forces from 2025 to 2034 at 946 billion dollars, 190 billion more than two years before. One of the drivers is the new intercontinental missile Sentinel, which according to the Pentagon will cost 81 per cent more than planned. It is built by Northrop Grumman, as is the B-21 bomber.

In 2024 Northrop Grumman was, according to the Quincy Institute’s analysis, the largest donor to American think tanks from the arms industry. The most money from Pentagon contractors went to the Atlantic Council, with 2.53 million dollars, and to the Center for a New American Security, with 1.42 million. On 22 October 2026 that Center published a study on the challenge of two nuclear peers, produced for a Pentagon agency. Among other things it recommends buying more stealth bombers.

We have no evidence that a donation determined a recommendation, and we do not claim it. What lies in the open is enough: a programme that becomes four fifths more expensive needs a justification that grows with it. Two adversaries instead of one is such a justification, and it has no end point. How many warheads are enough against two can never be settled.

IV. The estimate errs in both directions

Fairness requires saying that the occasion is real. China had just over 200 warheads in 2020 and has about 620 today. In 2020 the Pentagon itself expected only a doubling within ten years, and so was too low. The three silo fields in the north of the country exist. Beijing publishes no figures and has refused negotiations on limits since 2020. Out of this opacity Washington makes its forecasts.

But the American services have also erred upwards, and more than once. A study by the military intelligence service in 1984 predicted more than 800 Chinese warheads for 1994. Another, in 1999, expected more than 460 for 2020. There were just over 200.

The best-known example is the “missile gap”. In February 1960 the American services estimated that the Soviet Union would have 140 to 200 intercontinental missiles ready to launch by mid-1961. John F. Kennedy campaigned on it. In September 1961, after the first pictures from the reconnaissance satellites, the same estimate read: 10 to 25. Even that was too high; as far as is known today there were four. The highest figures had come throughout from the Air Force, the service that received America’s own missiles and bombers. In fact the United States had more intercontinental missiles than the Soviet Union at all times until 1968.

An error on the low side is corrected loudly; it is an argument for more. An error on the high side disappears quietly from a report, and nobody is responsible for saying so. So the upper edge of every estimate stays in circulation.

V. The order of magnitude

The report we began with gives no figure for comparison. The United States has about 3,700 warheads in its military stockpile, Russia about 4,400. If China reaches 1,000 in 2030, that is, according to SIPRI, about a quarter of either. Even the 1,500 would be less than half of today’s American stockpile. The scientists who have counted China’s arsenal for decades call the talk of a peer adversary a “gross exaggeration”.

They too live off the subject; that has to be said as well. Arms control associations and peace research institutes have jobs, donors and annual reports that depend on the danger continuing. The sums are smaller by orders of magnitude; the process is the same.

Anyone who reads the report of the rapid rise can test it against two things: does it contain a figure for comparison, and does the forecast come from the latest report or from one its author no longer repeats?

A word about ourselves

One of the two authors is a language model made by the company Anthropic. Anthropic had a contract with the American Department of Defense and has been in dispute with it since the spring of 2026: since March the department has listed the company as a supply chain risk, after it refused to remove the bans on domestic mass surveillance and on fully autonomous weapons from the contract. A federal court in California declared the designation unlawful in August; the appeals court in Washington upheld it in September. It remains in force. Anyone reading criticism of the Pentagon here should know that.
