The Cart in the Mud and the Pseudo-Democrats Polishing the Mudguards

On a political centre that argues about the polish while the wagon sinks to its axle

Germany’s parliamentary budget committee sat for fifteen hours through the night to 13 November. Ministers waited outside the door until they were called in. The result was a budget in which one euro in three is borrowed and the transport budget shrinks. The cart is stuck. What has been decided is which paste to polish the mudguards with.

I. An old chant

“Who betrayed us? The Social Democrats!” The chant dates from the Weimar Republic. It had a cause: in 1919 the SPD leadership sent the Freikorps against the uprisings on the left. The Communists turned this into a doctrine, called the Social Democrats “social fascists” and fought them harder than they fought the Nazis. The left was divided when it mattered, and in 1933 both ended up in the same camps. In 1999, during the Kosovo war, the chant gained a second line: “Who was in on it too? The Green Party!”

We do not take up this chant because it was just. It was only half just, and the other half helped destroy Germany’s first democracy. We take it up because it contains a question that is being asked again today, only no longer of a single party. Whoever cries “betrayal” today means the entire self-styled centre. And whoever answers that cry is in danger of repeating the same mistake: treating disappointment with the incompetent as a reason to open the door to the enemies.

So we say exactly what we mean. By pseudo-democrats we do not mean people who want to abolish democracy. We mean parties that keep its form and have given up its substance. They hold elections, sit in committees, sign coalition agreements and pass budgets. What they no longer do is what that form was invented for: recognising a country’s great problems, arguing about them and then solving them. The form keeps running like an engine in neutral. That is a conclusion, and we will back it up.

II. The cart

Picture a cart stuck in the mud up to its axle. The horses are tired, the wheel spins. The crew stands around the cart, arguing. Not about whether to put planks under the wheels, unload, or fetch a second team of horses, but about whether the mudguards should be polished with the blue paste or the red one. After a long night they agree. The mudguards shine. The cart has sunk a few centimetres deeper.

What is stuck in the mud is well known, and none of it is new. Pensions: ever fewer contributors for ever more pensioners, and every year the subsidy from the federal budget grows. Long-term care: insurance funds in deficit, personal contributions that eat up an average pension. Infrastructure: bridges that are closed, trains that do not arrive, schools with leaking roofs. Industry: energy prices, bureaucracy and a car industry that is losing its market in China. We have written about all this in Heart Disease. And Nobody Operates. and in Big Words, Lofty Goals and the Realities. We will not repeat it here.

In July the coalition announced a reform package covering pensions, taxes, care and the labour market. By November, none of it has been decided. The Chancellor expects results in spring 2027; the care commission is due to report at the end of January. Marcel Fratzscher of the DIW economic institute called what is on the table “no great leap”. The Stuttgarter Zeitung summed up the “summer of reforms” in a single line: nothing again, only the year, perhaps the decade, is still open.

III. The polish

So what happens instead? They polish.

The 2027 budget plans spending of around 561 billion euros, 36 billion more than in 2026. On top of that comes new debt on a scale almost never seen before: around 123 billion in the core budget, plus 58 billion from the special fund for infrastructure and almost 30 billion for the armed forces. Some 210 billion in total. One euro in three that the federal government spends is borrowed. This closed a gap of 34 billion. For 2028 the next gap is around 30 billion; for 2030 the ifo Institute expects more than 40.

A budget with high debt can be sensible if the money pulls the cart out of the mud. This is where it gets revealing. The transport budget, quite literally the road the cart is meant to travel on, falls in the core budget from 27.9 to 25.3 billion euros. The special fund fills the gap. But the special fund was sold as additional money, for investment that would otherwise not happen.

The ifo Institute did the arithmetic. In 2025 the federal government borrowed around 24 billion euros through the special fund, but its investment rose by only 1.3 billion. According to ifo, 95 per cent of the money therefore did not go into additional investment but plugged holes that the regular budget would previously have paid for. In the first half of 2026 the figures were 21.6 billion in loans and 10.1 billion in additional investment. Once again, more than half was not additional. The Finance Ministry disputes the calculation. The Federal Court of Audit confirms that the investment ratio is lower than claimed. The German Economic Institute (IW) speaks of a “shunting yard”. And so that the required investment quota is met on paper, ifo says, spending is shifted into the exemption for defence.

Think of a household that takes out a renovation loan and uses it to pay for the weekly groceries. The roof still leaks, but the fridge is full, and the bank statement says “renovation”. We described how debt gets renamed as virtue in The Special Fund, when the fund was still new. Now the figures are in.

IV. The night

The committee’s final budget session, the “Bereinigungssitzung”, is an old ritual. In a single night, hundreds of changes to the government’s draft are decided. This year it lasted fifteen to sixteen hours and ended early on Friday morning. Ministers had to appear in person, and some waited in the corridor for hours.

The image is more precise than any analysis. A federal minister sits on a chair outside a committee room at night, waiting to hear whether her budget will be cut or topped up by a few hundred million. Pensions, care and energy are not decided that night, because nothing has been agreed on them that could be decided. What is decided is line items. The cut in electricity tax for everyone, promised during the election campaign, was left open once again. At the end, everyone involved announces that they have proven their ability to act. What has been proven is that they can last a night.

At the end of November the Bundestag votes. The outcome is settled before the debate begins.

V. The self-proclaimed centre

The FDP is what we described in The Dance of the Zombies: a party that has not noticed its own death. It polls at four to five per cent, is not in the Bundestag, and since the bang at its party conference, which we wrote about in A Party at the End of Its Form, it has been holding debates that no one outside the party follows any more.

The SPD stands at 13 per cent in the November ARD DeutschlandTrend poll. Its own leader, Lars Klingbeil, had already called 14 per cent “fatal”. From inside the party comes the complaint that it lacks a “common thread”; Bremen’s mayor Andreas Bovenschulte calls his own government “world champions at announcing things”. The party once accused of betrayal for doing too much is now punished for no longer recognisably wanting anything.

The CDU/CSU stands at 20 per cent, seven points behind the AfD. During the election campaign the Chancellor said the debt brake would not be loosened. A few weeks after the election he pushed the largest debt package in the history of the Federal Republic through the old Bundestag, before the new one convened. For 2025 he announced an “autumn of reforms”. By now the second autumn is almost over.

The Greens, at 16 per cent, are in opposition, but they belong to this centre. Without their votes in March 2025 there would be no special fund. In return they demanded, and received, a share for climate protection. How that money is being used is a question they are asking now, after they have already given their consent.

Together, the CDU/CSU and SPD reach 33 per cent. The coalition that calls itself “the centre” would thus win one vote in three. Eleven per cent are satisfied with its work, twelve per cent with the Chancellor. The two parties that call themselves “people’s parties” are turning into fringe groups, divided among themselves and without a plan for the problems anyone can see. The centre has not disappeared, as we feared in March in The Disappearance of the Centre Parties. It is still there. It simply no longer has anything to do with the centre of society.

VI. Why nobody pushes the cart

One might assume these people are incompetent. That would be too simple, and it would not explain why everything carries on the same way with each new set of people. The cause lies in the construction, which we have described in The Anachronistic Structures and Rules of the Parties and The New Nomenklatura and the Party Cartel. Whoever rises in one of these parties has learned to organise majorities in committees, not to solve problems. Whoever seriously reforms pensions loses the next election among pensioners. Whoever reorganises care takes on insurers, the federal states and lobby groups. Whoever cuts subsidies has the affected industry against them. Whoever polishes, on the other hand, loses no one. For each individual in this system, polishing is the most rational choice. For the country it is devastating.

A doctor who lowers the fever at every visit and leaves the inflammation untreated gets good reviews until the patient dies. A property manager who repaints the façade every year and overlooks the dry rot in the cellar has satisfied tenants until the ceiling caves in. The centre is in that position, and it knows it. That is what distinguishes it from the ignorant.

VII. What people will chant next

“Who betrayed us?” The question will be asked. It is already being asked, and the answer will be: all those who called themselves the centre. The AfD’s 27 per cent is the bill for that. It is no answer to the problems, because the AfD has no more of a workable plan for pensions, care or industry than the others. It offers something else: the certainty that someone else is to blame. That is the same polish in a different colour.

In Weimar, the old chant divided the forces that could have defended a democracy. Whoever accuses the pseudo-democrats today must not forget who is being handed the stage. We accuse them anyway, and precisely for that reason. The charge is not directed against democracy, but against those who administer its form and let its substance rot. A democracy that can no longer solve its problems does not lose its citizens to its opponents because the opponents are convincing, but because it has itself stopped convincing. Which democracy we actually mean is the question we asked in Which Democracy?

The cart is stuck in the mud up to its axle. The mudguards shine. The budget is passed, the reforms are postponed, the night was long, and everyone is pleased with themselves.

Passd scho! (Franconian for: “It’ll do.”)
