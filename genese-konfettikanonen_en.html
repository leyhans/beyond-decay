The Genesis of the Confetti Cannons

How a capable and critical journalist became a perfect entertainer. There is a book from 2006 that should be read today as an epitaph. Not because its author has died — Gabor Steingart is alive, runs a newsletter, hosts a podcast, stages events. He is more visible than ever. But the person who wrote that book no longer exists.

I. The Book of 2006

Weltkrieg um Wohlstand (World War for Prosperity) is not a kind book. It opens with a sentence that reverberates as a ground note through all four hundred pages: We were born into a world that will soon cease to exist. What follows is not a lament but a diagnosis. A threat assessment, as Steingart himself calls it — the first that the West should have produced and never did.

He takes on everything. Colonial history as the dress rehearsal for globalisation. America's rise and its illusory successes. China's awakening under Deng Xiaoping. India's hesitant opening under Rao. The emergence of a world labour market that overnight placed one and a half billion people in direct competition with three hundred and fifty million expensive Western workers.

The book's power lies not in its numbers, though the numbers are devastating. It lies in the willingness to name things that no one wanted named. He calls the Asian rising powers attacker states — not out of hostility but because the word trading partner obscures reality. He calls the European welfare state, financed through payroll taxes, probably the largest import subsidy programme any state has ever devised — because it exempts foreign producers from the very charges it imposes on domestic ones. He calls Europe's debt-driven growth a narcosis of the people — because sovereign credit had become the opium of the masses.

II. On Ricardo

And then he takes on David Ricardo. Ricardo, the stock market speculator without academic education, who had a million pounds at twenty-five and called himself a chief favourite of fortune. Ricardo, whose theory of comparative advantage became the foundation of the World Trade Organisation, even though it is, as Steingart puts it, theoretical in the worst sense of the word. He dismantles the wine-and-cloth model with three objections, any one of which would suffice: that the specialisation trap drives any nation to decline that relies on its comparative advantages; that financial capital thinks in absolute advantages and finds Ricardo's national logic entirely alien; that the assumed symmetry of trade has never existed and recedes further with every trading day.

He cites Franz Oppenheimer and his theory of equilibrium disruption when too many people offer the same thing. He cites Erhard and the termite state in which the collective assigns the individual his tasks. He conducts an interview with the ninety-one-year-old Nobel laureate Paul Samuelson, who calmly confirms what Steingart has laid out across four hundred pages: that the globalisation balance sheet has tipped against the West, that there are losers, and that it is a great error to claim otherwise. And Steingart closes his chapter on Ricardo with a sentence that sounds like a eulogy for an entire epoch of economic thought: Their patron saint Marx has meanwhile been buried. It is time to pay David Ricardo the last honours as well.

III. Twenty Years Later

That was 2006. It was a book that spoke plainly, that asked systemic questions, that demanded of its readers that they seek answers rather than moods. The author was head of Der Spiegel's Berlin bureau; he had access, he had authority, and he used both not to court but to diagnose.

What happened to this person?

In 2025, Gabor Steingart runs Pioneer, a newsletter with podcast and event format. Every morning at six he delivers to his subscribers a summary of the day: five points, three minutes, one conclusion. The Draghi Report on European competitiveness — four hundred pages of deep analysis — is referenced in a single briefing, summarily praised, not analysed. Symptoms are listed, causes are not questioned. The why? is missing. It is missing not occasionally but systematically.

One might say: Steingart has become more pragmatic. More realistic. More marketable. But that would be — to turn his own method against him — a trivialisation of what has actually occurred.

What has occurred can be captured in a single instrument: the confetti cannon. Take other people's work — a four-hundred-page report, a government declaration, a study — cut it into colourful strips, throw it into the air, and call it conclusion. The confetti cannon produces effect without insight, attention without comprehension, the appearance of analysis without its substance.

The business model behind it is as intelligent as it is cynical: close enough to the truth to seem credible, far enough away to lose no one. The Steingart of 2006 called something like this narcosis of the people. He wrote: The mood-politician of the new type has drawn the lesson that his voters do not demand problem-solving but are already satisfied with skilful problem-displacement. He described the mood-politician — and then became one. Only not in politics but in journalism.

IV. A Pattern, Not an Isolated Case

The transformation is not an isolated case; it is a pattern. In Germany there is a remarkable tradition: people who in their younger years named things plainly become, over the course of their careers, what they once described. The critic becomes the operation. The diagnosis becomes content. The truth, once published as a book and honoured with prizes, becomes a business card whose text no one reads any more.

Steingart wrote a chapter in 2006 titled The New Honesty. In it he calls for a revolution in consciousness modelled on the environmental movement: that one must stop lying to oneself about the situation, that the willpower of peoples is the most frequently underestimated productive force, that once awakened it can accomplish great and magnificent things.

Twenty years later, the same author delivers to his readership at six in the morning a pleasantly tempered summary in which everything is referenced and nothing understood. In which problems appear as bullet points and causes remain in the realm of the vague. The honesty he demanded in 2006 would be fatal to his business model. Because honesty is not a profitable investment on the subscriber market — he wrote almost the same sentence about the voter market.

V. The Real Question

The real question is not why Steingart changed. The real question is what it says about a country when its sharpest analysts become confetti cannoneers. When diagnosis is not refined but abandoned. When the findings of 2006 — that the productive core of the West is shrinking, that the narcosis of debt obscures reality, that Ricardo must be buried — are not developed over twenty years but forgotten.

Steingart demanded of his readers in 2006 that they endure two hundred pages on Asia before he offered a single strategy. He demanded that they watch Ricardo dismantled over thirty pages, though most of them probably did not know who Ricardo was. He demanded that they take seriously the last sentence of his preface: Good politics begins with speaking what is. This book aims to encourage that.

Speaking what is — that cannot be done every morning at six. Not in five points. Not with sponsors behind you and subscribers in view. The confetti cannon is not a tool of laziness; it is the operating system of a media logic that has replaced analysis with reference, diagnosis with enumeration, asking why with reporting what.

And therein lies the true irony: Steingart wrote a book about decline in 2006. He described decline, analysed it, documented it with numbers, supported it with historical parallels, enriched it with voices from Samuelson to Oppenheimer. And then he chose to become part of it himself.

Not as a cynic. Probably not even consciously. But because the business model of the newsletter demands what the business model of the book forbade: proximity to the reader instead of imposition, confirmation instead of disruption, speed instead of depth. The confetti cannon is the logical consequence of a media economy that values attention more highly than understanding.

Steingart wrote in 2006: With the concern for voter-friendly communication of insight, the insight itself disappears. He was speaking about politicians. He could have been speaking about himself — twenty years later.

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo (Anthropic) — Nuremberg, February 2026.
