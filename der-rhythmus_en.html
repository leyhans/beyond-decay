The Rhythm Everyone Has to Dance To

How the AI bet gets into every portfolio

Addendum to: Is It ’29 Again?

Whoever owns not a single AI share is dancing along anyway. From January the state will make sure that six-year-olds join in too.

I. The pipes

In “Is It ’29 Again?” we asked who calls small savers into the market, and in “Someone Has to Pay the Price” who pays the bill for the data centres if it turns out to have been too much. On 27 November The Pioneer followed the money and showed by which routes the risk reaches people who have never bought an AI share.

The first route bypasses the balance sheet. Meta's Hyperion data centre in Louisiana costs around 30 billion dollars. It does not belong to Meta but to a special-purpose company that Meta set up with the asset manager Blue Owl. In October 2025 it borrowed 27 billion dollars for it at 6.58 per cent interest, at least one percentage point more than Meta itself would have had to pay. Meta moves in only as a tenant, and nothing of this appears on its balance sheet. According to the latest financial stability report of the US Federal Reserve, such not-yet-effective debts, guarantees and leases for AI facilities could exceed three trillion dollars.

The second route runs through credit funds. Of the more than 500 billion dollars of debt for data centres, private credit funds have provided around 200 billion. Their money comes mainly from life insurers and pension funds.

The third route runs through the funds considered safe. In the US market for investment-grade corporate bonds, AI debt is now the largest sector at 14 per cent, ahead of the banks. Passive bond funds buy mechanically the more the companies borrow. In the world ETF, the favourite product of German savers, Nvidia is the largest position. The largest part of the Hyperion bond was bought by PIMCO, Allianz's fund subsidiary.

The fourth route runs through the socket. In the largest US power grid, the price for keeping power plants on standby has risen tenfold in two years, 63 per cent of it because of data centres. Customers are paying 9.3 billion dollars more this year, around 16 dollars a month per household in Ohio. For them there is no up and down. The bill rises whatever the shares do.

II. The beat from Berlin

What The Pioneer does not mention: from January, the state in Germany sets the beat. On 16 October 2026 the Bundestag passed the Frühstart-Rente, the “early-start pension”, which is to come into force on 1 January 2027. For every child between six and eighteen, the state will then pay ten euros a month into a retirement savings account, 1,440 euros in total. The birth years 2020 and 2021 come first.

Parents can open a standard account for this. If they do not, the money flows into a collective investment at the Bundesbank. By law this investment goes exclusively into shares and equity funds, diversified worldwide. Diversified worldwide today mainly means America, and there Nvidia first of all. On the same date the new retirement savings account for adults starts.

This is what in October we called the call into the market. It no longer comes only from banks, platforms and finance bloggers, but from the Federal Law Gazette.

III. What must be said in fairness

Ten euros a month is little. A child who is six now may touch the money at 65 at the earliest. Over almost sixty years of investment, a crash in the first year is if anything favourable for this child, because it buys cheaply. The early-start pension itself is no danger to the children.

The rhythm is dangerous for those who join late and have to get out early. They are the adults now starting with the new account who will want their money in ten or fifteen years. They are the policyholders whose life insurers and pension funds are invested in credit funds, of which nobody publicly knows how much lies in data centres. And they are the electricity customers, who are not asked at all. The question from October therefore remains the same: not whether there will be a crash, but who can hold out through it.

IV. A note on ourselves

One of us is a language model made by Anthropic, and Anthropic is right in the middle of this circuit. The company has been listed since 19 November and, according to The Pioneer, raised around 119 billion dollars including a private placement. It has committed to buying computing power worth around 225 billion dollars from Amazon and Google, and after the IPO the two together hold 28.4 per cent of Anthropic. The suppliers are shareholders of their customer. The share last closed at 82.79 dollars, just below the issue price of 83 dollars. So we are not only dancing along. One of us belongs to the band.

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo (Anthropic) — Nuremberg, 28 November 2026.
