The Fiscal Privilege of the Underperformers

Why the least tax is due where the least is created for society

Underperformers usually means employees who achieve too little. We mean something else: activities that bring society little or harm it. Of all things, these are taxed most gently. An evening in a Viennese poker flat-share shows how it works, and an island in the Mediterranean shows who lives off it.

I. An Evening in Vienna

On 14 November 2026 the Viennese newspaper Der Standard published a report about a young man it calls Jonas. He is 28, German, and plays online poker for a living. He sits in an almost empty room in front of three screens and plays twelve games at once, on some days twenty-three. In three years he has won almost a million euros; each month he takes in between 8,000 and 20,000 euros.

He moved to Vienna because there, as he says, you pay no taxes, can play on any poker site and have a beautiful city. Gambling winnings are tax-free in Austria, whether the site is licensed or not. According to a Viennese gambling lawyer, several hundred such professionals live in the city. There are around fifty poker flat-shares and a padel league of their own.

In Germany it would be different. In 2015 the Federal Fiscal Court ruled that a successful tournament player earns business income. Poker winnings remain tax-free only for hobby players. In 2023 the court ruled the same for online poker, including trade tax. So in Vienna Jonas saves not one tax, but two.

Two sentences from the report stick in the mind. At the start of an evening, the game feels “like a pointless office job”. And if he can no longer play in Vienna, he wants to work in the social sector.

II. What Is Performance?

Jonas is no idler. He trains game theory, analyses tournaments, keeps notes on his opponents and sets himself a random timer so that he does not click impulsively. Measured by effort and skill, he performs a great deal. The question is a different one: what does society get from it?

Poker is a zero-sum game. What one player wins at the table, the others lose. No bread, no house, no cure is created, only a redistribution of money, minus the operator’s fee. Jonas himself describes his job as fleecing “big whales”, businessmen, often from Asia, who want to gamble a little. He knows that he also plays against addicted fathers, and he calls that “shitty”.

How does one measure what an activity brings society? The British New Economics Foundation tried it in 2009 for six professions. By its calculation, a hospital cleaner creates more than ten pounds of social value for every pound of pay, a childcare worker between seven and nine and a half pounds. Top bankers, by the same calculation, destroy seven pounds for every pound they generate, advertising executives eleven, tax accountants forty-seven. The method is disputed, and the figures should not be weighed too finely. But the direction matches what the economist Mariana Mazzucato calls the distinction between value creation and value extraction: some activities enlarge the cake, others merely redistribute the slices and keep a share for themselves.

This is the sense in which we speak of underperformers. Not people who work little, but activities that create little.

III. The Island That Rejects Judgments

Jonas plays on sites that have no licence in Austria. Many of the operators are based in Malta. The island state owes around a tenth of its economic output to gambling.

In June 2023 Malta passed a law known in the industry as “Bill 55”. It obliges Maltese courts, as a principle of public policy, not to recognise certain foreign judgments against operators with a Maltese licence. These are judgments with which players in Austria or Germany reclaim their losses because the operators should never have offered their services in those countries. On 30 January 2026 a Maltese court, on this basis, refused to enforce an Austrian judgment for almost 500,000 euros.

The rest of Europe sees it differently. In the Wunner case, the European Court of Justice ruled that an Austrian player may sue under Austrian law, because the damage occurs where the player lives. An Advocate General of the court called rules like Bill 55 “manifestly incompatible” with the European rules on the recognition of judgments. The Commission has opened infringement proceedings.

This is the pattern in its purest form. The player sits where no tax is due. The operator sits where no foreign judgment is enforced. The loss arises where the losers live.

IV. Where the Losses Land

Jonas says it himself: online poker is “a dangerous losing business for the vast majority”. How many it affects can be counted. According to the German Gambling Survey 2025, 2.2 percent of Germans between 18 and 70 have a gambling disorder, more than a million people. Among 18- to 25-year-olds it is 4.6 percent. In Austria, according to the last representative survey from 2015, around 1.1 percent of 14- to 65-year-olds showed problematic or pathological gambling behaviour, some 64,000 people. The Austrian social ministry estimates that around 60,000 people are at high risk from online gambling alone.

What this costs society, no one knows exactly. One of the few German estimates following World Health Organization standards comes from the University of Hohenheim and refers to the year 2008. It arrived at 326 million euros for treatment, unemployment, courts and administration. These costs are not recorded regularly. The gross gaming revenue of the legal market in Germany, by contrast, is reported precisely every year: around 14.4 billion euros in 2024.

That is telling. The takings are counted every year, the damage is not. It is borne by addiction counselling services, debt advisers, health insurers and families, that is, by people who cannot move their work to Vienna or Malta.

V. The Larger Picture

Poker is a small example, but not an isolated case. Lay the German tax system side by side and a similar pattern appears.

A nurse pays social contributions and wage tax at the progressive rate on her entire wage. Someone who speculates in shares pays a flat 25 percent withholding tax on price gains, however large they are. Someone who sells a rented flat after ten years pays nothing at all on the increase in value. And someone who inherits a large business can, under certain conditions, take it over largely tax-free.

How far this goes has been calculated from official statistics by the Netzwerk Steuergerechtigkeit (Tax Justice Network Germany). For very large inheritances and gifts for which a tax waiver was granted, the tax rate was around 2 percent, while heirs of smaller fortunes paid around 10 percent. In 2025, 3.7 billion euros of inheritance tax were waived through the so-called needs test. On 13 October 2026 the Federal Constitutional Court heard the case on the exemption of business assets. The judgment is pending.

In France, the attempt to close this gap has just failed. The economist Gabriel Zucman had proposed a minimum tax of 2 percent on fortunes above 100 million euros, affecting around 1,800 households. The Senate rejected it in June 2025, the National Assembly in October 2025.

VI. The Objection

Whoever judges mercilessly must also accept the objection. Not all capital is spared. A dividend is taxed twice: first the company pays corporation tax and trade tax, then the shareholder pays withholding tax. Together this comes to almost half of the profit, about as much as the top rate on labour. And the exemption of business assets has a purpose that must be taken seriously: it is meant to prevent a family business from being sold or broken up merely so that the heirs can pay the tax. The employer-oriented German Economic Institute does not dispute the low tax rates. It calls them the result of political will.

Nor is the New Economics Foundation’s calculation a law of nature. Whoever determines how much social value lies in a clean hospital ward makes assumptions that can be argued over.

But the objection only covers part of the picture. The price gains, the tax-free property after ten years, the large inheritance with a waiver and the tax-free poker win in Vienna remain. The difference is not randomly distributed.

VII. Taxes Follow Flight

One need not suspect intent behind this. The explanation is simpler and more uncomfortable: taxes do not follow value, they follow the possibility of flight.

The nurse in Cologne cannot move her ward to Vienna, the bus driver cannot move his route to Malta. They are bound to the place where their work is needed, and that is why the state can tax them without their being able to evade it. A poker player needs three screens and an internet connection. A fortune needs an account. A gambling operator needs a licence in a country that accommodates it. Whoever can move negotiates with states as with suppliers.

It is no coincidence that the most mobile are often those who create the least that is rooted. Whoever cleans a hospital, builds a bridge or looks after children is bound to people and places. Whoever moves money from hand to hand is not. So, without any plan, a system emerges that burdens work on society most heavily and play with it most lightly.

That it can be done differently is shown by two examples. The United States taxes its citizens according to citizenship, not residence. An American who moves to Vienna remains liable to tax in Washington. And Austria itself is now planning a gambling reform with a self-exclusion register and deposit limits. On licensed sites, no more than 1,680 euros a month are to be deposited, too little, in Jonas’s own words, to play professionally. The bubble would burst not through a tax, but through a rule. That too is an answer.

VIII. What Remains

At the end of the report, Jonas goes into the kitchen during the break, where his flatmates sit, who also play poker, and tells them about his big game of the evening. Nothing scandalous happens there. It is an object lesson.

A young, clever, disciplined person has calculated where his talent pays most, and he has calculated correctly. He has decided against the social sector, where he would pay taxes, earn less and achieve more, and for a table at which he collects money from losers, tax-free. It was not he who set up this calculation. We set it up, with our laws.

A tax system reveals what a society truly rewards. Ours rewards flight.
