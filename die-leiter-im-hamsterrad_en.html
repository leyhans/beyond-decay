The Ladder in the Hamster Wheel

Why we most like to belittle the neighbour, and what it costs

Whoever wants to climb the ladder must above all overtake the neighbour. If the ladder stands in a hamster wheel, the wheel turns with every step, and everyone stays where they were.

I. The rung next door

In the 1990s two Harvard economists, Sara Solnick and David Hemenway, asked a simple question. Prices are the same in two worlds. In world A you earn 50,000 dollars a year and the others 25,000. In world B you earn 100,000 and the others 200,000. Where would you rather live? Half of the respondents chose world A, that is, half the income, as long as it is more than everyone else's. The wish was strongest for praise from a supervisor. Praise only really counts if the colleague gets none.

The psychologist Leon Festinger had already described in 1954 whom we compare ourselves with: not the billionaire and not the homeless man, but those who are similar to us and close to us. The neighbour with the new car hurts more than any chief executive. In the allotment association people argue about the height of a hedge as if it were about property in Monaco. At universities there is the saying attributed to the American political scientist Wallace Sayre: the fights there are so bitter because so little is at stake.

This is not chance but logic. If one's own rank is relative, belittling the person next to you improves your position just as much as your own rise, and it comes cheaper. Those far above or far below play no part in this game. The fight is over the rung next door.

II. I'm OK, you are not

In “I'm OK – You're OK” we described the four life positions of the transactional analysis of Eric Berne and Thomas Harris. The fourth, “I'm OK, you're OK”, is the only one that needs no opponent. The ladder forces a different one: “I'm OK, and what I do is OK. You are not OK, and what you do is rubbish or of no importance.”

There we also noted that economic structures push whole classes into the wrong positions, the worker towards his superior for instance. That is the view upwards. The more common case runs sideways. The position “you are not OK” is taken above all against the colleague, against the competitor, against the one who does the same work. With him it has consequences for one's own place.

III. The wheel

In 1971 the psychologists Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell coined a term that has run through the literature ever since as the “hedonic treadmill”. Whoever gets more gets used to it, and after a while is as content as before. To keep the feeling of progress, he has to keep running. In 1976 the economist Fred Hirsch added why running does not help: some goods are scarce by their very nature, the top position, the best neighbourhood, the front row. When everyone becomes richer, they do not become more common, only more expensive.

If one pictures both together, a strange image results. The ladder does not lean against a wall; it is fixed inside a hamster wheel. Whoever climbs moves the wheel. The rung he has just reached comes round again under his feet. The neighbours climb too, and so the distances stay the same. The wheel is driven by no one but the climbers themselves. It needs no overseer, only people who compare themselves with the one climbing beside them.

IV. The rift

Most people do not lose the ability to see themselves and their work critically. They know what is weak about their performance. They just may not show it, because on the ladder every weakness is exploited at once. So they put themselves in the best light and know at the same time that the light is false.

In 1983 the American sociologist Arlie Hochschild studied flight attendants to find out what it costs to show, as part of the job, feelings one does not have. Whoever does this for a long time loses touch with what he really feels. The psychologist Christina Maslach, whose questionnaire is still used to measure burnout, counts cynicism among its three signs, alongside exhaustion and the feeling of no longer achieving anything. Cynicism is a protection: whoever no longer takes his own self-presentation seriously is no longer burdened by it. Whoever lacks this protection falls ill. The rift between what one knows and what one shows is paid for one way or another.

In the so-called free economy this game is the rule. One of us experienced it for decades in industry: everyone tries to take something from the other in order to present himself better, an idea, a success, a merit. Whoever does not join in is trampled underfoot.

V. Who lives off it

In “The Anthropology of Appropriation” we described which type arrives at the top of the megamachine: the one who carries other people's work upwards and says he did it. The anatomy of this type is set out in “Die selbst ernannten Leistungsträger” (in German). The ladder in the hamster wheel shows where he comes from. He is not especially wicked. He has merely learned the rule of the ladder most thoroughly: that belittling the neighbour brings as much as one's own work, and that appropriating his work brings even more.

The machine needs no tyrants for this. It needs climbers who measure their rank against the person next to them. In “The Moloch” we described how the bottom rung is moving further up for the young. Whoever starts there finds a wheel that is already turning.

VI. Is there a way out?

We have no recipe for this, and we do not want to pretend to have one. In fairness it must be said that comparison does not only destroy. Whoever sees that a colleague can do something better often becomes better himself, and without competition many a craft and many a science would not exist.

One thing can be observed, though. The wheel turns more slowly where one's own rank does not depend on belittling others: in workshops where the master is measured by the workpiece and not by the apprentice, in cooperatives where the proceeds belong to all, in teams where the success of one is the success of all. There the fourth position of Berne and Harris is not a pious wish but the most sensible attitude. If my neighbour's success takes nothing from me, I do not have to make him small.

One does not get out of the wheel by climbing faster. One gets out by ceasing to take the person next door as the yardstick. How many can do that, and how to build structures that make it easier for them, is an open question. It is worth asking again and again.
