We – the Quality Media – Decide the Right Measure of Criticism

Addendum to “The Titanisation of the Confetti Cannon”

On the morning after the largest leak in banking history, Gabor Steingart defends the CEO of Deutsche Bank against the “journalistic guillotine”. That Christian Sewing was guest of honour on his ship in July, he does not mention.

I. The briefing

In July, in “The Titanisation of the Confetti Cannon”, we described how Gabor Steingart wants to moor his planned third media ship in front of Frankfurt’s bank towers. He himself called it a success to have completed the first “Pioneer Finance Week”, “with the Hessian prime minister and the CEO of Deutsche Bank on board the Pioneer Two”.

On 17 November more than 50 media organisations, among them ZDF, Spiegel, the Financial Times and the New York Times, published the investigation “Inside Deutsche Bank”. It is based on more than 15 million internal documents. The next morning Steingart wrote in his Pioneer Briefing about the “most humane guillotine in world history”: the head stays on, only the reputation is gone. The verdict, he wrote, is handed down not in the courtroom but in the newsroom. The media had “de facto suspended” the presumption of innocence. His third example is headed “The Deutsche Bank case: the victim becomes the perpetrator”.

II. What is missing from the briefing

One sentence is missing. It should have read: Christian Sewing was the main guest on my ship in July. The second-largest shareholder in my company, with 35.9 percent, is the Springer group, and its CEO Mathias Döpfner launched the business initiative “Made for Germany” together with Sewing in 2025.

The photo Steingart attaches to his text shows Sewing, of all places, at the annual review of that very initiative. The briefing ends with a line by Kurt Tucholsky: “The big press has no proper ear for the noises in its own interior.” We have nothing to add.

III. The gnat and the elephant

Steingart singles out a sentence Sewing wrote in an internal email in 2017: “KYC is breaking our revenue neck.” KYC stands for “Know Your Customer”, the legally required check of who a customer is. That a manager groans about the cost of such checks is normal, he says. “The gnat makes its entrance as an elephant.”

On its own, the sentence would indeed amount to little. But it does not stand alone. According to the reports, in 2019 alone the bank processed more than 171,000 transactions worth over 20 billion euros for Colombian banks and filed hardly any money-laundering reports that year. In the same year it reported around 582 million dollars in suspicious transactions from Jeffrey Epstein’s circle, after keeping him as a client for six years. Only against this background does the sentence about the neck make sense. Whoever lifts it out does not turn the gnat into an elephant, but the elephant into a gnat.

IV. The victim

“The victim becomes the perpetrator,” Steingart writes. The bank is no victim suddenly falling under suspicion. What its recent history contains we compiled in “We Are a Different Bank Now”. In 2020 the Frankfurt public prosecutor’s office fined it 13.5 million euros in the Danske complex. The FinCEN Files showed in the same year that a team from the internal audit department, then headed by Sewing, had given the Moscow office a clean bill of health although it could not even produce a list of its clients. And the bank’s internal review of the Epstein case repeatedly contains the sentence: “Without negative press this would probably not have been reported.” The bank itself thus recorded that it was the press that made it act.

Steingart also chooses his words as needed when it comes to the origin of the documents. In the first part of the briefing, two “insiders” have “taken” the documents, and 50 media organisations are “turning the data theft into business”. Further down in the same briefing they are “two whistleblowers”.

V. What is justified in the criticism

Steingart is not wrong about everything. The presumption of innocence is a rule for courts, but it is also a good standard for the press. A CEO is not a convicted man because 200 journalists have read his old emails. The outrage over the Audi CEO who announced a plant in Georgia standing next to Trump can be considered excessive. And the bank’s Russia plan was small in absolute terms.

The charge that the media judged “often without hearing the other side” does not apply in the Deutsche Bank case, however. The bank’s statement is in the reports: the events were historical and had been dealt with, and the bank had invested seven billion euros in its controls from 2019 to 2025. Steingart quotes that statement himself.

VI. The tarantulas

For his warning against those who punish, Steingart turns to Nietzsche, to Zarathustra’s discourse “On the Tarantulas”: “Mistrust all in whom the impulse to punish is powerful!” In Nietzsche the sentence continues: “They are people of bad race and lineage; out of their faces peer the hangman and the bloodhound.” The image of the executioner, which Steingart paints across a whole paragraph, already stands there one sentence later.

Zarathustra also says who the tarantulas are. They are the “preachers of equality” who, out of envy of those above them, call their revenge justice. His answer to them is: “For thus speaks justice to me: men are not equal.”

With this quotation Steingart turns 200 journalists who analyse a bank’s files into the envious people of bad lineage, and the CEO into the higher man to whom ordinary standards must not be applied. Whether he means it that way, we do not know. But he chose the quotation. What we wrote in “The Right Stable Scent” about the self-assurance of those at the top, we could not have put more briefly.

VII. Who sets the measure

The briefing opens with a comparison: a finance minister who runs up record debt need at most fear a reprimand from the Court of Audit, while a businessman must expect the blade at any moment. That is the measure at issue. Those who sit on the ship with the powerful of business quickly find criticism of them excessive. Those who do not sit on the ship with them often find it appropriate.

Steingart writes that the industry is deaf to self-criticism. He means the others. He sets the measure of criticism himself, and he has nothing to disclose in doing so.
