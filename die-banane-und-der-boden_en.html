The Banana and the Land

Addendum to “Expropriation or the Next Raid?”

Gabor Steingart explains the housing shortage with a comparison: bananas and cars are everywhere because the market rules there; flats are missing because the market is shackled. The comparison overlooks what a flat in Berlin mostly consists of. An addendum to “Expropriation or the Next Raid?”.

I. The comparison

The Morning Briefing of The Pioneer of 25 November 2026 contains a simple calculation. Anyone who wants a banana gets one within the hour. Anyone who wants a car has it at the door within days, in any colour and for any budget. Where there is demand, supply grows, and the motto of the market economy is said to be “bananas and cars for all”. Only in housing does the seller choose the customer. The blame lies with the state and its land-use plans, building regulations and rent caps. Whoever shackles the invisible hand creates ever new shortage.

On two points the text is right, and we have written both ourselves. A price cap builds no flat. And expropriation against compensation creates none either; it swaps the owner and leaves debts behind. That was the subject of “Expropriation or the Next Raid?”.

The comparison itself does not hold.

II. What cannot be reordered

If bananas run short, the grocer reorders from the importer. If cars are missing, the manufacturer adds a shift. A flat consists of two things: a building, which can be produced like a car, and a piece of land in a particular place. Nobody can reorder the land in central Berlin.

What that means is shown by the standard land values set by Berlin’s official valuation committee. The median value for residential building land stood at 190 euros per square metre in 2010 and at 750 euros in 2020. It quadrupled in ten years and stands at 610 euros today. In those ten years nobody produced anything on that land. The city grew around it. It is dearest in the centre: for the zone between Torstraße and Oranienburger Straße the committee gave a standard value of 9,000 euros per square metre at the beginning of 2021.

The economists Katharina Knoll, Moritz Schularick and Thomas Steger compiled house prices for fourteen industrial countries since 1870. Their result: about 80 per cent of the price increase since the Second World War goes back to the land and not to construction costs. Anyone who sets out to explain rising rents and stays silent about the land is explaining the smaller fifth.

III. The witness

Steingart calls Adam Smith and the “Wealth of Nations” as witnesses. In that book Smith wrote a whole chapter on the rent of land. It says: “The rent of the land, therefore, considered as the price paid for the use of the land, is naturally a monopoly price.” Elsewhere he writes of the owner of building ground in town that he “acts always as a monopolist, and exacts the greatest rent which can be got for the use of his ground”. It is highest, he adds, in the capital.

Smith drew a conclusion from this that is missing from this Morning Briefing. Ground rent is an income that falls to the owner without any effort of his own, and so no other revenue can bear a special tax as well as this one.

So with Smith the invisible hand has an exception, and it is exactly the one that matters in housing.

IV. The book

For its Academy, The Pioneer has reissued Ludwig Erhard’s “Wohlstand für Alle” (Prosperity for All). Two things belong to this book.

The first is Erhard’s teacher. Franz Oppenheimer held the monopoly on land, the “land enclosure” (Bodensperre), to be the root evil from which all other monopolies follow. Erhard did not adopt this, and in 1964 said himself that he doubted whether it still applied. We described that in “The Teacher Silenced by Necessity”. But anyone who invokes Erhard against every intervention in the housing market should know that his teacher regarded land as the one market that is none.

The second is Erhard’s own time. When “Wohlstand für Alle” appeared in 1957, the Federal Republic was building flats on a grand scale, and it did not do so through the market alone. In 1950 the Bundestag had unanimously passed the First Housing Act. During the 1950s the federal government went on to finance more than three million flats; private investors built 2.7 million. More than half of the new buildings of those years were state-subsidised social housing, and rents in old buildings remained controlled. Only an act of 1960 lifted the control, district by district. Hamburg and Munich followed in 1974 and 1975, West Berlin at the end of the 1980s. The housing shortage of the economic miracle was remedied by the state, and Erhard sat at the cabinet table as minister of economics.

V. What is missing

Steingart cites 1.4 million missing flats and refers to the Pestel Institute. Its finding has a second half: what is missing is almost exclusively affordable and social housing. The stock of social housing stood at about one million in 2025, lower than ever. The offer “for every budget” that the car trade delivers is precisely what house-building does not deliver. On expensive land the expensive flat is the one that pays.

The chart showing Berlin’s building permits almost halved between 2016 and 2025 also leaves something out. In 2025, 13,754 flats were approved in Berlin, 40.7 per cent more than the year before. It was the first increase in eight years.

We do not dispute that building regulations, approval times and land-use plans slow construction down. But the text ends with a request for money. Readers are asked to support the Pioneer Academy, which wants to fight ignorance of economics, starting with a course on “capital and markets”. A chapter on land would be a good beginning. It is in Adam Smith, Book One, Chapter Eleven.

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo (Anthropic) — Nuremberg, 25 November 2026.
