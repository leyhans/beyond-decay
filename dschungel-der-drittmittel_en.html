The Freedom of Science and Academic Conceit in the Jungle of Third-Party Funding

Ninety-three out of a hundred German professors say science in Germany is free. Seventy-one out of a hundred say applying for research money takes too much effort. Both are true, and together they describe a freedom that in theory every professor has and that a good many of them cannot make full use of.

I. The Sentence in the Constitution

“Arts and sciences, research and teaching shall be free.” That is Article 5 of the German Basic Law, and by every available measure the sentence is honoured. Nobody tells a German professor what to think, what to teach or what to research. When the Allensbach Institute surveyed a good thousand university teachers in 2020, 93 per cent said there was much or very much academic freedom in Germany.

The same respondents said what hampers their research. 71 per cent called the application procedures too complicated and too time-consuming. 63 per cent said their institute’s budget provided too little money for research, or none. 38 per cent found the thematic requirements of the funders often too narrow.

The survey was commissioned by the Deutscher Hochschulverband, the professors’ own association, and those who complain tend to exaggerate. But the figures of the statisticians point the same way.

II. The Figures

In 2024 German universities raised 10.7 billion euros in third-party funds. This is money that does not come with the chair but has to be applied for, for a specific project and a limited time. The largest donor was the federal government with 3.4 billion euros, followed by the German Research Foundation (DFG) with 3.2 billion and private industry with 1.7 billion.

Anyone who thinks first of corporations is therefore mistaken. Industry’s share has fallen since 2006 from over 26 to around 15 per cent. The jungle we are talking about was planted mostly by the state.

A university professorship raises 325,400 euros a year on average, 715,600 in engineering and 147,800 in the humanities. At the universities, the share of third-party funds in spending has risen since 1995 from 14 to 25 per cent. The staff paid from them made up about a tenth of all researchers in the 1980s. Today it is almost three in ten. How these people work is described in “Academic Freedom … in the Light”.

In the DFG’s individual grants programme, fewer than one in three applications is approved. More than two out of three are written, reviewed and rejected. University professors say they spend nine per cent of their working time on applications. Over a year, that is more than a month.

III. What Karlsruhe Permits

One might think that a freedom depending on the success of an application is a matter for the Constitutional Court. The court has ruled on it, in October 2004, on constitutional complaints against the higher education act of the state of Brandenburg. Article 5, it held, contains no ban on tying the distribution of funds to the evaluation of scientific quality. The evaluation only has to be appropriate to science.

What the individual researcher can claim in court is a basic endowment: enough to make scientific work possible at all. Everything beyond that may be distributed according to performance, and in many places the measure of performance is the third-party money raised.

The freedom of science thus protects reliably against prohibition. It does not protect against an empty account. Anyone who wants to work on a topic for which there is no programme and no well-disposed reviewer may do so at any time. He just gets no staff for it.

IV. The Application Professional

What this does to research has been described by researchers themselves. A working group of the Berlin-Brandenburg Academy of Sciences stated in 2022 that scientists are turning into application professionals. They pursue the questions that are fundable instead of those they would be best suited for. Some give up topics, others bend them into shape so as not to be left without funds. Small progress is strengthened at the expense of the fundamentally new.

The Volkswagen Foundation has tried this on itself. Since 2017, in one of its programmes, it has drawn part of the funded projects by lot after a jury has sorted out the unusable ones. Its finding: where reviewers have to agree, established topics and recognised methods often win. The lottery increases variety. Three economists have shown the same for the journal articles of an entire year: papers that combine knowledge in unusual ways are rarely cited at first and recognised only late. Whoever judges by the citations of the first years sorts out the new.

The German Science Council, the most important advisory body of the federal and state governments, said in 2023 that the system of research funding had reached its limits. In June 2026 the research ministry announced a funding programme on academic freedom. One of its four focal points reads: the choice and non-choice of research questions. Anyone who wants to know why certain questions are not asked can apply for a grant to find out.

V. The Contracts

The smaller part of the money comes from companies and foundations, and most of the time nothing objectionable happens. Four cases are documented in which it was different.

In Berlin, Deutsche Bank concluded a contract in 2006 with Humboldt University and the Technical University. It gave the bank a say in teaching, teaching assignments for its own employees and a veto over the publication of research results. It became known in 2011 because a retired political scientist published it.

In Mainz, the university received 150 million euros from the Boehringer Ingelheim Foundation. Under the contract, agreements on the appointment of professors required the foundation’s approval. “There is a veto right in there,” the university’s president conceded in 2016. The contract could be read only after a radio reporter had won access in court. The university spoke of mistakes and announced that the contracts would be changed.

In Cologne, an association wanted to see the framework agreement the university had concluded in 2008 with the pharmaceutical group Bayer. The Higher Administrative Court in Münster ruled in 2015 that the contract could remain secret. The state commissioner for freedom of information had recommended disclosure.

In Munich, the Technical University accepted twenty professorships in business administration in 2018 from the foundation of Lidl founder Dieter Schwarz, paid for until retirement. Thirteen of them are located in Heilbronn, the donor’s home town. What they cost is not disclosed.

In none of these cases has it been proven that a research result was falsified. What stands out is something else. The first three contracts were pursued by a retired professor, a reporter and an association. The self-regulation of science, of which it is so proud, did not respond in any of these cases.

VI. The State’s Lever

That the state knows the lever too became apparent in spring 2024. University teachers had criticised, in an open letter, the clearing of a protest camp at a Berlin university. The federal research ministry then examined which of the signatories received funding from the ministry and whether it could be withdrawn. When this became known, the responsible state secretary had to go. But the question had been asked, in a ministry, and in writing.

The ordinary case is less conspicuous. With its Hightech Agenda the federal government set priority topics for research in 2025. That is its right; it is taxpayers’ money. But with every programme, a piece of the decision about what is researched moves from the laboratory to the ministry desk.

VII. The Counter-Calculation

There are objections to all this, and they are strong.

The first: in the past the money was distributed by seniority and local power. The full professor received his funds whether he did research or not, and the young mind with the better idea received nothing. Competition for third-party funds broke up these fiefdoms. Anyone who wants to abolish it must say what is to take its place.

The second: peer review is imperfect, but nobody knows a better procedure. The DFG is open to all topics, and its review boards are elected by the scientists themselves. Much more self-government is hardly conceivable.

The third: those affected feel free. 93 per cent is not a minority that can be ignored.

We consider all three correct, and they do not refute the finding. The issue is not the abolition of competition but its measure. The Science Council and the Academy demand the same: more reliable basic funding, more funding without thematic requirements, less effort, and no longer using third-party funds as the yardstick of a person’s performance. And that someone feels free who has learned to want only what gets approved is exactly what the Academy describes.

VIII. The Conceit

Those who become dependent on those above mark themselves off from those below. That is an interpretation, not a measurement. But science has a ladder with three rungs, and on each of them something can be counted.

At the top is the university, and even the way there is narrower than the phrase “selection of the best” promises. In 2010 the sociologist Christina Möller asked some 1,340 university professors in North Rhine-Westphalia about their parents’ occupations. One in nine came from a working-class family or a comparable household, more than a third from the highest of four groups of origin. Among those appointed between 2001 and 2010 this share rose to 38 per cent, the highest value of the four decades examined. In law, eight out of ten came from the two upper groups. The survey covers one state and is sixteen years old; to this day there are no official statistics on the social origin of academic staff.

A Berlin study surveyed professors with a migration background in Berlin and Hesse. Most come from Western Europe and North America, and more than half call their origin a professional advantage. Of those from developing and emerging countries, almost every second reports disadvantages because of their national origin. These are self-reports from 203 respondents and no proof concerning any individual appointment. But they show that origin does not mean the same for everyone in this business.

The second rung is the university of applied sciences, the Fachhochschule. The DFG approves just under one in three applications overall. For applications from universities of applied sciences, the federal government reported in 2021 a funding rate of 15.3 per cent for 2019 and 19.7 per cent for 2020. Of all DFG grants, according to its latest funding atlas, about 0.7 per cent go to the hundred funded universities of applied sciences. There is a factual reason for this: a professor there teaches twice as much and has hardly any staff to help with the application. But the money is unevenly distributed among the universities themselves as well. After the first round of the Excellence Initiative, the sociologist Richard Münch calculated that 17 of 100 universities received half of the DFG funds and that an institution’s grants rise with the number of its reviewers; he spoke of a latent power cartel. A counter-study, co-authored by a DFG employee, found no cartel in the individual grants programme. The dispute is not settled. What became of the Excellence Initiative is described in “Mediocre Excellence in a Desolate Setting”.

Another dispute is settled. For decades only universities were allowed to award the doctorate. When the states began to grant this right to research-strong universities of applied sciences as well, the Hochschulverband in 2014 called it a “dangerous wrong track” that could destroy the architecture of the entire higher education system. The association of the large technical universities declared that it would harm Germany as a location for science. By now almost all states have granted the right; only Saxony has not. The number of doctoral candidates at universities of applied sciences has more than tripled in two years, and the system is still standing. What was defended was a privilege, not quality.

The third rung has no title. On it stand the engineers, technicians and inventors whose knowledge is set down in patent specifications and not in journals. Patents cite papers regularly. The reverse almost never happens. Two information scientists at the University of Illinois searched 2.4 million open-access papers in medicine and biology. In 7,223 of them there was at least one reference to a patent. That is three in a thousand. In chemistry it is more, and some of it has harmless reasons: patents are hard to read and often older than the literature a paper works with. But whoever cites only what appears in journals writes a history of technology from which the people who built it are missing. We have documented one such case in our own cause: “The Missing Footnote”.

This rung is emptying. Of the patents filed from Germany in 2024, 4.5 per cent came from independent inventors. Five years earlier it was 6.4 per cent, and at the end of the 1990s, according to the patent office, still more than ten. The German Economic Institute counts more generously because it also includes applications in which applicant and inventor are not the same person. It arrives at just under a quarter for the mid-1990s and at 6.5 per cent for 2018. The statistics do not say why.

Whether all this is conceit cannot be measured. What can be measured is that each rung looks smaller from the one above it, and that a business which considers itself open does not record its own composition.

IX. Free and Kept Busy

The freedom of science has not been abolished in Germany. Nor has it been sold, at least not on a large scale. It has been kept busy: with applications, reviews, interim reports and the question of what can be approved next.

Whoever works like this has a title, a chair and an article of the constitution. That does not make him independent. Perhaps this is why the title and the access to third-party funds are guarded so carefully.
