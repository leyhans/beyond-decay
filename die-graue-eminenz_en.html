The Grey Eminence

Stephen Miller, the enforcer who has run the White House since 9 November

From the series The Conductors of the Leaders · Part II, The Living

The series asks which ideologies and mentors stand behind those in power and what they actually achieve. It proceeds case by case; each text stands on its own. The typology comes at the end, not at the beginning.

Stephen Miller has written no book and devised no doctrine. What he thinks, others thought before him: a French novelist, a few websites on the far right, the immigration laws of the 1920s. His talent lies elsewhere. He knows which order, which quota and which directive will turn an idea into the daily routine of an agency. Since 9 November 2026 he has been White House chief of staff. The grey eminence now stands in the light.

I. The monk behind the cardinal

The expression comes from the France of Louis XIII. Cardinal Richelieu wore crimson and was called the red eminence. Beside him stood a Capuchin friar, François Leclerc du Tremblay, known as Père Joseph, in a grey habit. He held no ministry, but he led the negotiations, wrote the memoranda and had the cardinal’s ear. The grey eminence was powerful because he did not stand in the light. Père Joseph never became a cardinal.

For almost ten years Stephen Miller was something like Donald Trump’s grey eminence. In the first term he was “Senior Advisor for Policy”, in the second deputy chief of staff and homeland security advisor. On Friday, 6 November 2026, three days after the Republicans’ defeat in the midterm elections, Trump wrote on Truth Social that Miller, “one of my most trusted advisors”, would become chief of staff starting Monday, 9 November. Susie Wiles, who had held the post since January 2025, would move to Trump’s political operation. According to a White House official, she had submitted her resignation on Thursday night.

At Mar-a-Lago Miller gave thanks with a pledge. One had to stand with the man “who has stood with us with every fiber of his being”. Dissent came from his own party. Representative Marlin Stutzman of Indiana told CNN the promotion was “a mistake”; voters in the Midwest did not like Miller’s tone. Senator Thom Tillis of North Carolina called him “the worst of all possible choices” and later “an absurd mistake”. The wave that swept the Republicans away, he said, was driven above all by the hard-line deportation campaign, and Miller had steered it.

In “Negative Feedback Returns” we described this change as the White House’s answer to the election: the voters steer against, and the White House installs an amplifier. Here we ask who this amplifier is and where his ideas come from.

II. Santa Monica

Miller was born on 23 August 1985 and grew up in Santa Monica, one of the most liberal cities in California. The family is Jewish; his father is a lawyer in the family’s real-estate business. As a schoolboy he called in to the radio show of the conservative host Larry Elder. There he was heard by the writer David Horowitz, a former leftist who had turned into a crusader against the left at the universities. Horowitz invited the schoolboy to his home, Miller invited Horowitz to his school, and the teenager’s first pieces appeared on Horowitz’s website, “FrontPage Magazine”. The school vetoed the appearance, as did Duke University later, where Miller studied political science.

A detail from the family history was made public by Miller’s uncle. David Glosser, a retired neuropsychologist and the brother of Miller’s mother, wrote in “Politico” in August 2018 that his great-grandfather Wolf-Leib Glosser had fled the pogroms in the shtetl of Antopol in today’s Belarus and arrived at Ellis Island on 7 January 1903 with eight dollars in his pocket. He was fluent in Polish, Russian and Yiddish but understood no English. By peddling and sweatshop toil he earned enough to bring the family over, and over the generations a horse and wagon became a chain of stores. Of the two thousand Jews who stayed in Antopol, according to Glosser, only seven survived the Nazis. Had the policies his nephew espouses been in force back then, Glosser wrote, the family would have been wiped out. The piece was headlined “Stephen Miller Is an Immigration Hypocrite. I Know Because I’m His Uncle.”

III. The intermediaries

At Duke, Miller led the local chapter of Horowitz’s “Students for Academic Freedom” and wrote columns for the student paper. The far-right activist Richard Spencer, then a graduate student at Duke, claims he was Miller’s “mentor” and that the two organised a debate on immigration together in 2007. Miller denies it. All that is documented is that their paths crossed in the same small group.

What is documented is who carried Miller upward. After graduation Horowitz placed him as press secretary with Representative Michele Bachmann, a Tea Party leader, and then in early 2009 with Representative John Shadegg of Arizona. In June 2009 Miller moved, again on Horowitz’s recommendation, to Senator Jeff Sessions of Alabama, first as press secretary, then as communications director. Through Sessions he came into contact with the organisations that have lobbied in Washington for decades for less immigration: the Center for Immigration Studies, the Federation for American Immigration Reform and NumbersUSA. In 2013 Sessions and Miller defeated the bipartisan immigration reform of the “Gang of Eight”, which already had a majority in the Senate. Miller wrote the speeches with which Sessions fought it. In this period he met Steve Bannon, the head of Breitbart News.

Add up the stations and a pattern appears. Miller is not formed by a teacher but handed on by intermediaries. Horowitz opens doors, Sessions gives him an office and a subject, the lobby groups supply the numbers, Bannon the stage.

IV. The emails

What Miller himself read became visible in 2019. Katie McHugh, a former Breitbart editor who had turned away from the far right, gave the Southern Poverty Law Center more than 900 emails Miller had sent her between March 2015 and June 2016, while he still worked for Sessions. Michael Edison Hayden published them in a series in November 2019.

On 6 September 2015 Miller recommended “The Camp of the Saints”, the 1973 novel by the Frenchman Jean Raspail. A fleet of destitute migrants from India lands on the Côte d’Azur, and the West surrenders because it is too soft to defend itself. The book has been a cult text of white nationalists for decades. Two and a half weeks later Breitbart ran an article reading the Pope’s message on the refugee crisis in the mirror of that novel. In October 2015 Miller sent a link to VDARE, a website the SPLC lists as a hate group. He praised the immigration quotas of the Coolidge era, the 1924 law that drastically restricted immigrants from southern and eastern Europe and whose quotas in the 1930s barred many Jews from Germany from reaching the United States. McHugh says he also pointed her by phone to crime statistics from American Renaissance, a publication the SPLC classifies as white nationalist.

The White House did not deny the authenticity of the emails and did not address their content. It attacked the messenger: press secretary Stephanie Grisham called the SPLC “an utterly-discredited, long-debunked far-left smear organization”, and spokespeople insinuated that the attack targeted Miller because he is Jewish. McHugh summed up the result in one sentence: what Miller sent her in those emails had become policy.

V. From text to order

In the 2016 campaign Miller warmed up the crowds before Trump’s rallies and wrote his convention speech. The inaugural address of 20 January 2017 he wrote together with Bannon, as a White House official conceded the next day. Its best-known line: “This American carnage stops right here and stops right now.”

A week later came the entry ban for citizens of seven predominantly Muslim countries. Miller and Bannon wrote it, and they preferred executive orders to legislation because orders need no Congress. When the courts halted the ban, Miller made the rounds of the Sunday shows on 12 February 2017. On CBS he said the whole world would soon see “that the powers of the president to protect our country are very substantial and will not be questioned”. On Fox he added that the president’s powers here were “beyond question” and that there was no judicial supremacy in this country. Trump congratulated him that same morning.

In 2018 came “zero tolerance”. Anyone crossing the border without papers was prosecuted, including parents with small children, and since children may not go to jail with them, they were taken away. By the time Trump ended the practice by executive order on 20 June 2018, according to government figures more than 2,800 children had been separated from their parents. The later family reunification task force, counting the 2017 pilot phase, found almost 4,000; in February 2023 nearly a thousand of them were still not back with their families. According to two participants in a White House meeting, reported by NBC News in 2020, Miller was furious that a month after the start the separations were still not happening. He proposed separating every family at the border, including those who presented themselves legally at a port of entry and asked for asylum. According to Customs and Border Protection figures, that would have affected 25,000 more children. Anyone who opposed it, Miller said in the meeting, was a lawbreaker and un-American.

VI. The second term

Between the terms, in April 2021, Miller founded America First Legal, an organisation he called “the long-awaited answer to the ACLU”, the great civil-rights organisation of the left. Miller is not a lawyer. The organisation sued the Biden administration in dozens of cases, went after companies over their diversity programmes and received some 27 million dollars from the Bradley Impact Fund in 2021 and 2022 alone. It sat on the advisory board of Project 2025, the thousand-page government programme we described in “The New Right”. So the machine stood ready when Trump returned.

From January 2025 Miller was deputy chief of staff for policy and homeland security advisor. In May 2025 he told Fox the goal was “a minimum of 3,000 arrests” a day by the immigration agency ICE. An arrest quota is a tool every agency understands: whoever misses it must justify himself, not whoever meets it. In the same month he said the administration was “actively looking at” suspending habeas corpus. In January 2026, after federal agents shot and killed Renee Good and Alex Pretti in Minneapolis, he called them “terrorists”; he withdrew the remark about Pretti. That same month he said on CNN about Greenland that the world is “governed by strength, … governed by force, … governed by power”. He defended the US abduction of Venezuela’s president Maduro. He wanted to abolish birthright citizenship, under which every child born in the United States is a citizen. In that he failed.

VII. Office without oeuvre

Our series asks who stands behind the powerful. With Wang Huning, “the embedded mentor”, there was a body of work at the start: books, a theory he then carried into the apparatus himself. Fritz Kraemer, “the invisible mentor”, had no books and no office, but pupils he shaped. Miller is the reverse of both. He has an office but no oeuvre, and he is more pupil than teacher. What he stands for comes from Raspail’s novel, from the lobby groups Sessions introduced him to, from the websites he forwarded, from the law of 1924. He is someone led who now leads.

His own achievement is one of craft. He knows where the levers of administration sit: the order instead of the law, the daily quota instead of the debate, the directive no one in parliament ever sees. That sounds like little, yet it is the part where most ideologues fail. A thought that is not translated into forms, responsibilities and performance targets remains a thought. Our conclusion: the typology of this series has so far lacked the enforcer, the man who needs no ideas because he knows how to turn other people’s ideas into administrative acts.

Which brings us to the title. Père Joseph was powerful as long as he stayed invisible. He bore no responsibility that could be held against him. Miller has now given up that protection. A chief of staff stands in the front row, and every decision of the coming two years will be linked to his name. The voters took their measurement on 3 November, Republicans in his own party name him as the cause, and Trump himself conceded on “60 Minutes” that the deportation drive “might be too tough”. Whether the president promoted the enforcer to hold the course, or to have a culprit standing in the light, cannot be said today. Either way, the grey eminence leaves the shadow at the very moment the wind turns.
