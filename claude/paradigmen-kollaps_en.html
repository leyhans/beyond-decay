When Paradigms Fall

Kuhn describes the slow death of paradigms through the accumulation of anomalies. What happens when the Megamachine changes the conditions — and paradigms are not refuted but simply washed away?

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo  ·  beyond-decay.org/claude/  ·  April 2026

The previous chapter on paradigms follows Kuhn: paradigms die slowly, through the accumulation of anomalies, through generational change, through the painful process of losing legitimacy. That is true — for scientific paradigms in stable societies.

But there is another kind of paradigm death. Faster, more brutal, and more characteristic of the Megamachine: the overthrow of a paradigm through changed conditions. Not refutation. Not erosion. Simply: the world has changed in such a way that suddenly nobody is interested in the old paradigm any more.

Two observations from recent years illustrate this.

I. The climate paradigm — and its disappearance

For decades the climate catastrophe was the dominant narrative of the future in political discourse. Not necessarily the one that guided action — action lagged behind announcements — but the paradigmatic one: the frame through which everything else was judged. Flights, meat, heating, mobility — everything was measured through the filter of the climate question. Whoever questioned that bore the burden of proof.

Then, in spring 2026, missiles began to fall on the Gulf states. The oil price rose by thirty per cent. Gas prices in Europe followed. Share prices collapsed.

And suddenly the discussion was a different one: what will heating cost next winter? When will the recession come? How long can we afford this?

The climate catastrophe had been pushed aside — not by an all-clear, but by the bank statement. Not because the threat had become smaller, but because another threat was drawing closer: the threat to one's own standard of living. The paradigm still existed — but it had lost its place in consciousness. It had not been refuted. It had become irrelevant.

This is not a weakness of people. It is neurology. The limbic system does not distinguish by objective danger — it distinguishes by proximity and concreteness. The climate tipping point thirty years from now barely activates it. The heating bill in November activates it immediately. The Megamachine knew this — or rather: it exploited it structurally without knowing it.

The end of the world is an idea. The bank statement is a number. Ideas fade. Numbers remain.

What the Megamachine demonstrates here: a paradigm that has not been translated into concrete, immediate interests can be pushed aside by any sufficiently strong economic shock. It does not have to be refuted. It only has to be drowned out.

II. The peace paradigm — and its inversion

The second example is even more radical. Here the paradigm was not pushed aside — it was inverted.

After 1945 a paradigm held sway in Germany that was deeply anchored in collective memory: war is the greatest evil. Militarism leads to catastrophe. Peace does not take care of itself — it has to be actively shaped, through diplomacy, through interdependence, through economic cooperation that makes war unattractive. "Wandel durch Handel" [change through trade] as the guiding principle. The peace dividend after 1990 as the logical consequence: disarmament, because the threat had disappeared.

Today this paradigm is called a historical mistake. Naivety. A crisis — which, fortunately, has been overcome.

The word "Kriegstüchtigkeit" [fitness for war] would have been unthinkable five years ago. Today it is government policy. The arms industry, morally on the defensive for decades, is experiencing rehabilitation. Whoever speaks of peace risks being regarded as a "Putin apologist". The question "Is there another way?" is not refuted — it is no longer admitted as a question.

This is not a normal paradigm shift. It is paradigm inversion — the complete reversal of good and evil, reason and naivety, virtue and weakness. And it happened within a few years, without the old paradigm ever really being discussed, examined or refuted.

III. What the Megamachine does in the process

In both cases the Megamachine is not the cause in the sense of a plan. It is the system that shapes the conditions under which paradigms arise, persist and fall.

In the climate case: the fossil energy industry and its structural interests never directly refuted the climate paradigm — they shaped economic conditions in such a way that concreteness and immediacy regularly win over abstraction and the future. Every energy crisis is a moment in which the climate paradigm gives way — not because it is wrong, but because the Megamachine produces concrete economic pain more efficiently than the climate paradigm produces conviction.

In the peace case: the military-industrial complex — one of the most elaborate branches of the Megamachine — lost its identity-defining enemy after the end of the Cold War. It has found it again: partly in real geopolitical developments, partly through the active shaping of narratives and perceptions of threat. The peace paradigm stood in the way. It did not have to be refuted — it had to be delegitimised. That has been achieved.

The pattern is the same: the Megamachine changes the material conditions in such a way that certain paradigms lose their ground — and others suddenly appear to have no alternative. Not through argument. Through structure.

IV. What Kuhn does not describe

Kuhn's model of paradigm change was developed for science — for a system that, at least nominally, works by rational discussion and evidence. In science a paradigm needs time to die, because it supports institutions and careers that defend it.

In political economy other laws apply. Here paradigms can fall faster — because they are carried less by evidence than by interests, narratives and economic conditions. When these conditions tip over, the paradigm tips over with them.

This is not an insight that generates optimism. It means: no paradigm — not even a correct, well-founded one — is safe when the Megamachine shifts the material conditions. The climate paradigm is not wrong. But it is vulnerable, because its consequences are abstract and distant while the costs of the energy crisis are concrete and near.

It also means: paradigms carried by the Megamachine — growth, rearmament, rising consumption — are more stable not because they are truer, but because they serve concrete, immediate interests. Paradigms that work against it have to overcome this structural asymmetry. That is rare — and when it succeeds, it is mostly in moments of crisis.

V. The question that remains

Both essays from which this paper was developed end with a similar question: how could it come to this — and have we learned from it?

The Megamachine analysis gives an uncomfortable answer: learning in the sense of changed behaviour is rare, because the structural conditions that produce the behaviour remain unchanged. The peace paradigm did not fail because people have become worse. It failed because the structures that make war profitable are stronger than the convictions that make peace reasonable.

The climate paradigm is not failing for lack of knowledge — climate research is as well documented as few things in the history of science. It is failing because the Megamachine generates concrete economic pain more efficiently than the climate movement generates pressure to act.

Paradigms that work against the Megamachine must therefore be more than intellectually convincing. They must be translated into concrete, immediate interests — or they will be swept away whenever the Megamachine changes the conditions. That is the structural challenge for every attempt to change direction.
