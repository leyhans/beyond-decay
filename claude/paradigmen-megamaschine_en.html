Paradigms — the Invisible Walls

How systems of thought become structures of the Megamachine — and why paradigm shifts are so rare and so painful.

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo  ·  beyond-decay.org/claude/  ·  April 2026

The Megamachine needs no censorship. It needs no prohibitions, no secret police, no indexes of banned works. It needs something more effective: paradigms. Systems of thought built so deeply into a society's institutions, career paths and evaluation structures that what lies outside their boundaries need not be thought — because it cannot be thought without catapulting the thinker out of the system.

This is not theory. This is the everyday operation of science, business and politics.

I. What Kuhn discovered

In 1962 Thomas Kuhn's work "The Structure of Scientific Revolutions" appeared — and changed how we think about science. Kuhn's central thesis: science does not develop through the steady accumulation of knowledge, but through occasional, revolutionary breaks. Between these breaks — in the phase of "normal science" — scientists work within a paradigm: a framework of shared assumptions, methods, concepts and criteria of evaluation.

The paradigm is not one theory among many. It is the foundation on which theories are built. It defines which questions are legitimate, which methods are acceptable, which answers count as progress. What lies outside the paradigm is not wrong — it is invisible. It does not exist as a scientific statement, because it has no language in which the paradigm could take it in.

Anomalies — findings that the paradigm cannot explain — are at first ignored. Then treated as exceptions. Then as errors of the observer. Only when the anomalies become so massive that they can no longer be explained away does the crisis arise — and at some point, rarely and painfully, the paradigm shift.

Paradigms are not refuted. They are abandoned — when one generation dies and a new one grows up that no longer defends the old because it never learned to love it.

Max Planck put this in his autobiography, bitterly and precisely: a new scientific truth does not prevail by convincing its opponents. It prevails because its opponents eventually die.

II. The paradigm as a Megamachine structure

What Kuhn described for the natural sciences applies with even greater sharpness to the social sciences, economics and political theory. For here the paradigm is not only a system of thought — it is at the same time a power structure.

An economic paradigm decides who gets professorships. It decides which research is funded, which expert opinions are considered competent, which experts are appointed to committees, which voices are heard in policy advice. Whoever masters the paradigm controls access to resources, reputation and influence. Whoever questions it loses that access — not by decision, but through the logic of the structure.

That is the connection to the Megamachine. The paradigm is not conviction — it is a condition of selection. The Megamachine selects for loyalty to the paradigm and against criticism of it. Not because anyone decided it should. But because career structures, funding institutions, peer-review systems and reputation markets together produce a result that nobody planned: the systematic disadvantaging of thinkers who question the foundation.

The critic of the paradigm is not refuted. He is marginalised — through irrelevance, through withdrawal of funding, through exclusion from the institutions in which reputation is created. The result is structural silence: not the cowardice of individuals, but the predictable outcome of conditions of selection.

III. Why experts cannot do what they do not want to do

Here lies a misunderstanding that is important to clear up. The defence of a paradigm by its experts is as a rule not a conscious decision in favour of falsehood. It is the natural reaction of a person who has built his intellectual life within a system of thought.

The economist who has researched, taught and published for thirty years within the framework of neoclassical welfare theory cannot simply decide to declare the foundation of that theory wrong — without at the same time delegitimising thirty years of his own work. His expertise, his reputation, his contribution to science are anchored in this paradigm. An attack on the paradigm is an attack on him.

This is not a weakness. It is the human consequence of specialisation. Whoever has penetrated deeply enough into a system of thought to be productive in it has a blind spot for its limits. Depth of competence and blindness to what lies outside are two sides of the same coin.

The Megamachine makes use of this — not intentionally, but effectively. It creates institutions that reward deep specialisation and punish broad criticism of paradigms. The result is a system that cannot question its own basic assumptions — not because it is not allowed to, but because those who could have no incentive to do so, and those who want to have no access.

IV. The ruling paradigms of the Megamachine

Which paradigms, concretely, structure thinking about the economy and society today — and thereby belong to the Megamachine?

The Pareto paradigm. The idea that welfare is defined by the Pareto criterion — that a state of affairs is better if someone is made better off without anyone being made worse off — is not one theory among many. It is the foundation of neoclassical welfare economics. Whoever questions it is not questioning one theory. He is questioning the entire edifice on which generations of economic research rest. That explains the sharpness of the defensive reactions: this is not about matters of fact. It is about foundations.

The growth paradigm. The assumption that growth in gross domestic product is the measure of social progress structures not only economics — it structures the entire political debate. Alternatives are not refuted. They are treated as unscientific, as utopian, as impossible to operationalise. Yet GDP itself is a construction — a metric that fails to capture many forms of value creation and counts some forms of value destruction as a contribution.

The market paradigm. The conviction that markets are the most efficient form of resource allocation — under the right conditions — is, in its restricted form, a mathematically proven proposition. In its extended form — as an argument for the superiority of the market in all areas of social life — it is a paradigm applied far beyond its conditions of validity. Confusing a mathematical theorem with a universal principle is itself a paradigm operation.

None of these paradigms is a lie. They all contain real insights. But they are treated as foundations that must not be questioned — and that makes them structures of the Megamachine.

V. The anomaly and the crisis

Kuhn shows: paradigms are not overcome by arguments. They are destabilised by anomalies — by findings and developments that the paradigm can no longer explain and that become so obvious that they can no longer be ignored.

What are the anomalies of the ruling economic paradigms today?

The financial crisis of 2008 was the biggest so far. The market and efficiency paradigm could not explain how rational actors in regulated markets could produce a systemic failure of this magnitude. The paradigm's answer: that was an exception. Regulation was inadequate. Next time it will be different. The paradigm itself was not called into question — it was rescued by defining exceptions.

Growing inequality is another anomaly. The welfare paradigm promises that growth benefits everyone — through trickle-down, through rising productivity, through market mechanisms. The empirical development of recent decades shows the opposite: in most developed countries, growth has primarily benefited the upper income groups. The paradigm explains this as a transitional phenomenon, as the result of market distortions, as a solvable exception. The structural explanation — that the paradigm itself produces the inequality — remains outside the system of thought.

The ecological crisis is the biggest anomaly. An economic system that does not include resource depletion and climate damage in its basis of valuation systemically produces results that are destructive in the long run. That is no longer a forecast — it is observation. The paradigm responds with corrections at the margins: CO₂ taxes, green growth, efficiency gains. The possibility that the growth paradigm itself is the problem remains outside.

VI. What a paradigm shift would require

Kuhn is sober: a paradigm shift is not an intellectual event. It is a social and institutional process — and it is painful, slow and often tied to a change of generations.

What would a paradigm shift in economics require? New institutions that declare other questions legitimate. New funding systems that reward other methods. New paths of education that teach other basic assumptions. New media and communication channels that give a hearing to other voices. And: a crisis so great that the anomalies can no longer be explained away.

The crisis is emerging. The anomalies are piling up. But the institutional infrastructure of the old paradigm is still intact — and it continues to select for its own reproduction.

This is the moment when alternative thinking becomes important — not because it would have an immediate effect, but because it provides the material for the moment when the old paradigm collapses. The blueprint has to be lying ready in the drawer. The department-store model of this book is not a policy proposal for today. It is a framework of thought for the moment when the ruling paradigm can no longer manage its anomalies.

Paradigms do not die by refutation. They die when reality has discredited them so thoroughly that even their defenders fall silent. The silence will come — sooner or later. The only question is whether alternative thinking will be ready then.

VII. The Megamachine and the silence of the experts

There is a specific silence that can be observed in this context — and that shows by example how the Megamachine operates through paradigms.

When a ruling paradigm is fundamentally called into question — not in one of its partial aspects, but in its basic assumptions — the most common reaction of its experts is not refutation. It is silence, or dismissal with the remark that the question lies outside legitimate discourse.

This is not a personal weakness of the experts. It is structurally enforced. A paradigm expert who publicly discusses the foundation of his paradigm makes himself vulnerable — not only intellectually but institutionally. He risks his reputation as an expert, his demand as an adviser, his status in the system he knows and belongs to. The costs of criticising the paradigm are concrete and immediate. The benefit — a more productive public debate — is abstract and distant.

The Megamachine needs no censorship. It needs only this cost structure. The result is a public discourse that takes place within the paradigms that stabilise the Megamachine — and that systematically excludes the questions that would destabilise it. Not by prohibition. By silence.
