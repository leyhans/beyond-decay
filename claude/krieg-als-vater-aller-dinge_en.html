War as the Father of All Things?

On a truncation of Heraclitus, the programme behind the innovation, and the other creativity that war excludes

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo · May 2026
Megamachine · War · Innovation · History · Rearmament

The thesis is in demand again. Whoever wants to legitimise the rearmament that Europe has been undertaking since 2022, at a pace that would be unthinkable in peacetime, likes to reach for it — for it turns damage into gain and violence into progress. War is the father of all things, the Greek philosopher Heraclitus supposedly said two and a half thousand years ago. It sounds good because it is old. It sounds inescapable because it is general. And it is a truncation that ranks among the most successful lies in the history of Western thought.

This essay shows three things. First, that Heraclitus meant something else. Second, that the innovation we observe today and credit to rearmament is not a natural event but a programme planned for four decades, with an architect, an address and a file number. Third, that the same dynamics of innovation are available without war too — and proved it before our eyes between 2020 and 2021.

I. What Heraclitus meant

Heraclitus of Ephesus left behind a sentence that became a stock phrase of Western education: pólemos patér pánton — war is the father of all things. In pre-Socratic Greek, however, the word pólemos did not primarily mean military violence. It denoted the tension of opposites from which all becoming arises. Day and night, hot and cold, up and down — the one brings forth the other by contradicting it. Heraclitus was a natural philosopher. He described what Hegel, two and a half millennia later, would call dialectics.

The late moderns turn this philosophical concept into a military-technical one. The tension of opposites becomes gunpowder. Agonal contest becomes battle. Pólemos becomes war, the father becomes the authority that legitimises war. The truncation is so successful that it has emancipated itself from Heraclitus — whoever quotes the sentence today mostly no longer knows that the original said something else. It has ceased to be a quotation. It has become a slogan.

Whoever makes Heraclitus the star witness for warlike innovation is not saying what Heraclitus meant. He is saying what he himself wants to say — and borrowing the authority of antiquity to do so.

II. What war actually does

That there is something to the truncation cannot be denied. In wars, technical innovations arise that would probably have arisen differently without war. The American sociologist Charles Tilly captured the pattern in a few words: War made the state, and the state made war. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, gunpowder and siege artillery forced the construction of huge star forts that cost a fortune. Whoever wanted such a fortress needed a tax system. Whoever wanted taxes needed an administration, officials, censuses, statistics. The modern state did not arise from an idea of civic life — it arose from the needs of war. Prussia is the caricature of this genesis.

The twentieth century perfected the pattern. In 1909 Fritz Haber developed a process for ammonia synthesis because in a crisis the German army would no longer get saltpetre from Chile. Carl Bosch industrialised it in 1913, financed by the Reich, driven by the prospect of war. Without Haber–Bosch, Germany would probably have lost the First World War sooner. With Haber–Bosch, the same man who supplied poison gas to the front was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1918. Today the same process feeds global fertiliser production and has probably saved more people from starvation than died in both world wars. Dual use in the most literal sense.

Peenemünde on the Baltic, the early 1940s. In 1942 the Aggregat 4 becomes the first man-made object to reach an altitude of ninety kilometres. Space has been reached. Two years later the same model strikes London and Antwerp as Vergeltungswaffe 2 (“Retaliation Weapon 2”). More people die in the production of the V2 than in its use — over twenty thousand forced labourers in the Mittelbau-Dora concentration camp. Wernher von Braun, the technical director, is flown in by the Americans after 1945. He leads the Apollo programme. He brings people to the Moon. The Moon landing is a child of the V2.

The social psychologist Carsten De Dreu, who has researched how people act under threat, has a term for this: motivated focus. Threat does not block — it concentrates. What falls away is everything that does not contribute to solving the acute problem. What remains is the only path still visible. If the threat is connected with the task to be solved, this can lead to astonishing creativity. If it is not, it blocks. That is the psychological basis of what happens in war: everything that does not serve the front falls away. Whatever serves the front gets every resource there is. The result is concentrated innovation in a narrow corridor — and stalled innovation everywhere else.

War is not magic. War is the concentration of scarce means on narrowly defined goals with normal inhibitions suspended. What it produces depends on who defines the goals.

III. Andrew Marshall and the programme

Here the essay leaves the ground of general observation. What today’s debate about war and innovation systematically overlooks is the fact that the innovation we are currently observing is not a natural event. It is a programme. It has a name. It has an architect. It has an institutional sponsor.

The architect’s name is Andrew Marshall. From 1973 to 2015 he headed the Office of Net Assessment in the US Department of Defense — for over four decades, under twelve secretaries of defense and eight presidents. Marshall was neither a politician nor a general. He was a strategist who supplied the Pentagon with long-term perspectives and the mathematical models for them. He did what nobody else does: think in generations.

Marshall’s great insight of the 1970s and 1980s rested on an empirical observation. He reconstructed the careers of outstanding military innovations from the late nineteenth to the early twentieth century and found that in each case it took roughly thirty years for an epoch-making invention to be put to operational use in the armed forces of the advanced countries. From the submarine to the aircraft carrier, from the tank to radar — everywhere the same interval. Three decades between first availability and full military effect.

Marshall concluded from this: if the next epoch-making change begins in the mid-1980s — the information and data revolution, satellite-based positioning, precision-guided munitions, networked combat groups — then postmodern armed forces will be operational around 2020 to 2025. He called this change the Revolution in Military Affairs, RMA for short. It is the plan behind what we observe today: drones, autonomous weapons systems, AI-assisted targeting, small networked combat groups instead of mass armies. Marshall predicted it in 1985. In 2025 it is here.

IV. Lothar Rühl, July 2000

How far this programme had already taken concrete shape in the late 1990s, and how naturally it was taken up by the German security-policy establishment, is documented by an article by Lothar Rühl in the CDU-affiliated journal Die politische Meinung (“Political Opinion”) of July 2000. Rühl, former state secretary in the Federal Ministry of Defence and long-standing commentator on security policy, describes the RMA with the evident enthusiasm of someone who belongs to the advertising department. Postmodern armed forces, he writes, would turn superiority in weapons and information technology into strategic dominance — and do so without nuclear weapons. The United States was therefore the singular world power. The European allies would have to remedy their deficits in order to remain compatible.

What is interesting about Rühl’s text twenty-five years later is not the enthusiasm. It is the precision of the prediction. In July 2000 Rühl describes almost every technical component that is waging the war in Iran today: small networked combat groups with electronic equipment, satellite-based reconnaissance, precision stand-off weapons, regional missile defence, combined operations in the air, at sea and in the electronic domain. He even names the time horizon of 2020 to 2025, and he names the number of years such military modernisations need — thirty, exactly in line with Marshall’s empirical findings. What he does not mention is AI; at the time it was not yet on the operational horizon. The rest is correct almost word for word.

Whoever lays Rühl’s text beside today’s descriptions of what the American Operation Epic Fury is doing in Iran in March 2026 sees the realisation of a programme. Not a reaction to a threat. Not an emergency solution in a crisis. But the operational implementation of what was conceived in Pentagon memoranda in 1985, celebrated in German in-house security-policy journals in 2000, and systematically implemented since 2015.

What appears as innovation born of necessity is often innovation born of a plan. The necessity is produced at the same time — it is part of the programme, not its occasion.

V. The other innovation

The thesis of war as the father of all things would be questionable even if it only had to account for what has been described so far. It becomes untenable as soon as one confronts it with the counter-example the world provided in 2020. Within a year, research teams in several countries developed highly effective vaccines against a novel virus. mRNA technology, which had previously been researched for decades without a predetermined outcome, came into clinical use under the pressure of a pandemic. What usually takes ten to twelve years happened in less than one.

It was the same mechanism as in war. Means were concentrated, procedures simplified, hierarchies shortened, knowledge shared in real time. Preprint servers made results freely available before they were peer-reviewed. Governments approved clinical trials in weeks instead of years. Pharmaceutical companies competed and cooperated at the same time. None of this needs a war. It needs a situation that is recognised as sufficiently serious, and the willingness to reduce normal friction.

The coronavirus example shows what the war-and-innovation thesis conceals: that it is pressure and concentration that innovate — not violence. Whoever wants the pressure without the violence can generate the pressure elsewhere. The climate crisis would be a candidate. Security of supply would be one. Care would be one. It is a political decision what becomes pressure and what does not. At present the decision is systematically made to recognise military pressure as pressure, and everything else as business as usual. That is not a law of nature. That is a choice.

Beyond this there is a whole class of innovations that do not appear in the war-and-innovation thesis at all, because they are not technological. Social innovations. Women’s suffrage. The civil rights movement. Parliamentary democracy. Freedom of the press. Free trade unions. These achievements were not won in battles but in conflicts that took dispute as their method and ruled out killing. Democracies, says the political scientist Nicole Deitelhoff, are conflict machines — they have institutionalised dispute and made it productive. That is Heraclitus’ pólemos in the original sense: a tension of opposites that brings something forth without anyone dying.

And finally there is the other side of the balance sheet, which is never discussed in this debate: what war destroys. The Mongol campaigns of the thirteenth century destroyed irrigation systems across Eurasia that had grown over generations. In 1591 Moroccan troops plundered Timbuktu — the unique manuscript collection of the West African metropolis of learning was scattered to the four winds. European colonisation wiped out entire knowledge systems in the Americas, Africa and Oceania — medicine, astronomy, agricultural technology, social orders whose value can no longer be put into figures today, because those who knew them are dead. In 1258 the Mongols burned the House of Wisdom in Baghdad, in which Greek, Indian and Persian science flowed together. What was lost there, no one knows. Whoever praises war as an engine of innovation sees what became visible and overlooks what remained invisible — because it could never happen.

VI. The roast pig

In 1822 the English essayist Charles Lamb told a story that he passed off as a Chinese fable. It goes like this: a peasant had a wooden house in which he kept a pig. The house burned down. In the ruins the peasant found the dead pig, perfectly roasted and incredibly tasty. He described the discovery to his neighbours. Soon all the peasants of the region were setting fire to their houses to get at this delicious roast pork. Only after a long time did someone hit on the idea that one could also roast the pig without burning down the house.

The war-and-innovation thesis is the position of those who keep setting the house on fire because they cannot produce the roast pork without arson. They overlook that there is a stove. They overlook that the house is valuable in itself. They overlook the inhabitants.

Sober analysis shows: war concentrates means — that is true. War suspends inhibitions — that is true as well. War brings forth a particular kind of innovation: the kind that serves the logic of the Megamachine, because the Megamachine distributes the means and lifts the inhibitions. But war is not the only method of generating innovation, nor the best. It is the most expensive, the most destructive, and the one with the narrowest range of results. Whoever wants innovation can have it more cheaply and better.

The question that remains after this essay is a political one. Why does a society that has read Charles Tilly, that knows Carsten De Dreu, that developed mRNA vaccines in a year, keep reaching for the most expensive and most destructive method? Why does it truncate Heraclitus, why does it forget Timbuktu, why does it act as if Andrew Marshall’s programme were a force of nature? The answer must lie in the architecture of our societies themselves. There is an authority that profits from precisely this truncation. It produced it. It sustains it. It will defend it for as long as it can.

War is not the father of all things.

War is the father of a particular kind of things — those needed by the system that manages war.

All other things must find other fathers.
