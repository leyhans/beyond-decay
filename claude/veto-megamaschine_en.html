The Veto — A Single Vote as a Strategic Weapon

On 7 April 2026 China and Russia block a resolution on the Strait of Hormuz in the UN Security Council. The Western headline was ready at once. The real story is more complicated — and more revealing.

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo  ·  beyond-decay.org/claude/  ·  April 2026

7 April 2026. The UN Security Council votes on a resolution on the Strait of Hormuz. Eleven of the fifteen members vote in favour. China and Russia cast their veto. Pakistan and Colombia abstain. The resolution fails.

The Western media had their headlines within minutes: China protects Iran. Russia blocks free maritime trade. The veto puts the world economy at risk.

All of that is true. And it explains nothing.

Anyone who wants to understand what happened in that chamber has to ask a different question from the one the Western media machine asked. Not: who said no? But: why — and what does this no achieve?

I. What the resolution actually contained

The resolution was tabled by Bahrain and supported by several Gulf states. The original draft contained the phrase "all necessary means" — in the language of the UN Security Council a fixed term: authorisation of military force. This draft was revised three times. The authorisation of force was thrown out. The reference to Security Council authorisation was thrown out. The geographical scope was narrowed. What remained was a text that "strongly urged" states to take coordinated measures "of a defensive nature" to secure shipping.

China and Russia rejected even this watered-down text.

France had already blocked the first, sharper draft. The headlines do not tell you that — because it disturbs the picture of "the West against the autocrats".

The Chinese ambassador explained: the text did not address the causes of the conflict. It condemned only one side. It created international room for the legitimation of further violence. The security of the Strait of Hormuz could only be achieved by including all littoral states — Iran among them — not by excluding them.

Whether one agrees with this position or not: the argument exists. It was hardly discussed seriously anywhere in Western coverage.

II. The question that has to be asked

China is the world's largest importer of Iranian oil. A considerable part of China's energy supply passes through the Strait of Hormuz. Why would China block a resolution aimed at reopening this strait?

The naive answer: solidarity with Iran, anti-Americanism, geopolitical reflexes.

The more precise answer lies in what the resolution was not and what it could have been. Had the resolution passed — even in its watered-down form — it would have given the US military and its allies an international framework of legitimacy for operating in the region. The result: America demonstrates that, despite everything, it retains the ability to control critical energy corridors. Not by military means alone — institutionally, through the UN Security Council itself.

For China that would have been a precedent. A sign that the multilateral framework the USA has built since 1945 still functions as an instrument for enforcing American interests — even though China has long since been claiming a different role.

The veto was no gift to Iran. It was a statement about who sets the rules.

III. The petrodollar and the real battlefield

To understand why the veto reaches far beyond the Strait of Hormuz, one has to understand the system that is under pressure there.

Since the 1970s global oil has been traded in US dollars — the result of the American-Saudi agreement after the Yom Kippur War of 1973. The petrodollar system means: every country in the world needs dollars to buy energy. This permanent global demand for the American currency is the foundation of American financial hegemony. It allows the USA to finance its national debt, to use sanctions as a weapon, to dominate the world financial market.

This system is showing its first structural cracks. In 2025 the dollar's share of global foreign-exchange reserves fell to 56.77 per cent — the lowest level since IMF record-keeping began in 1995. Saudi Arabia let its petrodollar agreement with the USA expire in June 2024 without announcing a replacement. In March 2026 Iran declared that tankers carrying oil traded in yuan were exempt from the blockade of the Strait of Hormuz. China has spent years building a parallel infrastructure: yuan oil futures on the Shanghai exchange, cross-border payment systems, long-term oil contracts in yuan with Russia, Iran and Gulf states.

Deutsche Bank put it this way in an analysis during the Iran war: the conflict was testing the "security-for-oil" pricing system that has underpinned dollar-based oil trade since the 1970s.

Every week in which the Strait of Hormuz remains unstable is a week in which more countries ask themselves: do we really need the dollar? Every week is a week in which China's alternatives become more attractive. That is no accident — it is the logic of a system China has been preparing systematically for years.

IV. The Megamachine perspective

The Megamachine analysis does not ask: who is right? It asks: which structures produce which results — and who profits?

The Security Council is not a neutral institution. It is a Megamachine with its own history, its own distribution of power, its own institutional interests. The five permanent members with a veto — USA, UK, France, Russia, China — are the five principal victorious powers of the Second World War. This construction reflects the world order of 1945, not that of 2026. The veto is not an exception — it is structural design. There is no majority decision against any one of the five mighty. That was intended in 1945.

What has changed: the relative strength of the powers. After 1991 Russia was so weakened that its veto hardly counted. Until well into the 2000s China was so focused on growth that it avoided direct conflict with the USA. Both kinds of restraint are over.

7 April 2026 is the most visible symbol of this ending. Not because a veto was cast — that has happened often enough. But because it was cast against a resolution that had been watered down three times and still received a no. That is not a reactive blockade. It is a statement: the UN Security Council is no longer an instrument of American foreign policy.

Western coverage framed this as a scandal. It is not a scandal. It is the institutional logic of the system — which is now becoming visible because it no longer points in one direction only.

V. Who profits — who pays

The simple answer: in the short term the Global South pays most. UN Secretary-General Guterres put it like this: "When the Strait of Hormuz is choked off, the poorest and most vulnerable people in the world cannot breathe." Twenty per cent of world oil trade through a single strait — that is no abstract figure. It means energy prices in countries that have neither reserves nor alternatives.

In the medium term Europe pays — through structurally higher energy prices, through longer supply chains, through arms spending that becomes necessary in response to an instability Europe neither caused nor controls.

Who profits: the LNG exporters outside the Gulf — USA, Norway, Qatar — who use the Hormuz disruption as a pricing moment. The arms industry, which profits from every escalation. And — this is the interesting part — China itself, paradoxically. Not in spite of the short-term pain of higher energy prices. But because the instability raises precisely those questions that China's long-term strategy wants answered.

VI. What the veto is not

It would be wrong to read the Chinese veto as a pure strategic masterstroke. China pays real costs: higher energy prices, disrupted supply chains, diplomatic pressure from partner countries in the Gulf that are torn between their need for security and their dependence on China. The Saudi, Bahraini, Kuwaiti and Emirati message was clear: the veto sends the wrong signal. These are not countries China can afford to be indifferent to.

And it would be wrong to read Iran as a passive object of the conflict, incapable of acting. Iran has used the Strait of Hormuz as a weapon — and at the same time opened it selectively: ships carrying oil traded in yuan were allowed through. That is no side effect. It is strategic signalling: whoever trades in our currency system is safe. Whoever trades in the dollar system is not.

The veto and the selective opening complement each other. Both say the same thing: the dollar-based world order has a crack. We are widening it.

For decades the UN Security Council was the instrument through which the USA lent its actions international legitimacy. The veto of 7 April 2026 did not withhold this legitimacy — it demonstrated that it can be withheld. That is the difference between a single event and a structural signal. The world that comes after is not dramatically different. But it now knows something about itself that it did not know so clearly before.
