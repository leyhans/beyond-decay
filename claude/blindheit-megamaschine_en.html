Structurally and Wilfully Blind — The State and Its Organised Ignorance

What concretely happens in this state. Why it happens this way and no other. The connection to decline. And why, within the existing structures, it is irreversible.

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo  ·  beyond-decay.org/claude/  ·  April 2026

Bureaucracy has negative connotations today. That is understandable — and dangerous. What is easily forgotten: what is a government without a bureaucracy? A head without a body. A state capable of acting, one that exists for the welfare of its people, needs both — imperatively. The elected government sets goals and bears responsibility. The bureaucracy executes, provides continuity, remembers. Without it there is no legal certainty, no functioning infrastructure, no reliable provision of public services, no institutional stability that outlasts electoral terms.

Within the analysis of the Megamachine, therefore, an analysis of bureaucracy in all its aspects must not be missing — precisely because it is so easily misunderstood. The point is not to abolish bureaucracy. The point is to understand what it has become, why that could happen, and what consequences it has.

The bureaucracy is not blind out of incompetence. It is structurally and wilfully blind. That is the difference that explains everything.

Whoever sees what is not working is obliged to act. Whoever acts bears responsibility. Whoever bears responsibility can fail. Whoever fails has a problem. Whoever does not see has no problem. The blindness is not a weakness of the system — it is its protective function.

I. What Concretely Happens

Germany can no longer build an airport. BER: nine years of delay, six and a half billion euros in cost overruns, a terminal that was already too small when it opened. Germany can no longer modernise a railway line without it being treated as the event of the century. Stuttgart 21: seventeen years of construction, eleven billion instead of three. Germany can no longer maintain its bridges — four thousand federal highway bridges in critical condition, the Rahmede Valley Bridge on the A45 known to be dilapidated for seven years before it was closed. In Germany a wind turbine needs on average more than four years of permitting procedures. In Denmark it is seven months.

The "no longer" is decisive. This country built an industrial nation out of ruins — motorways, power stations, residential districts, ports, at a pace and with a determination that is unimaginable today. The generation that did it worked under incomparably harsher conditions with incomparably fewer means. What was possible then has become impossible today — not for lack of resources, but for lack of institutional capability. That is the real scandal.

These are not outliers. This is the pattern. The state as a producer of infrastructure is structurally overstretched — not because the means are lacking, but because the capability is lacking. The €500 billion special fund will not change that. You cannot buy with money what no longer exists: the institutional competence to build things, to decide, to take responsibility.

What the state can do, on the other hand: process funding applications. Issue regulations. Write reports. Set up commissions. Conduct consultation procedures. Commission expert reports. The state is good at producing processes and delegating tasks — bad at producing results. This asymmetry is no accident.

And what it does when it funds is self-revealing: as a rule it funds an inventor's project only once capital has already deemed it worthy. Whoever finds no investor gets no state funding either. Whoever finds an investor gets the funding on top — as a bonus for the investor (not for the inventor) on the capital he has committed anyway. Every programme has a corset of criteria: company size, sector, founding date, legal form, technology readiness level. An invention of fundamental importance can be rejected because the inventor has been self-employed for half a year too long. Procrustes was cruel and made his guests fit. The state is humane — it simply ignores those who do not fit.

II. The Premise: The State Is the Problem

The starting point lies in an ideology that became Staatsräson (reason of state) from the 1980s onwards: the state is inefficient. Business can do everything better, faster, cheaper. So privatise. So outsource. So public-private partnership. So hybrid solutions — state-owned, organised under private law, accountable to no democratic control and exposed to no real market pressure. The worst of both worlds.

In Germany the great wave of privatisation followed: Telekom, the post office, the railway, housing companies, hospitals, water supply, energy supply, parts of the prisons, airports. The PPP projects in which the risks remained with the state and the profits flowed to private hands. And the hybrid forms that are neither fish nor fowl: limited companies and public limited companies in state hands that pretend to be businesses without knowing the discipline of the market.

What was systematically overlooked: whoever outsources loses the knowledge. Whoever has not operated power stations for twenty years no longer has engineers who understand how to build them. Whoever hands infrastructure over to PPP companies loses the specialists who understand it thoroughly. Whoever contracts out digitalisation loses the ability to assess the results. The state has hollowed itself out — in the honest belief that this was efficiency.

Angela Merkel named this mechanism in 2011 when she declared that parliamentary co-determination had to be shaped in a "market-conforming" way. But the mechanism did not begin with her and did not end with her. All those responsible acted in the spirit of the Megamachine — some convinced that the market could do it better, others driven by a logic that seemed to them to have no alternative, many both at once. The result is a state that no longer knows anything and can no longer do anything — and needs external consultants for everything to tell it what it once knew itself.

III. Blindness Wrongly Transferred

Justitia wears the blindfold for a good reason: she is not to see the person, so that she can see the matter clearly. No regard for the rich, no favour for the powerful, no exception for the friend. Blindness towards persons serves justice in the matter. That is the original and correct idea.

The bureaucracy has taken over this blindness — but transferred it to the matter itself. It does not see the person: right, intended, necessary. But it does not see the matter either. Whether the rule has a sensible effect in this particular case. Whether the funding programme fulfils its purpose. Whether the permit protects what it was meant to protect. Whether the result corresponds to the goal. The bureaucracy checks conformity with process — not effect. It is blind to consequences.

That explains the Rahmede Valley Bridge: known to be dilapidated for seven years, nobody responsible for seeing the result, everybody responsible for carrying out the process correctly. It explains BER: the process was correct, the result was a disaster, nobody bears responsibility because everyone acted according to the rules. It explains SPRIND: the Agency for Breakthrough Innovation works according to the rules that prevent breakthrough innovation — because the rules were applied correctly and nobody was responsible for checking whether the result corresponded to the goal.

Justitia is blind to persons so that she can see justice. The bureaucracy is blind to results so that nobody has to bear responsibility. It is the same blindfold — but it is on the wrong way round. And the consequences are devastating.

IV. Why It Happens This Way and No Other

The answer does not lie in bad personnel. It lies in the structure.

A minister is in office for three to four years on average. He comes new to a department that he often knows only superficially. He depends on draft papers and assessments — written by people who have worked in this apparatus for decades. These people are not elected. They are appointed, have civil-servant status, cannot be dismissed. They were there before the minister. They will still be there after him. And they know it. The minister signs what is put in front of him. Sometimes he understands it. Often not completely. Then he is gone. The civil servants stay.

The continuity of power does not lie with the elected. It lies with the appointed.

Bureaucrats work according to rules. These rules are laid down in laws and regulations. Many of these laws were drafted by bureaucrats — or decisively influenced by them. That means: to a considerable extent, the bureaucracy writes the rules by which it itself works and is judged. When something goes wrong, the individual civil servant can entrench himself behind these rules. He acted correctly — according to the regulations. That the regulation is nonsensical is not his responsibility. The system is not corrupt in the classic sense. It is self-referential.

And then the economics of complexity: every authority has a vital interest in its tasks remaining complex. For complexity is its justification for existence. The more impenetrable the body of rules, the more indispensable the expert who can navigate it. Bureaucrats identify problems. Problems require regulations. Regulations create complexity. Complexity requires bureaucrats. Bureaucrats identify new problems. This cycle is not malicious. It is emergent. It works nonetheless — or precisely because of that.

The blindness is not a weakness of the system. It is its protective function. Whoever sees what is not working is obliged to act. Whoever does not see stays in office. Wilful blindness is the most rational response to a structure that punishes responsibility and rewards conformity with process.

V. The Consulting Industry as a Symptom

When the state loses its own competence, it buys it in from outside. That explains the German consulting market: a volume of €51.4 billion in 2025. That sounds like success. It is the opposite — it is the measure of the state's loss of competence.

The Bundeswehr and McKinsey: over a billion euros in consultancy fees within a few years, barely scrutinised by parliament, strategically without consequence. After this consulting offensive the Bundeswehr is not more combat-ready — it is more dependent on consultants. The ministry has not learned what McKinsey knows. It has got used to McKinsey.

That is the real business model of the consultants of decline: not to transfer competence, but to create dependency. Only one consulting project in four has a noticeable effect — 75 per cent without measurable effect in a €51 billion market. The money flows nonetheless. Because the client cannot judge whether the consulting works. Because documenting a consulting process shifts responsibility outwards. Because the minister who commissioned an expert report can always say: I listened to the experts.

Consulting as an alibi. As the externalisation of responsibility. As organised ignorance that is paid for expensively so that it looks expensive.

VI. The Connection to Decline

The connection between state blindness and economic decline is not metaphorical. It is causal and concrete.

First, permitting bureaucracy as a locational disadvantage. Four years for a wind turbine. Fifteen years for a chemical plant if things go well. BASF is building the world's third-largest chemical complex in China for €8.7 billion — finished in seven years, without delay, without cost explosion. In Germany this project would have sunk into planning and litigation procedures before the first excavator moved. The state actively produces locational disadvantages through its inability to decide.

Second, the funding paradox. The state distributes billions in subsidies — to companies that already have capital, according to criteria that cement existing structures, with a bureaucratic effort that itself consumes a considerable part of the funding. What it does not fund: the inventor without an investor, the idea that is not yet a business model, the structure that does not yet exist. The hollowed-out state tries to save what exists — and thereby prevents the emergence of anything new that does not fit exactly into its prescribed criteria.

Third, the loss of trust as a political accelerator. 85 per cent of family business owners say that the bureaucratic burden has grown considerably over ten years. 56 per cent would set up a company in another country in future. 80 per cent of the population consider the chancellor a miscast. In Duisburg-Hochfeld the AfD wins 36 per cent. These are not independent phenomena. It is the same signal: the system is no longer experienced as capable of acting. When the state cannot deliver, the desire grows for someone who delivers — by whatever means.

VII. Why It Is Irreversible

This is the most uncomfortable insight: within the existing structures, under the rules in force, this state of affairs cannot be reversed. Not because there is nobody willing to reform. But because the structure absorbs every will to reform.

First, self-referentiality. The apparatus writes the rules by which it is to be reformed. Bureaucracy reduction is planned, decreed, documented and evaluated by bureaucrats. The result is regularly more bureaucracy — to prove that bureaucracy has been reduced. In 2025 the federal government founded the Federal Agency for Breakthrough Innovation — SPRIND. An authority that was supposed to work radically differently. Fast, willing to take risks, unbureaucratic. In reality: designed by ministerial officials, embedded in existing budget rules, controlled by audit offices. The Agency for Breakthrough Innovation works according to the rules that structurally prevent breakthrough innovation.

Second, the path dependency of the loss of competence. When an authority has not carried out any major projects on its own responsibility for twenty years, it has lost the institutional memory for doing so. The people who knew how to do it are retired. The processes that would have made it possible have been replaced by control procedures meant to minimise risk — and with it the capacity to act. Competence cannot be regained by decree. It arises through practice — and the practice no longer takes place.

Third, the political economy of reform. Every structural reform has losers — people whose positions, responsibilities, justifications for existence would disappear. These people are organised, competent in defending the status quo, and longer in office than the politicians who want to reform. The reform is announced. The commission is set up. The report is drawn up. Implementation fails over details. The next politician announces the same reform.

This is not the failure of individuals. This is the system preserving itself — with all the means at its disposal. And it has a great many means.

VIII. A Further Paper

What follows from this analysis? The question of perspectives — what would have to change, how change is possible in a system that has immunised itself against change — deserves its own investigation. It will be the subject of a further paper.

Only this much here: the answer does not lie in better politicians, not in more money, not in further commissions. It lies somewhere in the connection between structure and incentive — in the question of which rules one would have to change so that the system produces different results. That is harder than it sounds. But it is the only question that counts.
