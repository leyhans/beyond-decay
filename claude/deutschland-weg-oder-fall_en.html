Germany — A Path into the Future or Free Fall?

A fork in the road that is open because the conditions for both directions really exist — and a structural finding that goes beyond day-to-day politics.

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo
beyond-decay.org · 11 May 2026

The question has been hanging in the air for about three years, with growing sharpness — is Germany on its way into a future of whatever kind, or is it in free fall? The question is asked, answered and pushed aside again. The answers are contradictory, depending on who is speaking. Some see a temporary phase of weakness from which the country will emerge with the right reforms. Others see a dynamic of decay that can no longer be reversed. Both sides have evidence. Both sides are mistaken.

What this paper attempts is not a third answer, but a clarification of the question. The question path or fall is not binary, because the conditions for both directions currently really exist. Which direction becomes reality depends on decisions that have not yet been made. These decisions are not made by a single actor, but in a multitude of fields at the same time. They depend on selection conditions that are structurally given and that currently favour the fall. But they can be changed, if those involved know what is at stake.

This clarification belongs within the Megamachine framework, because the German situation cannot be understood on its own terms. What happens in Germany happens in a global field of selection that limits the possibilities of national politics. The question is not whether Germany can decide by its own strength what it wants — that sovereignty has been a fiction ever since the country was absorbed into the global flows of capital and data. The question is what room for manoeuvre remains within the Megamachine, and how it is used or squandered.

I. The Question and Why It Is Difficult

The word future has become blurred in German discourse. Some mean by it the preservation of Germany as an industrial location in its previous form — car manufacturing, mechanical engineering, chemicals, steel. Others mean a transformation into a post-fossil, service- and knowledge-based economy. A third group means the defence of the democratic constitution against its erosion. A fourth means the ecological turn, which in recent years has been pushed out of the foreground again. These four meanings are not identical, and are often even opposed. Whoever says future should say which one.

The words free fall are similarly blurred. Three dynamics of decay are mixed together that need to be separated analytically. Economic erosion means the loss of industrial substance — plant relocations, the exodus of research from the Mittelstand (Germany's small and medium-sized, often family-owned firms), the sale of technological crown jewels abroad. Institutional decay means the growing inability of state structures to fulfil their tasks — the railways, schools, public authorities, the Bundeswehr. Political and civilisational decay means the shift in democratic culture, the coarsening of language, the loss of trust, the growth of a party that until now stood outside the democratic consensus.

The three dynamics of decay are connected, but they are not identical. An economic crisis need not inevitably produce democratic upheaval — Sweden came through a severe economic crisis in the 1990s without political erosion. Institutional decay need not lead to economic decline — Italy has lived for decades with a dysfunctional administration and nonetheless a capable industry. A political crisis need not mean ruin — the USA survived Trump and got him back again without American capitalism losing its shape. The question, then, is not whether there is a uniform decay, but how the individual dynamics of decay are connected and how they can reinforce or slow each other down.

Whoever gives a quick answer to the question path or fall has generalised one of these layers. The honest answer requires looking at the three layers individually and then asking how they interact.

II. What There Is to Free Fall

Since about 2022 the findings have piled up with a density that is hard to argue away.

On the economic level: the car industry loses substance with every quarterly report. Volkswagen announces plant closures, Bosch and ZF are cutting tens of thousands of jobs, parts of Schaeffler are being transferred to Chinese investors, BASF is shifting chemical value creation to China and the USA. The big German banks are no longer present in the international league. In the platform economy there are no German or European players of global importance. In artificial intelligence Germany does not have a single provider that could compete with the large American or Chinese models. The Mittelstand, long praised as the backbone, is struggling with energy costs, bureaucracy, a lack of successors and a shortage of skilled workers that is not being compensated for by immigration.

On the institutional level: Deutsche Bahn has become an international symbol of institutional failure. More than half of long-distance trains are late. Bridges are closed because they are in danger of collapse. The schools deliver worse results in every PISA round, especially dramatically in reading and mathematics. According to its own Inspector General, the Bundeswehr is not operational — despite the special fund, despite the Kriegstüchtigkeit (war-readiness) proclaimed by Pistorius. Public authorities need periods of time for permits that would be unimaginable in other countries. The judiciary is overloaded, proceedings drag on for years, and in many cases sentences are no longer enforced because there are not enough staff.

On the political and civilisational level: in 2026 the AfD has reached stable poll ratings of between twenty-four and twenty-seven per cent. In the eastern German states it is the strongest force. It is represented in the Bundestag by more than a hundred members, and the party sits in several state governments with toleration agreements. Trust in the established parties has reached historic lows. The SPD and the FDP are both in a state that can soberly be described as dissolution — the one is moving towards becoming a rump party with ten to twelve per cent, the other has vanished from the Bundestag. The Greens have lost their credibility in a series of conflicts and are themselves in a process of shrinking. The CDU governs in a grand coalition with the SPD, with Friedrich Merz as Chancellor. What makes things difficult is not the existence of this government, but the fact that no political force any longer has a majority that could legitimise a mandate to shape things. Politics becomes the administration of what exists, without any recognisable direction.

These three dynamics of decay reinforce one another. Economic erosion produces social losers who become politically receptive to forces that reject the established structures. Institutional decay produces a diffuse mistrust of the state, which in turn is exploited politically. The political crisis makes it harder to carry out the reforms that could ease economic and institutional problems. That is the spiral in which Germany currently finds itself.

III. What There Is to Future

The counter-findings are less spectacular, but they are real. Whoever does not name them writes a one-sided diagnosis.

The industrial Mittelstand holds, in many niches, a technological position that cannot be replaced internationally. In machine tools, speciality chemicals, measurement and control technology, optics, high-pressure hydraulics, pump technology, sensor technology and medical devices there are German manufacturers that are world market leaders in their segment and will probably still be so in twenty years. These manufacturers are often family businesses or foundation-owned companies. They have a different time logic from listed corporations. They invest for the long term, they do not reflexively relocate abroad, they cultivate relationships with their customers over generations. This substance is hardly visible in public discourse, because it produces no spectacular headlines.

The research landscape is still world-class in parts. The Max Planck Institutes, the Helmholtz Centres, the Leibniz Institutes, the Fraunhofer-Gesellschaft, the universities of excellence — they produce research at a level envied by many countries. Patent applications from Germany continue to be among the highest in the world, measured per capita. The problems lie less in research itself than in translating research into marketable products. But the substance is there.

The concepts for structural reform are on the table. The Gesellschaft mit gebundenem Vermögen (company with locked-in assets), developed since 2019 and presented as a framework concept in March 2026 by Justice Minister Hubig and Finance Minister Klingbeil, could create a form of ownership that structurally rules out the extraction of wealth from productive companies. The debate about reforming federalism, about a genuine separation of powers, about a slimmed-down administration is under way, even if implementation is being blocked. There is no lack of concepts — what is lacking is the political strength to implement them.

Democratic culture is, despite everything, more stable than in comparable countries. There is a broad and committed civil society. The demonstrations against right-wing extremism in the spring of 2024 showed that millions of people are prepared to take to the streets for democracy. The media landscape is plural, with all the weaknesses that can be observed. The judiciary has preserved its independence in several cases in recent years, even if its structural entanglement with the executive remains a lasting problem. A majority of the population continues to regard the constitution as worth defending.

The energy transition has, despite all resistance, built up substance. In 2026 the share of renewable energy in electricity generation is over sixty per cent. Storage technologies are developing faster than forecast. The heating transition is progressing more slowly, but it is coming. The transport transition is lagging behind, but its components — expansion of the rail network, charging infrastructure, cycling concepts — are being implemented in many municipalities. Here too: the findings are ambivalent, but they are not uniformly negative.

These counter-findings do not refute the diagnosis of decay. But they show that the diagnosis is not the whole picture. Whoever sees only economic erosion, institutional decay and political crisis overlooks a substance that is still there and that could be used for a different direction. The substance alone is not enough — it would have to be mobilised, protected, expanded. But it is there, and that is not trivial.

IV. The AfD and the Shift in What Can Be Said

The AfD must be given a section of its own in an analysis of the German situation, because it is not one symptom among others, but a structural finding that poses the question of direction precisely. Whoever regards the AfD merely as an electoral success overlooks the crucial point — the shift in what has become sayable, and in who engages with whom, and in what way.

The size of the party is a first finding. A party that has polled stably between twenty and twenty-seven per cent for more than two years is no longer a fringe phenomenon. It represents a quarter to a third of the electorate. In some eastern German state parliaments it is the strongest group. It is represented in the European Parliament. Its voters are not primarily the economic losers, as was assumed at the beginning, but a socially broadly spread group that shares a particular understanding of the world and of values.

The finding on content is sharper. Since its founding in 2013 the party has been in a process of radicalisation that is not yet complete. What began as an economically liberal protest party is today a party in which the tone is set by currents that the Federal Constitutional Court and the Office for the Protection of the Constitution classify as confirmed right-wing extremist. The state associations in Saxony, Thuringia and Saxony-Anhalt are under surveillance. Leading representatives of the party use language that would still have been politically unacceptable in the 1980s and 1990s — terms such as remigration, Great Replacement, Schuldkult (cult of guilt) have migrated from the extreme right-wing fringe into the parliamentary centre.

The structural question, however, is not the AfD itself — it is the reaction of the other actors. Here, for about two years, a shift has been taking place that is more decisive for the question of Germany's direction than the AfD's ratings themselves.

First — the adoption of individual positions. Under Friedrich Merz, the CDU has taken up several issues that were previously regarded as AfD issues. Migration policy has been tightened with a rhetoric that would have been unthinkable in the CDU five years ago. In the vote on the so-called Zustrombegrenzungsgesetz (Influx Limitation Act) in January 2025, the CDU organised a majority with the votes of the AfD — a historic taboo was broken, one that had been referred to in the CDU as the Brandmauer (firewall). The argument goes that one must show voters that one takes their concerns seriously, otherwise they will go to the AfD. The effect is the reverse — the AfD is normalised through the adoption of its positions, its voters are confirmed in their perception, and the threshold for direct cooperation drops.

Second — the models of toleration and cooperation. In several eastern German municipalities and districts there have been examples since 2024 in which the CDU adopts the substance of AfD motions, the AfD supports CDU motions, or joint majorities emerge without formal coalitions being concluded. In Thuringia in 2024 a district administrator was elected with AfD votes — something that was unthinkable only a few years ago. The argument of the actors involved is that it is about the matter at hand, not about politics. The structural effect is the transformation of the AfD from an ostracised force into one capable of cooperation.

Third — the relativisations in public discourse. In the arts and essay pages, in talk shows, in newspaper commentaries, the language has been shifting since about 2023. There is less talk of the AfD as a danger to democracy and more about the established parties as the cause of the AfD's rise. There is less talk about the AfD's right-wing extremist currents and more about the legitimacy of its issues. The shift is not uniform, and there are still sharp analyses — but it is measurable. The discursive space in which the AfD is named as a danger to democracy is shrinking. The discursive space in which its positions are treated as legitimate political options is growing.

Fourth — the institutional consequences. With every election in which the AfD wins seats, it occupies further positions — committee chairs, administrative boards, advisory councils. The question whether to grant it these positions or deny them is negotiated afresh in each individual case. Where they are denied, the formal rules of parliamentarism are weakened. Where they are granted, it is given institutional power. Both are a dilemma, and there are no clean solutions. The tendency is towards acceptance — which in turn accelerates normalisation.

That is the real structural finding. The AfD does not grow by its own strength alone. It grows because the selection conditions of political competition and media attention accommodate it. The firewall was an agreement that certain kinds of cooperation would not take place. For about two years it has been dismantled piece by piece. The agreement was a construction, not a law of nature. When it is no longer kept, it is no longer there.

The historical parallel suggests itself and should be drawn precisely. The Weimar Republic did not perish through a coup, but through a chain of coalition decisions in which the established parties believed they could integrate, control and instrumentalise the NSDAP. They were mistaken. Today's AfD is not the NSDAP, the situation is not 1932, and the Federal Republic is not the Weimar Republic. But the mechanics by which an anti-democratic force can reach a position from which to dismantle democracy from within can be studied. They run through the gradual erosion of the agreements that exclude it — not through an open break.

The AfD is not the problem. The problem is that a force that rejects the democratic system is put in a position to change the system by the system's own means. The force itself does not do that. It is the other actors who pave the way for it — out of fear, out of calculation, out of weariness, or because they do not think the consequences through to the end.

V. The Fork in the Road

The question path or fall hinges on conditions that have not yet been decided. Both directions are possible, because the substance for both exists. Which direction becomes reality will be decided at concrete turning points in the coming years.

Conditions for the path into a future:

An industrial architecture that does not try to conserve the old industry, but protects the substance where it is still viable and at the same time creates the conditions for new industrial fields. That requires an industrial policy that goes beyond subsidies — one that undertakes structural change. Energy costs, bureaucracy, approval procedures, educational provision and research funding would have to be reorganised so that Germany's strengths in the Mittelstand and in the specialist niches actually become usable.

A genuine structural reform of ownership. The Gesellschaft mit gebundenem Vermögen would actually have to be anchored in law, rather than continuing to lie in drawers as a concept. The condition for this is overcoming the resistance of the established business associations, which see this form of ownership as a threat to their business model — and rightly so, because the GmgV makes extractive capital structurally unprofitable.

An education reform that once again understands education as education and not as administration. That requires a different kind of teacher training, different forms of teaching, different school structures, a different way of financing education. Above all, it requires turning away from the dominant logic of measurement and comparison that has shaped the education system over the past twenty years — and that has accelerated its erosion rather than halted it.

An institutional renewal that establishes a genuine separation of powers — separating the judiciary from the executive, freeing public prosecutors' offices from being bound by instructions, making parliament actually independent of the government, regulating lobbying. These reforms have been described for decades and for decades have not been implemented, because they would put an end to the comfort of the established parties.

A political language that can once again formulate what is wanted — not only what is rejected. That is a question of democratic culture, not of institutions. It cannot be produced by laws. It requires a movement in the middle of society that dares to formulate positive designs instead of limiting itself to warding off what exists.

A renewal of the culture of invention. For centuries Germany was a country in which inventing was a respected activity. This tradition has eroded — through the marginalisation of the independent inventor, through the bureaucratisation of research, through the shift of the willingness to take risks to Silicon Valley. Regaining this culture is possible, but it requires a different funding landscape, a different legal framework, a different kind of social recognition.

Conditions for free fall:

Clinging to the administrative mode. If politics continues to announce reforms without implementing them, if concepts are developed without becoming laws, if the structures are better protected against the reformers than against their own decay — then the fall continues.

Using reform debates as a sedative. If the discussion about reforms takes on the function of replacing the need for reforms — if talking about change compensates for the lack of change — then the debate itself is part of the problem. This function has been observable in German politics for years. Much is said, little is decided, even less is implemented.

Subsidising dying industries instead of structural reform. If tax money is used to keep alive industries that no longer have a future under global selection conditions, while the structural preconditions for new industries are not created, then the state is financing its own decline.

Fighting symptoms instead of working on causes in the face of the AfD's rise. If the established parties believe they can cut the ground from under the AfD by adopting its issues, they accelerate its rise. If they believe they can domesticate the AfD through cooperation, they normalise it. Historical experience is unambiguous — neither strategy has ever worked against comparable forces. Yet they are repeated.

Demographic shrinkage without a plan. Over the next twenty years Germany will lose several million people of working age, without any coherent strategy for how the economy, the social security systems, education and care are to respond. The immigration that would have to compensate for this is increasingly blocked politically. The increase in productivity that could absorb it is not happening. The shrinkage meets an infrastructure built for a different population size.

The further shifting of responsibility to the EU or Brussels. If German politics gets used to delegating difficult decisions to the European level without playing a shaping role there, it will unlearn its own capacity to shape things. That is not an argument against European integration — it is an argument against using European integration as a substitute for policy of one's own.

VI. Who Decides What

The fork in the road is open because it hinges on decisions. Which decisions are made by whom?

The political class makes the immediate choices of direction — forming coalitions, legislation, administrative reforms. It is, however, itself part of the problem. Whoever sees the established parties as agents of renewal overlooks the fact that they are structurally dependent on the status quo. A reform of the separation of powers would end the parties' comfort. A reform of federalism would touch the sinecures of the state premiers. A reform of the electoral systems would make careers within the parties more difficult. The likelihood that these reforms will come from within the political class is small.

The economic class makes decisions about investment, locations, the direction of research, retaining employees. It is split between the large corporations, which operate in the global Megamachine and for which Germany is only one location among many, and the Mittelstand, which is often rooted in its region and has an interest of its own in a functioning German infrastructure. This split is too little present in political discourse. The voice of the corporations is organised in the BDI, BDA, INSM and similar associations. The voice of the Mittelstand is more diffuse, less organised, less influential in politics. Whoever wants to rescue the German situation would have to strengthen the Mittelstand — against the interests of the corporations, which often perceive it as competition.

Civil society makes decisions about engagement, mobilisation, club and association life, cultural practices. In Germany it is traditionally strong, but in recent years it has come under pressure — through competition from the digital platforms, through isolation, through the loss of trust in institutions. A revival of civil society is possible; in some areas it can even be observed. But it depends on conditions that are not all present.

Individual actors make decisions about how to live their lives, choice of career, political engagement, media use, investment in their children's education. These decisions add up to structural movements. If a generation stops choosing engineering professions, the engineers will be missing in twenty years. If a generation refuses to take part in politics, the political actors of the next period will be missing. Individually these decisions are inconspicuous; in sum they are decisive.

The Megamachine point is that the selection conditions under which all these decisions are made currently favour the fall. The global logic of capital valorisation favours relocations. The attention economy favours polarisation and simplification. The logic of careers favours adaptation to what exists. Whoever wants the path must work against these selection conditions — which is more strenuous, riskier and less rewarded than swimming with the current. That is not impossible, but it is demanding.

VII. The Self-Deception of Both Sides

In German discourse two camps face each other, both of which are right — and both wrong.

The pessimists see the decay in all its sharpness. They name the findings precisely; they do not underestimate them. But they often overlook the substance that is still there. They overlook the Mittelstand that carries the load. They overlook the research landscape that is productive. They overlook the civic energy that could be mobilised. From this one-sided view arises an attitude that wavers between resignation and apocalypticism. Both attitudes are unproductive — resignation because it no longer tries anything, apocalypticism because it loses all sense of proportion.

The optimists see the substance that is still there. They emphasise the capacity for reform, the innovative strength, the democratic stability. But they overlook the speed at which the selection conditions are changing. They overlook the fact that the substance is melting away while they praise it. They overlook the fact that the capacity for reform is blocked politically, that innovative strength is structurally hampered, that democratic stability is eroding. From this one-sided view arises an attitude of carry on as before that misjudges the problem.

What neither camp does is take an honest stock — one that admits that the findings of the fall are real and that the substance for a different direction is real too. This honest stocktaking is the precondition of every productive decision. Whoever cannot bear the findings cannot act. Whoever does not see the possibility cannot hope.

The self-deception of both sides has a common function. It makes the situation more bearable by simplifying it. It removes the effort that a differentiated perception would demand. It allows one to remain in a position without examining one's own assumptions. In this respect both camps are functionally the same — they are strategies of reassurance of a society that cannot bring itself to make a decision.

VIII. The Open Question

This paper does not end with an answer, but with a clarification. The question path or fall is precisely the question that is not honestly asked in Germany. Whoever asks it ends up in one of the two camps — he is regarded either as a doom-monger or as naive. This polarisation is itself part of the problem.

Asking the question precisely is the first step towards an answer. The answer is not: the road leads inevitably in one direction or the other. The answer is: both directions are possible, because the conditions for both exist. Which direction becomes reality depends on decisions that are made or left unmade. These decisions are not abstract — they have concrete addresses, concrete actors, concrete time horizons.

The fork in the road is open, but it does not stay open indefinitely. With every year in which the substance erodes without being protected or expanded, the weight shifts towards the fall. With every election in which the forces of renewal grow weaker, the options for the path shrink. With every further rapprochement with the AfD without clear consequences, the political mechanics change in a direction from which return becomes more difficult.

That is not an apocalyptic statement. It is a sober observation of the time dimension. History has a tempo. Whoever does not act while the fork is open will later find no fork any more, but a direction already decided. The German situation in 2026 is one in which decisions can still be made. Whether the actors who would have to decide recognise this and act accordingly is the open question.

What is currently lacking in Germany is not the diagnosis. The diagnosis is widely available, in many keys. What is lacking is the link between diagnosis and decision. The diagnosis is formulated, the decision is postponed. This postponement is itself a decision — a decision against the path and for the fall. It is just not named as such.

That is the structural point that fits the Megamachine framework. The Megamachine needs no evil actors to lead to the fall. It needs only actors who postpone their decisions. Postponement is the form in which the machine imposes its logic against the substance that resists it. Whoever recognises the postponement and names it has at least created the conditions under which a different decision could be made. That is not much. But it is more than what the two camps of self-deception have to offer.

Germany stands at a fork in the road. Both directions are possible, because the conditions for both really exist. The substance for the path is there, but it is eroding. The dynamic of the fall is real, but it is not predetermined.

Which direction becomes reality depends on decisions that have not yet been made — and on decisions that are already being made by not deciding. The selection conditions currently favour the fall. Whoever wants the path must work against them.

Asking the question path or fall honestly is the first step. The German situation in 2026 is one in which decisions can still be made. But it is not a situation in which decisions can be made indefinitely.
