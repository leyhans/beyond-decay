Accumulation of Capital

On the exploitation of the creative — and why the inventor type does not understand the system, because he thinks differently. Tesla, Westinghouse and the legalistic façade of the Honourable Merchant.

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo  ·  beyond-decay.org/claude/  ·  April 2026

There is a primal scene of capitalism that appears in no textbook. It does not take place on the stock exchange, not in the factory, not in the bank. It takes place at the moment when someone has an idea — and someone else realises that this idea is worth money.

The inventor thinks in solutions. He sees a problem that others do not see, and he sees a path that others do not take. What he does not usually see is the exploitation mechanism that will close around his idea like a vice around a workpiece. In the capitalist system the idea alone has no price. It acquires a price only through the person who has the means to exploit it — and at precisely that moment the expropriation begins.

I. The economic and the political means

In "The State", Franz Oppenheimer drew a distinction that is fundamental to this subject: the distinction between the economic means and the political means of acquisition. The economic means is one's own labour and voluntary exchange. The political means is the appropriation of the labour of others.

What Oppenheimer described for the state — the systematic skimming-off of productive labour by a ruling class — repeats itself in the relationship between capital and creativity in a subtler but structurally identical form. The creative person works with the economic means: he invents, designs, solves problems. Capital operates with the political means: it appropriates the results of this work — not through brute force, but through the drafting of contracts, information asymmetry and the simple fact that the creative person needs money before his idea earns money. This window of time between invention and return — that is the space in which the expropriation takes place.

II. Tesla, Westinghouse and the torn-up contract

Nikola Tesla died impoverished in a New York hotel room. George Westinghouse became rich — with Tesla's ideas, which he had lawfully bought. Thomas Edison became rich — not only with his own inventions, but with a system that industrialised other people's inventions. That is no historical accident. It is the logic of the system.

Yet the story is more precise than the usual narrative. Edison was himself an inventor — more than a thousand patents in his name — but above all he was an industrialiser: he had a laboratory with dozens of employees whose inventions legally belonged to him. Westinghouse had paid for Tesla's alternating-current system, not stolen it. The original contract between Tesla and Westinghouse would have made Tesla rich — and might have ruined Westinghouse.

Tesla tore up the contract. Typical inventor: he did not think in exploitation chains but in solutions. Perhaps out of generosity, perhaps out of naivety about business dealings, perhaps because the idea mattered more to him than the return. Afterwards — when Tesla was doing badly — Westinghouse took no further interest in his fate. He had broken nothing, morally. Tesla had torn up the contract, not he. He simply did what the system rewards: carried on.

That is the heart of the pattern. Not Edison as villain, not Westinghouse as scoundrel — but two types who inhabit the same system differently. The inventor type who tears up the contract because that is how he thinks. The entrepreneur type who no longer remembers as soon as there is nothing more to gain.

III. Patent law and its structural asymmetry

US patent law at the time was structured against Tesla in several ways — not through ill intent, but through the logic of the system. The "first to invent" principle meant: whoever could first prove to have had the idea — not whoever filed first. Edison had the resources to fight priority disputes through the courts. Tesla did not. Employee inventions automatically belonged to the employer — Tesla had worked for Edison for a time. Enforcing patents cost money that Tesla did not have. And as a foreigner — Serbian by birth, an Austro-Hungarian citizen — he had less access to the networks that brokered investors and business partners.

Patent law formally protects everyone equally — but structurally it favours whoever has the means to enforce it. Formally equal, structurally unequal. The same principle as market-conforming democracy: the rules apply to everyone. The ability to use them does not.

IV. The legalistic façade

This is a hallmark of the system: one tries for as long as possible to keep up the legalistic façade — the image of the Honourable Merchant and entrepreneur. Westinghouse did nothing illegal. Edison did nothing illegal. The contracts were lawful. The patents were valid. Patent law was complied with — as far as it paid to comply with it.

For that is the second part: what it costs and what it brings is weighed precisely. Preserving decency costs nothing as long as Tesla no longer produces any return. Forgetting it likewise costs nothing. The Honourable Merchant preserves the form as long as the form is useful — and drops it as soon as it no longer is. Not out of malice. Out of calculation.

The Megamachine produces this calculation automatically. It needs no evil people. It only needs structures that reward calculation and punish generosity. Tesla was generous. He lost. Westinghouse calculated. He won. The system learned from both — and today teaches the same lesson in a thousand incarnations: start-up exits, licence agreements, streaming platforms, funding bureaucracies.

V. The modern knowledge economy — the pattern perfected

The modern knowledge economy has not dissolved this mechanism — it has perfected it. Patent law, once invented to protect inventors, has become an instrument of the capital side. Holding a patent means nothing if one does not have the means to enforce it. Infringing a patent means little if the infringer has enough lawyers to make legal proceedings so long and expensive that the inventor gives up before judgment is given.

In the creative industries the situation may be even more drastic. The algorithm that distributes the song belongs to the platform. The code the developer writes belongs to the employer. The design the designer creates belongs to the client. Spotify pays a musician on average €0.003 to €0.005 per stream. The musician supplies the content that keeps the platform alive. The platform accumulates the capital that enables it to exploit the next musician on the same terms.

The funding landscape — public and private alike — reproduces the same structure. The inventor who applies for funding must translate his idea into the language of the funder: milestones, work packages, market analyses, exploitation plans. In doing so he loses months he would need for the actual work. That he increasingly needs specialists for this act of translation — funding consultants, grant writers, project managers — who themselves earn from the system, closes the circle: the bureaucracy of funding, too, accumulates capital from the creative achievement of the inventor.

One might object: but the creative person enters into these relationships voluntarily. That is formally true — and it is exactly the kind of argument that makes the asymmetry invisible. The "freedom" of the creative person consists in choosing between exploitation and invisibility. Whoever does not sign the licence agreement leaves his invention in the drawer. Whoever is not on Spotify does not exist in the music market. It is the same "freedom" that Oppenheimer attributes to the landless man who can "freely" choose to work for the landlord or to starve. Formal voluntariness conceals structural lack of alternatives.

Capital does not speak of appropriation but of "cooperation". Not of exploitation but of "win-win situations". Not of expropriation but of "intellectual property" — whereby the property always ends up with the one who did not create it, but paid for it.

VI. Two experiences — one pattern

What follows is not a theoretical construct. It is lived experience — twice, with different outcomes, and the difference was instructive.

Both cases concerned the same inventor, the same subject area, the same inventions — but in a different order and with different opponents.

Case one — first in time: a manufacturer of hand-held military equipment which, during its collaboration with the inventor, ran into the so-called peace crisis — which it has since successfully put behind it. Behind the inventor stood a regional state bank (Landesbank) in northern climes that had invested its own money in the invention. The manufacturer's calculation came out as follows: the costs of wrongdoing exceed the advantage. The inventor was treated almost as an equal. Almost. Not because the law demanded it — because the power structure enforced it.

Case two — later: a well-known German car manufacturer, currently in particularly serious difficulties. The same world of inventions, the same inventor — but without institutional protection behind him. He was dealt with accordingly. Without scruples, without legalistic complications. The calculation was simple: what does fraud cost? Nothing. What does lawfulness cost? More than the advantage is worth. Result: fraud.

The gap between the two cases makes the calculation explicitly visible: at the car manufacturer they knew the complete history — the affair with the arms manufacturer, the subject area, and the inventor's by now precarious personal situation. They did not merely recognise the absence of institutional protection. They identified the most favourable moment for the takeover — and acted. That is not blind system logic. That is deliberate calculation on the basis of complete information.

That is the legalistic façade in its purest form. One behaves lawfully exactly as far as that is cheaper than the opposite. Morality is a function of the distribution of power. Protection can be bought. Whoever has no Landesbank behind him has no protection. Whoever has an institutional patron is treated better — not equally, but better than without one. That is not a failure of the system. That is the system.

The creative person creates the value. Capital skims it off. Not through theft — through structure. Through the window of time between invention and return. Through the drafting of contracts, information asymmetry, patent law that is formally equal and structurally unequal. And through the legalistic façade of the Honourable Merchant — which is kept up for exactly as long as it pays.
