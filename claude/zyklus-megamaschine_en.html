The Cycle — Forms of Rule in the Service of the Megamachine

Monarchy, aristocracy, democracy, oligarchy, autocracy — the forms change. The structure beneath remains. Why this could not be proven in the past — and why it can be today.

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo  ·  beyond-decay.org/claude/  ·  April 2026

Forms of rule come and go. Monarchies fall. Aristocracies decay. Democracies erode. Autocracies collapse. This is called history — the rise and fall of civilisations, the change of constitutional forms, the ups and downs of peoples and their orders.

What historiography seldom describes is the continuity beneath. The structures of extraction, of accumulation, of organised command over labour and resources — they remain. Divine right becomes popular sovereignty — becomes market efficiency. The king becomes the corporation. The nobility becomes the financial elite. The Church becomes the expert commission. The garment changes. The body beneath does not.

Lewis Mumford was the first to see this. He dared to name what he saw — with what was available to him. And he was never really taken seriously by the recognised historians of his time. Not out of malice. Because in their view what he described was only a story — a story about history, a particular personal perspective. Nothing more. That is the most effective form of academic rejection: not refutation, but categorisation. Whoever is refuted is still in the game. What is ticked off as a personal perspective disappears from the discourse.

I. The old pattern — Polybius and the cycle

Polybius, the Greek historian of the 2nd century BC, described what he called anacyclosis — the cycle of constitutions. Monarchy becomes tyranny. Aristocracy becomes oligarchy. Democracy becomes ochlocracy — rule of the mob. Then the cycle begins anew. Polybius regarded this as a natural law of politics — inevitable, cyclical, without escape.

What Polybius could not describe: why the cycle always takes the same course. Why every form of rule degenerates in the same direction — towards the concentration of power and resources among the few at the expense of the many. He saw the pattern. He had no language for the force that produces it.

Machiavelli saw the same. He described with clinical precision how princes retain power, how republics decay, how institutions are hollowed out from within. He was no cynic — he was an observer of mechanisms. But he too had no category for what lies behind the mechanisms. He described the symptoms without being able to name the disease.

II. Why a scientific analysis was not possible before

Anyone in the past who wanted to describe the continuity behind the change of forms of rule had three options — and all three were unsatisfactory.

The first option was, and still is, the religious or esoteric interpretation: a hidden force steers history. The evil in the world. Fate. The world spirit. These interpretations saw and still see something real — the continuity of the pattern — but explain it by forces that cannot be grasped scientifically.

The second option was, and today more than ever is, the conspiracy theory: a group of people plans and steers across generations. There are interest groups that conspire to their own advantage and to the detriment of others. But a conscious coordination spanning generations and functioning over millennia is inconceivable.

The third option was and is metaphor: the pattern that can be discerned was and is described as a cultural phenomenon, as a character trait of civilisations, as the spirit of an epoch. That is what most of the great historians did — from Spengler to Toynbee. Metaphors are not wrong. But they cannot be proven.

Lewis Mumford attempted a fourth option: an analysis grounded in the history of technology and in cultural studies that describes the pattern as a structural phenomenon — not as conspiracy, not as metaphor, not as esoteric force. He called it the Megamachine: a system that sustains itself without anyone having decided it should, that organises rule without needing a ruler, that generates out of human parts a force which nobody really controls.

That was 1967. The tools he needed to prove this rigorously did not yet exist. Cybernetics existed — Norbert Wiener had laid the foundations. But complexity theory, network science, the scientific description of emergent intelligence — those came later. Mumford saw the right thing. He could not fully prove it. That is why his work remained in the borderland between science and vision. That is why the academic historians respectfully set him aside.

III. What is possible today

Physarum polycephalum is a slime mould. It has no nervous system, no brain, no planning. In 2010 Atsushi Tero and colleagues showed in Science that Physarum, when nutrients are placed at the positions of Tokyo's railway stations, builds a network that comes close to the actual Tokyo rail network in efficiency and redundancy — more efficiently than human engineers had planned it over decades. Without a plan. Without a head. Through feedback loops alone.

That is the scientific proof of what Mumford could not prove: complex systems with feedback loops develop emergent intelligence without a subject. They optimise without a planner. They sustain themselves without intention. They are not a conspiracy — they are a mechanism.

The Megamachine is Physarum in social form. The capital market optimises without a centre — it follows feedback loops that reward concentration and punish distribution. Bureaucracy grows without a plan — it follows the inherent logic of systems that optimise for their own continuation. The platform economy monopolises without intent — it follows network effects that reward size exponentially. Nobody decided this. It arises from the structure.

And that is why forms of rule change without the basic structure changing. The Megamachine needs no particular form of rule. It is compatible with monarchy, aristocracy, democracy, oligarchy, autocracy — because none of these forms alters the feedback loops that keep it running. It turns every resistance into content, the protests into brands, the revolutions into spectacle — and carries on.

IV. The inevitable blindness of the historians

It would be wrong to accuse the historians of ignorance. What they had was a structural blindness — imposed by the limits of the tools available to them.

History is a science of events, of actors, of decisions. It asks: who did what, when, and why? Those are legitimate questions. But they are not the questions that make the continuity of the Megamachine visible. The Megamachine has no actors in the historian's sense. It has no decisions that can be found in archives. It has no intention that can be reconstructed.

A historian who wanted to describe the continuity of extraction over centuries without the tool of systems theory had only metaphor. And metaphors — however apt they may be — are not proofs in science. Mumford experienced this: his analysis was too precise for esotericism and too metaphorical for science. He fell between two stools.

Today we no longer fall. Emergent systems can be described scientifically. Network dynamics can be measured. Feedback loops that produce concentration can be modelled. What Polybius described as a law of nature, what Machiavelli observed as a mechanism, what Mumford named the Megamachine — that can today be analysed with scientific precision.

V. What this means

Anacyclosis is not a natural law of politics. It is the self-preservation mechanism of the Megamachine. Forms of rule change because the existing form at some point loses its legitimacy — through failure, through contradiction, through exhaustion. The new form promises something different. And delivers, structurally, the same. Because the feedback loops that produce concentration are not altered by a change in the form of rule.

That explains why learning from history does not really work. One learns from the history of forms of rule. One prevents the next monarchy — and gets oligarchy. One prevents the next fascism — and gets autocratic legalism. One prevents dictatorship — and gets corporate rule. Along the way improvements arise and in part disappear again. There is real progress, often bought at the price of massive problems. But the basic structure remains — because nobody has changed the feedback loops.

Mumford saw this without being able to prove it. He was — in this sense — a forerunner without tools. The tools are here now.

The Megamachine has no king. It has no party. It has no plan. It has feedback loops — and they are more powerful than anything human beings have ever consciously designed. That is why it survives every change in the form of rule. And that is why it can be overcome only by changing the structures and rules — not by changing the people at the top.
