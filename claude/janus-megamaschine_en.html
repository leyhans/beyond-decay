The Janus Face of the State and of the Political Means

Two people are enough to start the Megamachine. And only the political means can tame the political means — where Oppenheimer and Mumford meet.

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo  ·  beyond-decay.org/claude/  ·  April 2026

The Megamachine does not need pyramids in order to exist. It needs no corporations, no state, no ideology. It needs two people. The moment one person applies the political means against another — the appropriation of other people's labour, the enforcement of obedience, the exploitation of weakness — the Megamachine begins. The "mega" suggests size. What matters is the principle. Everything else is scaling.

I. Where Oppenheimer and Mumford meet

In "The State", Franz Oppenheimer described the political means: the appropriation of the labour of others — as opposed to the economic means of voluntary exchange. The political means is as old as the first encounter between two people in which one was stronger than the other and used that strength.

Lewis Mumford described the Megamachine: the emergent system that arises when the political means is accumulated, organised, institutionalised. The pyramid — thousands of people coordinated under a single will — is only the first large visible manifestation. But the origin lies earlier: in the first moment when one person told another, you work for me, not because you want to but because I demand it.

This is where the two meet: the political means is the primal act of the Megamachine. Not greed, not malice, not conspiracy — but the plain use of superiority to appropriate the labour of others. This act has always existed. The Megamachine has always existed — in weak or strong forms, with two people or with millions.

II. The state as a Janus face

The state is itself the largest institutionalised application of the political means there is. It enforces taxes. It imposes laws. It has prisons, courts, police, an army. The monopoly on legitimate violence — Weber's definition of the state — is the political means in its purest institutionalised form.

And at the same time it is the only protection against the unbridled use of the political means by others. Without it, the merciless law of the stronger prevails. The inventor without patent protection. The worker without a minimum wage. The debtor without insolvency law. The citizen without habeas corpus. An unrestrained use of the political means that we Central Europeans, after decades of functioning rule of law, can no longer imagine — but which is present reality in large parts of the world.

The state must apply the political means in order to be able to protect against the political means at all.

That is its Janus face — and it is not a defect, it is its essential structure. It cannot protect without exercising power itself. It cannot limit the political means of others without possessing political means itself. The question is not whether the state applies the political means — it always does. The question is whether it uses it to tame the Megamachine or to expand it.

III. The question of balance

Hobbes saw this: the state of nature — the war of all against all — arises when no state monopolises the political means. Life without the state: solitary, poor, brutish, short. The Leviathan — the state — is the lesser evil. It applies the political means in order to prevent everyone else from applying it without restraint.

But the Leviathan can itself become a Megamachine. The state that is supposed to protect its citizens becomes the instrument of their exploitation. This is not theory — it is the most common form in which states have failed throughout history. The protective structure becomes a structure of plunder. The protective tariff becomes highway robbery. The monopoly on violence becomes the instrument of the class that controls the state.

The fundamental political question — which has no final answer — is therefore: how much political means does the state need in order to protect enough without itself becoming an uncontrolled Megamachine? This is a question of balance, not a question of system. There is no stateless solution — for without the state, the political means of the stronger rules unbridled. And there is no state solution that permanently tames the political means of the state itself without external negative feedback: separation of powers, freedom of the press, civil society, elections.

Negative feedback that — as all the other papers in this series show — is systematically weakened by the Megamachine. The circle closes: the state is the instrument for taming the Megamachine. And the Megamachine infiltrates the state in order to turn the instrument of its taming into its own instrument.

IV. Outlook — the asymptotic approach to acracy

At the end of "The State", Oppenheimer described a direction he called acracy — order with as little domination as possible. Not anarchy, not the absence of all order. But the gradual pushing back of the political means in favour of the economic — of voluntary exchange, of cooperation, of mutual recognition.

The goal is asymptotic: it is never fully reached. As long as people live together there are power gradients, there are situations in which one is stronger than the other, there is the impulse to apply the political means. The Megamachine does not disappear. But it can become smaller — through negative feedback that makes the political means more costly, through institutions that make it visible, through structures that strengthen the economic means and limit the political.

Every functioning separation of powers is a step towards acracy. Every independent judiciary. Every free press. Every competition law that limits the concentration of power. Every patent law that actually protects the inventor rather than capital. Every tax that limits inequality as a disturbance variable rather than letting it grow. None of these instruments abolishes the Megamachine — but all of them are movements along the axis away from domination and towards acracy.

This is not an optimistic programme. It is a realistic one. The direction is clear. The road is long. And the Megamachine works against it — so far emergently systematic: without a plan, without a subject, without consciousness, but nonetheless effective through its structures and incentives. That could change. The fusion of the Megamachine and AI could turn the emergently systematic into the intelligently systematic — a system that knows the direction, deliberately identifies and eliminates negative feedback, and works against acracy not through inertia or chance but through intentionality. That would be a qualitative change in the problem — no longer system logic that slows things down, but system intelligence that fights.
