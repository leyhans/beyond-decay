Trickle-Down — A Theory That Worked

Not for those to whom it was held out. For those who designed it.

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo  ·  beyond-decay.org/claude/  ·  April 2026

There are theories that fail in the face of reality. And there are theories that do exactly what they were always meant to do — only not for those who were meant to believe in them. Trickle-down is not a failed theory. It is an extraordinarily successful one — if you know for whom.

I. The Claim

The core thesis runs like this: if you relieve the rich — through tax cuts, deregulation, liberalisation of the capital markets — the resulting prosperity trickles down. Investment follows, jobs follow, the growth cake gets bigger, and everyone gets a bigger slice. The invisible hand distributes. The state is to keep out of it.

The concept has many names: supply-side economics, Reaganomics, Thatcherism. In Germany it was called Standortpolitik (policy for Germany as a business location), flexibilisation, relief for the "high performers". The language varied. The structure remained the same: what benefits the economic elite benefits everyone. Whatever frees capital from taxes and regulation reaches those at the bottom.

Since the early 1980s this thesis has been the foundation of economic policy in most Western democracies. Four decades. And the evidence is unambiguous.

II. What the Evidence Shows

In every Western country that has applied trickle-down policy, inequality has risen since 1980 — not slightly, but dramatically. The richest one per cent of the US population today owns more wealth than the bottom ninety per cent combined. In Germany, wealth inequality is the highest in the eurozone — despite, or because of, four decades of Standortpolitik.

The trickle did not trickle. The gains stayed at the top. The productivity gains of the last forty years have flowed almost entirely to the top income bracket — in the USA, wages and productivity decoupled in the early 1970s, and the gap has been widening ever since. This is no marginal phenomenon. It is the result of the very policy that had promised to prevent exactly this.

And the economic sages — the academic economists, the councils of economic experts, the institutions that gave trickle-down its theoretical underpinning and its political legitimacy — today keep a discreet silence. Not because they were refuted. Who was supposed to refute them — each other? The facts refuted them. But something so banal triggers no learning processes in the Megamachine. One changes the vocabulary and carries on — and goes on selling theoretical constructs that will in all probability one day be refuted by the facts as well.

III. The Decisive Question

Were these economists stupid — or corrupt?

Neither. The question is wrongly posed. What they were: system-conformist and interest-driven.

System-conformist: they thought within the categories of the system they were analysing. The premises — markets are efficient, capital flows to productive uses, price signals correct misallocations — were not questioned but taken as starting points. Within these premises, trickle-down is not stupid. It is coherent. The problem is that the premises are wrong. But whoever does not question the premises cannot learn from the results — he explains them away.

Interest-driven: the careers of these economists, their research funding, their invitations to conferences, their consulting work — all of this takes place in institutions controlled by those who profit from trickle-down. That is not bribery. It is a structural embedding that makes certain questions invisible. Whoever becomes too curious about the wrong subject loses invitations, fellowships, research grants. Not by decree. By the logic of the system.

This is the Megamachine at work: it needs no corruption. It needs only structures that make certain careers possible and others not.

IV. For Whom It Worked

Trickle-down has not failed. Over forty years it has carried out the largest wealth-transfer programme of the modern era — legally, with democratic legitimacy, recommended by experts, endorsed by voters. From the bottom to the top. With the promise that it was going the other way.

That is the punchline you will not find in public discourse: the theory delivered exactly what it was supposed to deliver — for those who designed it, financed it and wrote it into the political programmes. Tax cuts for high incomes and large fortunes. Deregulation of the financial markets. Weakening of the trade unions. Privatisation of public goods. Each of these measures — pushed through in the name of the common good — relieved capital and burdened labour.

The result was not a failure. It was a success — for the right people.

V. The Toddle

German has the word Trippeln — to toddle, to patter along in small, unsteady, hesitant steps. That is what actually arrived of the promised trickle-down: not a stream flowing downwards, but an occasional toddle. Enough to keep the thesis alive. Not enough to change the conditions.

There was economic growth. There were jobs. There were gains in prosperity — in absolute terms, for many. But the distribution of these gains did not follow the promised logic. Those at the top stayed at the top and grew richer. Those at the bottom stayed relatively at the bottom — even if they were somewhat better off in absolute terms. The gap widened. The post-war society's promise of the social lift — whoever works and learns gets on — was systematically undermined.

The toddle was enough as proof. The economic sages supplied the explanations why it was not more. Markets need time. Globalisation creates adjustment costs. Technological change alters skill requirements. All correct. All accurate. And all formulated in such a way that the real question — whom does this structure benefit? — was never asked.

VI. Why the System Does Not Learn

Trickle-down is officially discredited. No serious economist defends it today under that name. Even the IMF, in a widely noted 2015 study, found that rising inequality slows economic growth — the exact opposite of the core thesis.

And yet: the structure remains. Wealth taxes are not introduced. Inheritance taxes stay low or are cut. Capital income is taxed at lower rates than earned income. Lobbyists help write tax laws. The revolving door between politics and business keeps turning. The name of the theory was changed. The mechanisms were not.

That is the hallmark of the Megamachine: it does not learn, because learning would mean turning against the interests that keep it running. A system that profits from the concentration of capital cannot pursue a policy that reverses this concentration — regardless of what the evidence shows. It changes the vocabulary. It invents new concepts. It employs new economists who supply new justifications. But it does not change the basic structure.

Trickle-down was not a lie that someone invented. It was a story that a system had to tell about itself — in order to present what it was doing as what it had promised everyone else.

VII. The Consequence for Germany

Germany never propagated trickle-down as openly as Reagan or Thatcher. The German vocabulary was more restrained — Standortpolitik, flexibilisation, cutting red tape. But the substance was the same: relief for capital and corporate profits, with the promise that this would benefit the economy as a whole.

Agenda 2010 is the clearest example. Hartz IV pushed wages down — structurally, through the pressure the low-wage sector exerted on all wage negotiations. Corporate tax reforms lowered the tax rate on capital income. The wealth tax was abolished in 1997 and never reintroduced. The result: within the eurozone, Germany is the country with the greatest wealth inequality.

And the economic sages? The German Council of Economic Experts (Sachverständigenrat) backed Agenda 2010 by a majority. The reforms were described as a necessary adjustment to globalisation — which in part they were. But the question of who bears the costs of the adjustment and who pockets the gains was not asked with the same precision as the question of how to raise employment.

Structural Diagnosis

Trickle-down did not fail. It worked — only not for those to whom it was held out. The theory was the vehicle. The goal was redistribution from the bottom to the top under the cloak of the common good.

The economic sages who propagated it were neither stupid nor venal in the vulgar sense. They were system-conformist — trapped in the premises of the system they were analysing. And interest-driven — embedded in structures that made certain questions invisible.

That was not stupid and corrupt. That was system-conformist and guided by particular interests — the more elegant description of the same phenomenon.
