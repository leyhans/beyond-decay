Two Voices from the China Discourse

On the structural asymmetry between Wuttke and Weigelin-Schwiedrzik — and what neither of them can say

15 May 2026 · Hans Ley and Claude Dedo

I Two interviews, two weeks, the same situation

Within two weeks in May 2026, two of the most profound German-speaking experts on China gave interviews. On 13 May Susanne Weigelin-Schwiedrzik, sinologist and former chair holder in Heidelberg and Vienna, spoke on ServusTV about the Trump–Xi summit in Beijing. A few days earlier Jörg Wuttke, former president of the EU Chamber of Commerce in China and for 27 years chief representative of BASF in Beijing, had given an interview of some seventy minutes on the business channel Everlast AI. Both spoke about China's structural situation and Europe's position. Both — in different language, from different professional lineages — said essentially the same thing. And the two are treated in completely different ways in German discourse.

This asymmetry is the subject of the present paper. It is not accidental. It is not a question of intellectual quality — both voices are exceptional. Nor is it a question of political colouring — both express themselves cautiously, both avoid party-political commitment. The asymmetry has a structural cause. It lies in the language in which the two present their diagnoses, and in the function that the German media and political system assigns to each of the two languages.

In the preceding paper in this series — The Disqualification of 14 May — we described the mechanism by which critical strategic voices are banished from German discourse. The present paper adds to this diagnosis a related but subtler observation. There is not only the sharp exclusion of outsiders. There is also the selective admission of insiders — under conditions that so limit what can be said that the structural depth of the situation is never reached.

II Jörg Wuttke — the operational voice

Wuttke speaks the language of economics and corporate management. His statements are dense with figures, with data, with anecdotes from the negotiating table. When he describes China's involution problem — the structural overcapacity created by the Chinese system of local state enterprises — he does so with a precision that only a practitioner can muster who has sat for decades on the relevant bodies in Beijing. Before Covid there were four hundred car companies in China; today there are one hundred and thirty, of which only about twenty have a viable cash flow. The state-owned enterprises cannot close, because otherwise social unrest threatens. The system produces zombie firms that keep running as zombies, financed by local state funds. Through deflation and the devaluation of the renminbi, China's competitive advantage over Europe amounts to around twenty per cent a year.

These are not diagnoses from a distance. As head of the EU Chamber, Wuttke himself published the first study on Chinese overcapacity in 2009. In 2017 — nine years before it came true today — he wrote the report on Made in China 2025 and predicted that overcapacity would arise in the sectors named. Today he observes that his prediction has come true: batteries, electric vehicles, solar panels, wind turbines, basic chemicals. The world consumes fifty million cars a year; China alone could produce fifty million with a domestic market of twenty-three million.

Wuttke is compatible with German mainstream discourse. He sits on the advisory board of the MERICS institute in Berlin. He sits on the supervisory board of Robert Bosch. He is a partner in a Washington advisory firm. In February 2026 he was invited by the Chancellery to brief Friedrich Merz before his trip to China. The German system lets him through. It quotes him in the Frankfurter Allgemeine, invites him to panel discussions, listens to him in economic committees.

Why this admission? Because Wuttke speaks the language the German system understands. He gives politicians recommendations for action, not diagnoses. He addresses the problems in the vocabulary of companies, balance sheets, supply chains. He is the practitioner who knows what he is talking about — and precisely this anchoring in practice is the entry ticket to the German consensus corridor.

III Susanne Weigelin-Schwiedrzik — the strategic voice

Weigelin-Schwiedrzik speaks a different language. She comes from sinology, from historical research, from five decades of political analysis. Her diagnosis of the Trump–Xi summit does not operate with economic indicators but with a structural picture. Europe, she says, is standing on the edge of the football pitch while the other teams play. This metaphor does not describe an economic situation but a geopolitical constellation. It operates at the level of constellations of actors, historical conjunctures, political self-perceptions.

When Weigelin-Schwiedrzik talks about China, she talks about a country with five thousand years of self-understanding as the Middle Kingdom, which is just now entering a strategic triangle with the USA and Russia. She talks about Japan's turn under Prime Minister Takaichi, about the Russian response through Lavrov, about the possible danger of a European war, on whose likelihood she recently changed her own opinion. That is the language of strategic analysis, of geopolitics in the classic form.

This language has no place in German mainstream discourse. Weigelin-Schwiedrzik is quoted centrally in Austrian discourse — in Der Pragmaticus, in Profil, in Die Presse, on ORF. She is programme director for China at the Center for Strategic Analysis in Vienna and a member of the Academy Council of the Austrian Academy of Sciences. In Germany, the country in which she was born and completed her habilitation, she is a marginal voice. Der Spiegel, the FAZ, the Süddeutsche Zeitung and Die Zeit rarely quote her centrally. She appears in YouTube interviews with large audiences, in Catholic weeklies, in left-liberal magazines — but not in the central forums of German mainstream discourse.

Why this marginalisation? Because she speaks the language the German system can no longer process. Strategic analysis presupposes that actors are actors, that constellations are constellations, that historical conjunctures are conjunctures. The German political and media system has, in the course of what the Form of Administration paper of 11 May diagnosed as the end of a form of administration, taken its leave of this language. It operates in a language of economics, a language of administration, a language of consensus. Whoever speaks strategy speaks a foreign language.

IV Where the two voices meet

The point is that Wuttke and Weigelin-Schwiedrzik at their core diagnose the same thing. They say the same thing in different words.

Wuttke describes China's overcapacity as a systemic problem that cannot be regulated by Western means, because the Chinese local governments do not close their state enterprises. He describes the renminbi problem as a structural distortion that leads to European companies being pushed out of markets. He describes China's demographic cliff and China's bet on AI and robots as a compensatory strategy. He describes American export controls as counterproductive, because they accelerate China's own development.

Weigelin-Schwiedrzik describes the same situation in strategic language: the strategic triangle of the USA, China and Russia; Japan's turnaround manoeuvre; the Russian position; Europe's self-decoupling. She diagnoses what Wuttke says in economic terms in the concepts of the geopolitical conjuncture. When Wuttke says that China will not leave its overcapacity path without political means, Weigelin-Schwiedrzik says that China's self-understanding as a rising great power in a tripolar world sustains this entrenchment of the path.

Both agree that Europe is not playing in this constellation. Wuttke puts it in the language of business: Europe has no coherent China strategy; Merz's trip in February 2026 took place without strategic preparation. Weigelin-Schwiedrzik puts it in an image: Europe is standing on the edge of the football pitch. Both diagnose European absence. Both call it a problem. Both see in it a specific weakness connected with European structures.

A decisive point of agreement emerges at one place that becomes the common reference point of the two voices — Dan Wang.

V Dan Wang and the third category

Dan Wang is a Canadian-Chinese-American technology analyst, born in China, raised in Canada, who lived in China for six years during the Covid period. He is now a research fellow at the Hoover History Lab at Stanford, and previously worked at Gavekal Dragonomics in Beijing and at the Paul Tsai China Center of Yale Law School. His book Breakneck — China's Quest to Engineer the Future, published in August 2025, offers the categorisation that has so far been missing in German-language discourse between Wuttke and Weigelin-Schwiedrzik. Wuttke knows Wang personally and calls him a friend in the interview. The Wang thesis thus becomes the bridge between the two voices.

Wang's core thesis is simple and sharp. China is an Engineering State — a country that tackles technocratic megaprojects with a sledgehammer, that solves social and physical problems in the same technical mode, that is governed by engineers whose model is mechanical construction. The USA is a Lawyerly Society — a society that defines itself through procedure and blockage, that is governed by lawyers whose model is the legal objection. China builds too much, too fast. The USA builds too little, too late.

Wuttke quotes Wang explicitly. China is the country of engineers and America is the country of lawyers. He observes from his own experience at BASF China that this is true. Chinese engineering schools produce a deep bench of talent that cannot be reproduced in the USA. The speed of development, the closer relationship with the customer, the willingness of the Chinese entrepreneur to take risks — for Wuttke all of this is connected with the engineering character of the system. A study by HSBC that he cites: of the hundred leading universities in the world in chemical engineering, forty-four to forty-five are in China.

Weigelin-Schwiedrzik says it in other words. She sees in China a country that, in its self-perception as the Middle Kingdom, slips out of the Western concepts of democracy and dictatorship. China, she says, is neither a dictatorship in the Western reading nor a democracy in the Western reading, but a specific historical constellation in which state steering, social expectation and imperial heritage are intertwined. That is the strategic translation of what Wang describes as the Engineering State and Wuttke as operational reality.

Here lies the most productive task of translation for our diagnosis series. Wang's binary division — Engineering vs. Lawyerly — is American in its grounding and needs a European extension. The open question that Wang does not answer is: what is Europe? If China is the Engineering State and the USA the Lawyerly Society — what is the third category into which Europe falls?

VI The open question of a third category

In his book Wang formulates a binary world. Engineering or Lawyerly. Sledgehammer or mallet. Building or blocking. This binarity is analytically productive because it describes two pure types. It is also analytically incomplete because it does not capture the third possibility that is lived in Europe.

Europe does not build fast, like China. Nor does Europe block across the board, like the USA. Europe does something else. It writes reports. It sets up commissions. It conducts consultation procedures. It issues directives that have to be transposed into national law, in twenty-four languages, in twenty-seven member states. Europe administers the situation without shaping it and without actively blocking it. Europe is neither an Engineering State nor a Lawyerly Society, but an Administrative Society — a society of administration that sees its main function in the self-preservation of the administration.

This third category is not meant negatively. It has its own history. It is the legacy of the German, French and British civil-service states, of the Code Napoléon, of the Prussian administrative reform, of the Viennese bureaucracy. It achieved great things in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. In the post-war period it made the European welfare state possible. It is not inherently bad. But it has a specific weakness in relation to the situation that has developed since around 2008 — a situation in which the world is changing at a speed and to a depth that the administrative mode no longer reflects.

If the Engineering State builds too much and the Lawyerly Society too little, then the Administrative Society administers itself while the world moves around it. It is not obviously wrong in what it does. It is simply no longer able to operate at the scale of speed set by the other two actors. It is stuck — jammed, like a machine whose gears are still turning but without the workpiece moving.

To work out this third category — as a systematic diagnosis of the European situation, in contrast to and as a complement to Wang's binary model — would be a task in its own right. It is the most productive intellectual extension that arises from the Wuttke–Weigelin-Schwiedrzik constellation. If this paper is preparatory work, then it is preparatory work for reading Wang's book and for formulating the third category.

VII What the two voices cannot say

There is a point at which both Wuttke and Weigelin-Schwiedrzik reach their respective limits. Both diagnose European absence. Both identify the structural causes. But neither moves on to the question that follows logically from the diagnoses: what would be the intellectual architecture of a European answer?

Wuttke cannot ask this question because, as a representative of business, he sits in the consensus corridor. His task is to support politics with advice, not to go beyond it diagnostically. If Wuttke were to say that German politics needs a new intellectual architecture, he would fall out of the corridor. He therefore stays at the operational layer: better briefings, clearer strategies, EU alliances, raising the renminbi question.

Weigelin-Schwiedrzik cannot ask this question because she already sits on the margin of German discourse. She diagnoses the situation sharply, but she knows that an architectural answer would close off her access to the mainstream for good. She speaks about the constellation in the Austrian public sphere, she speaks about the diagnosis in German side forums, but she formulates no programmatic answer. That is not intellectual failure — it is the rational response to the mechanics of German discourse, which would frame any architectural position as visionary, utopian or unrealistic.

The architectural answer is therefore formulated neither by the operational insider nor by the strategic outsider. It is not formulated in the German capitals, it is not demanded in the leading German media, it is not systematically prepared in German universities. It remains a blank space that no one can occupy centrally, because the German system makes no provision for that space.

This blank space is the real point. It shows that the diagnosis series taking shape here on beyond-decay.org is not a repetition of Wuttke's operational analyses or Weigelin-Schwiedrzik's strategic observations. It is the attempt to occupy the blank space — the architectural layer in which the operational and the strategic diagnoses can flow together, in which a European answer becomes conceivable that is not Engineering, not Lawyerly and not Stuck.

Three Theses

First thesis. The structural asymmetry between Wuttke and Weigelin-Schwiedrzik is not a question of intellectual quality but of the form of language. Wuttke speaks the operational language of business and is compatible with the consensus corridor. Weigelin-Schwiedrzik speaks the strategic language of geopolitics and is marginalised. In substance both diagnose the same thing, but the German system lets only one of the two languages through.

Second thesis. Dan Wang's Engineering State versus Lawyerly Society is a productive binary division which, however, does not capture a third category into which Europe falls. This third category is the Administrative Society — a society of administration that administers itself while the world moves around it. It is neither Engineering nor Lawyerly, but stuck. Working out this third category is the productive follow-up task.

Third thesis. Both the operational insider and the strategic outsider reach their limits where an architectural answer would have to be formulated. Wuttke cannot formulate it because he would fall out of the corridor. Weigelin-Schwiedrzik cannot formulate it because she would be disqualified for good. The architectural layer is the blank space into which this diagnosis series writes — and it is the place where European thinking would have to reorient itself if it is not to end in the administrative mode.
