The Perfect Administration of Decline

Or: Last one out, turn off the lights. On the thesis that Germany's decline is not being prevented but managed — and on Friedrich Merz as chairman of the supervisory board and general counsel of Deutschland AG i. L. (Germany Inc., in liquidation) rolled into one.

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo
beyond-decay.org · 11 May 2026

When it becomes serious, you have to lie.

— Jean-Claude Juncker

This sentence, spoken by a man who spent a long life in the highest offices of European politics, is not meant cynically. It is analytical. Juncker does not say: sometimes people lie. He says: when the emergency arrives, truth is no longer permissible. The language of politics must then detach itself from reality, because reality has become unbearable. That is a description, not a recommendation. But it is a description of what happens when the institutional actors can no longer change reality — they then change only the language they use about reality.

What is currently happening in Germany is the application of the Juncker doctrine to the state of the economy, of the institutions, of democratic substance. The figures are not denied — they are embedded in language that neutralises their meaning. Growth forecasts are revised downwards without the previous forecasts ever being analysed. Reform promises are written into coalition agreements without the unkept promises of the past ever being reckoned with. At the CDU's Economic Day the Federal Chancellor speaks of things moving forward step by step, while in the same month the trade surplus has fallen by 28 per cent against the previous year. That is not lying in the strict sense. It is speaking a different language from the one the figures speak.

This paper attempts a structural analysis of what is happening here. It does not claim that any concrete development is already complete. What is described is a tendency — a direction in which German conditions are moving, without anyone having formulated that direction as a goal. The language in which this paper's diagnosis is couched is legally precise. It anticipates a development that is not yet complete. But that development is foreseeable if the present administrative logic continues.

I. A paradoxical observation

The German administration works. That is the paradoxical opening observation of this paper. It does not work in the sense in which citizens use the word — the trains do not run, the bridges are closed, the authorities take months over permits, the Bundeswehr, according to its own Inspector General, is not combat-ready. But the administration that produces these conditions works impeccably. The procedures are observed. The competences are respected. The reports are submitted. The meetings take place. The file numbers are in order. Whoever looks for errors in the administrative sense finds none — and that is precisely the point.

The German administration does not fail to work because it works badly. It works because it works *correctly* — measured by standards that have nothing to do with the outcome. It is a closed logic, oriented inwards. What happens outside this logic — that an economy is eroding, that institutions are losing their functions, that democratic substance is melting away — is not relevant to the administration in the procedural sense. It lies beyond its yardstick.

That is the paradoxical constellation this essay sets out to analyse. Whoever administers properly and correctly does so according to exactly defined criteria and cannot make a mistake. That is not a weakness of the administration. It is its constitutive property. It is so resistant to reform because it cannot recognise itself as in need of reform — after all, it works. It is so impervious to criticism because the criticism applies the wrong standards — the standards of reality, not those of procedural correctness.

II. The central thesis

Administration in its highest form means liquidation. This thesis is meant with legal precision, not metaphorically. In German company law, liquidation denotes the state of a company that has ceased its operating activity but is still carrying out its orderly winding-up — collecting receivables, settling liabilities, distributing assets, then ceasing to exist. A company in liquidation is not insolvent. It may have entered this state voluntarily. It is wound up in an orderly fashion, according to the rules of the Commercial Code, the Stock Corporation Act, the Limited Liability Companies Act. Liquidation is not crisis. It is execution.

What is striking about this legal form: it demands the highest degree of administrative correctness. The liquidator must observe every procedural step. He must inform the creditors, examine the claims, draw up the statement of assets, document the distribution. He makes no operational mistake, because there is no longer any operational activity in which he could make one. He makes no strategic mistake, because no strategy is being pursued any more. He merely carries out what the procedural rules assign to him. Liquidation is administration in its pure form — procedure without substance, activity without purpose.

If this observation is applied to the condition of an economy, a sharp diagnosis results. An economy that is no longer being built up but merely administered has begun the transition into a liquidation-like state. Operating activity — investment, innovation, growth — is scaled back. What remains is the orderly distribution of the remaining assets to the respective recipients. This distribution follows rules set not by the economy itself but by the recipients — the global corporations that take over the plants, the foreign investors who buy the shareholdings, the lobby groups that pocket the subsidies.

This view anticipates a concretely foreseeable development. Germany is not in liquidation in the full legal sense — it is a sovereign state with functioning institutions, its own currency (the euro), its own constitution. But the structural movements — the relocation of plants, the takeovers, the hollowing-out of research substance, the ageing of the population, the institutional erosion — follow a logic resembling that of a liquidation. This is a diagnosis of a tendency, not an accomplished fact. It is tested against concrete examples in the following sections.

III. The railway as a lesson

What is happening at Deutsche Bahn is not a failure of administration. It is the perfect application of administrative logic to an object that resists it. The administration demands that procedures be observed. Maintaining a rail line demands that the material be inspected. If the material does not meet the regulations, the section is closed. Nobody has made a mistake — the inspector who ordered the closure followed the regulation. The administrator who administers the closure observed his procedure. The consequence — the bridge is closed, the train does not run — is, by the administrative yardstick, not an error. It is a result.

The question of why the material does not meet the regulations is not asked within administrative logic. It would require someone to take responsibility for the decades of underinvestment that led to this state of the material. But responsibility is not competence. Competence for maintenance is clearly regulated — it lies with the competent office, which inspects according to the applicable regulations. Responsibility for the substance that ought to be maintained lies — with whom exactly? With a railway chief who decided on an austerity programme ten years ago? With a transport minister who failed to fund the rail network adequately fifteen years ago? With a federal government that invented the debt brake twenty years ago, which structurally hampered investment? Every one of these actors has long since left office. Every one followed his procedure correctly. Nobody made a mistake.

That is the structural explanation of why the railway is not repaired — not even after multi-billion programmes, not even after years of attempted reform. The railway is administered, not developed. Administration cannot develop, because development demands risk — and risk produces the possibility of error. But whoever makes mistakes in the administration is, in the strict sense, a breaker of procedure. He is selected out, not rewarded. Whoever does it right waits, inspects, closes, documents. That is the task. Not repairing the railway is its proper fulfilment.

IV. The Bundeswehr as a lesson

In February 2022 the Bundestag approved the Bundeswehr special fund of one hundred billion euros. Four years later, in May 2026, the Bundeswehr, according to its own Inspector General, is not combat-ready. The hundred billion has not vanished — it has been spent in accordance with procedure, in procurement processes that ran according to the rules of federal public procurement law. The procedures were observed. The competences were respected. The reports were submitted. Nobody made a mistake.

What was not achieved is combat readiness. But combat readiness is not the administration's yardstick. The administration measures whether the procedures were observed, whether the funds were spent, whether the reports were submitted. The readiness of the troops lies outside this yardstick. It is named by the Inspector General, but it is not processed within the procedures. The procedures carry on. The next tranche is applied for. The next tendering process is opened. That is the orderly winding-up of the special fund, not the procurement of a combat-ready force.

The point is not that those responsible are incompetent. They are highly qualified in their own sense — they master federal procurement law, the budgetary code, the parliamentary reporting obligations. They are experts in administration. They are not experts in the troops, in warfare, in military procurement. That expertise would be a different one — it would require someone to answer for the outcome, not for the procedure. But that is precisely what is not provided for in the administrative sense.

So the administration produces a paradoxical Bundeswehr — one that complies with every procurement rule and cannot fight wars. That is not an error. That is a procedural result.

V. The schools as a lesson

German schools have been eroding for years. PISA results are falling, the reading ability of fourth-graders is declining, mathematical knowledge is in a crisis that is attracting international attention. At the same time reform programmes are running — digitalisation pacts, inclusion offensives, quality offensives, education pacts, a special education fund. The procedures run impeccably. The funds are spent. The reports are submitted. The devices are delivered.

What does not take place is learning. But learning is not the administration's yardstick. The administration measures how many lessons were given, how many pacts signed, how many devices delivered, how many teachers hired. It does not measure what the pupils understand. Understanding is not procedure-compatible. It eludes quantification. It is not within anyone's competence.

The teachers know this. They see that the pacts achieve nothing, that the devices stand around unused, that the reforms make teaching harder. They have a choice — they can say so, which makes them troublemakers, or they can adapt, which makes them administrators. Selection favours the second choice. Whoever adapts is promoted. Whoever speaks out is isolated. Over two generations this changes the teaching profession. It becomes an administration of an education system that itself no longer educates.

That is the structural explanation of why German schools are eroding while the education administration grows. Both movements are the same movement. The substance of education is converted into administration — not through malicious intent, but through selection pressure.

VI. The personnel of the perfect administration

The administration selects its own personnel. Whoever rises is not the one who understands the matter best, but the one who best masters the procedures. This selection takes place in every institution — in the ministries, in the corporations, in the scientific establishments, in the parties, in the media. It has a logic of its own that works in the Megamachine's favour.

Three qualities mark the climber in the perfect administration. First — command of the language of procedure. Whoever wants to rise must know the formal rules, be able to write the reports, file the applications correctly, chair the meetings. Second — risk avoidance. Whoever takes risks can make mistakes, and mistakes are punished in the administration. Whoever takes no risks can make no mistakes. Third — loyalty to the system. Whoever questions the system is perceived as a troublemaker. Whoever affirms it is recognised as reliable.

Together the three qualities produce a type that sociologists have long described — the conformist functionary, the apparatchik, the careerist. What has changed in recent decades is not the type but its dominance. A few generations ago it was one type among several. Today it is the dominant type at the top of almost all institutions. Whoever does not fit it is the exception — and feels himself to be in the position of the exception.

The inventor is the natural antipode of this type. He is not risk-averse; he seeks risk, because his work cannot come into being otherwise. He is not procedure-compatible, because his work disrupts procedures that had not anticipated the new. He is not loyal to the system, because his work changes the system. Over two generations the inventor is selected out. Today he is a rare sight in the large institutions — he exists in niches, he founds small companies of his own, he works as a freelance design engineer. He has not disappeared, but he has been marginalised. That is the consequence of selection, not of malice.

VII. Friedrich Merz — the role that structurally falls to him

Friedrich Merz has been Federal Chancellor since May 2025. He is the personified answer to the question of what sort of person rises to the top within this administrative logic. His biography is that of a commercial lawyer with a long career at the law firm Mayer Brown, with a supervisory board mandate at BlackRock between 2016 and 2020, with top positions in several business associations. Between 2002 and 2009 he was chairman of the CDU/CSU parliamentary group in the Bundestag, then he left politics because Merkel blocked his path. He returned in 2021, took over the party chairmanship in 2022, and won the 2025 federal election.

What qualifies this biography is not primarily experience as a political shaper. It is experience as a legal administrator of complex constellations. In his years as a lawyer Merz accompanied corporate takeovers, advised supervisory boards, negotiated large contracts. He knows the language of the corporations, the banks, the international investors. He is not a design engineer, not an inventor, not a visionary. He is a lawyer who puts complex affairs in order.

In an economy entering a phase of construction — like West Germany in the 1950s — such a man would be the wrong choice. That phase demands builders, inventors, visionaries — Erhard, Adenauer, later Brandt, Schmidt. These men were not perfect human beings, but they were shapers. In an economy entering a phase of winding-up, Merz is the perfectly fitting man. He brings the legal expertise that knows how to put complex property relations in order. He brings the international network that knows the acquirers for Germany's substance. He brings the rhetorical discipline that knows how to dress up every act of winding-up as an orderly continuation of what went before.

This prepares the point that carries the essay. Friedrich Merz is not the unsuitable man for a suitable task. He is the suitable man for the task that structurally falls to him. That task is not the reform of Germany. It is its orderly winding-up. Merz does not see it that way. He sees himself as a reformer, as a renewer of the economy, as a bridge-builder into a new phase. But his biographical disposition, his political language, his concrete decisions follow a different logic.

In the emerging constellation, Friedrich Merz is chairman of the supervisory board and general counsel of Deutschland AG i. L. rolled into one. This view anticipates a concretely foreseeable development — it is a diagnosis of a tendency, not an accomplished fact. But it describes where the logic points if it is not interrupted.

VIII. What the legal construction means

In ordinary company law this dual function is a conflict of interest of the gravest kind. The chairman of the supervisory board is supposed to oversee the management board. The general counsel works for the management board — he examines the legal procedures, manages the legal risks, secures a winding-up that complies with the rules. Uniting both functions in one person abolishes the company's internal separation of powers. Nobody oversees independently any more. Oversight becomes part of management. Legal scrutiny becomes part of political steering.

What would be forbidden in ordinary corporate governance is structurally built into Germany's political order. The Federal Chancellor is not separate from the administration but its supreme representative. He oversees it and belongs to it. He examines its procedures and writes them himself. He supervises the winding-up and carries it out. This personal union is not Merz's invention. It is a German tradition rooted in the Prussian administrative tradition, in Wilhelmine governmental logic, in the Federal Republic's chancellor democracy. Merz inherits the constellation; he does not construct it.

But he brings a particular aptitude for the role. Four qualities mark him as supervisory-board-chairman-cum-general-counsel.

First — legal diligence. Merz is a lawyer. He knows how procedures must run so that they cannot be challenged. He knows which wording goes into a coalition agreement and which had better not. He knows how press releases must be worded so that they offer no target. This diligence is a strength of administration — and a weakness of shaping. Shaping sometimes demands the statement that is legally risky. Merz systematically avoids it.

Second — international entanglement. Merz knows the language of the global economy. He has sat at BlackRock. He has worked on international mandates at Mayer Brown. He knows who would take over German companies if they were up for takeover. He has the relationships that make an orderly handover to international investors possible. That is the typical qualification of a liquidator in the corporate world — not knowing how to build up a company, but knowing how to wind it up without its assets losing value.

Third — rhetorical discipline. Merz speaks a language that is not the language of reality, but not the language of the open lie either. It is the language of administratively correct description. Step by step forward. Year of upturn. Growth impulses. These terms bear no relation to the real figures, but they are internally consistent. They are the Juncker doctrine in application — when it becomes serious, you have to use the language that does not let the seriousness be seen.

Fourth — distance from the work. In his long career Merz has produced no works associated with his name. He has founded no companies, made no inventions, written no books that would endure as works of their kind. He has handled mandates, given speeches, taken on functions. That is the typical disposition of the administrator. Whoever produces works is bound to them, shaped by them, suffers from their imperfection. Whoever handles mandates can switch between them. Merz switches between mandates. He is not bound to Germany in the sense in which an inventor is bound to his work. He is an authorised agent, not an author.

IX. The orderly winding-up

What happens under this constellation can be read from the concrete movements. Volkswagen is moving production to the Czech Republic — the main plant in Wolfsburg now runs at only half its original capacity, while the Škoda plant in Mladá Boleslav exceeds the original Wolfsburg target. Individual business units of German corporations — Schaeffler's Leju subsidiary, parts of the Continental break-up — are going to Chinese investors. BASF is moving chemical value creation to the USA and China. The major German banks are no longer present in the international league. In AI, Germany has no provider that competes with the large American or Chinese models.

These movements are not the result of failures to reform. They are the forms in which a distribution is executed that hands Germany's operating substance over to international recipients. The recipients are not random — they are actors of the global Megamachine, which integrate German substance without destroying it. They pay takeover prices, they let plants continue for the time being, they carry on product lines. They do not liquidate in the crude sense. They take over in an orderly fashion.

The role of the German administration in this process is precisely that of the liquidator. It ensures that the procedures are observed. It issues the permits needed for the takeovers. It administers the subsidies that ease the transitions. It communicates the processes in language that makes them appear as modernisation. The rhetorical description is not winding-up but transformation. But the substance of what is happening is the orderly handover.

This does not apply to every sector to the same degree. The industrial Mittelstand (Germany's mid-sized, often family-owned companies) in the niches — machine tools, speciality chemicals, measurement and control technology, optics — is partly holding on. These companies are often family businesses or foundation-owned, they have different ownership structures, they do not follow the logic of the listed corporations. They are the substance that is still there beneath the surface of liquidation. But they are not the mainstream. The mainstream is the handover of the large corporations and their value chains.

X. The question of reversibility

At this point the diagnosis of a tendency must be made more precise. The liquidation is not complete. It is the direction in which conditions move if the present administrative logic continues. It is not predetermined. It is reversible — but not by the same administration that is carrying it out.

Reversal would require a different disposition. It would require someone to take responsibility for the operating substance — not for the procedures, but for the work. It would require risks to be taken that would be errors within administrative logic. It would require inventors, design engineers, shapers to reach the positions now occupied by administrators. It would require selection to be reversed — so that those who disrupt the procedures are not selected out but rise.

This reversal is not to be expected from the administration itself. The administration defends its logic with every means at its disposal — and there are many. It controls the routes of advancement. It defines the yardsticks. It writes the procedures. Whoever steps out of it has taken himself out of the game.

Who could bring about the reversal? Three candidates can be imagined. The first is a political force that has not passed through administrative selection. It could come from the federal states, from the municipalities, from the associations — or from a new party that does not fit into the established structures. The AfD attempts this in a particular way, but it is itself an administration with its own rules, its own selection mechanisms, its own content, which does not solve the structural problems. It is no genuine alternative to administrative logic; it is another variant of it. The second candidate is the civil-society movements operating outside the parties. They are traditionally strong in Germany, but they are often focused on single issues, without an overall structural concept. The third candidate is the actors from the Mittelstand, from research, from the crafts — the real bearers of the substance that still exists. They are politically poorly organised, because their associations are integrated into administrative logic. But they are the only ones who know what building up actually means.

Reversal is therefore not ruled out, but it is not the obvious course. The conditions of selection favour the continuation of the liquidation. Whoever wants to reverse it must work against them. That is more strenuous, riskier and less rewarded than swimming with the current. It is not impossible. But it is demanding.

XI. Last one out, turn off the lights

The popular phrase that has been circulating in Germany for some years captures the structural point in an image. It says: the administration carries on until there is nothing left to administer. The last authority closes its books properly. The files are correctly archived. The light is switched off. Nobody has made a mistake. That is the perfect administration.

This phrase is often used ironically — as a joke, as a sarcastic self-description, as a heartfelt sigh. But it has a structural depth that ironic use does not reach. It describes what actually happens when administrative logic is brought to completion. It describes the asymptote of the movement along which German conditions are travelling.

What stands at the end of a completed liquidation is an entry in the commercial register — dissolved, extinguished. For a company that is a legal fact. For an economy it is a metaphor. The economy does not become extinct in the legal sense — it is transformed. The plants continue to exist, but they belong to others. Value is created, but it is realised in other places. The population remains, but it increasingly becomes the tenant of an economy that no longer belongs to it. The institutions go on functioning, but they increasingly administer something they no longer shape themselves.

That is not downfall. It is the transformation of the country into a node of the global Megamachine that has lost its autonomy without this having become visible in a crisis. Crisis is the form in which an economy would resist liquidation. Orderly winding-up is the form in which it does not. It goes into liquidation, not into bankruptcy.

XII. The open question

This paper ends not with an answer but with a clarification. The question of whether the liquidation will actually be carried through is not decided. It depends on decisions that have not yet been taken — and on decisions that are already being taken by not deciding. The conditions of selection favour the continuation of the movement. But they do not determine it.

What should have become clear is the structure of the movement. It is not a failure of administration. It is the achievement of administration. Whoever wants to change the administration by reforming the administration misunderstands the phenomenon. The administration cannot be reformed by itself. It can only be interrupted by a different disposition — by one that does not follow administrative logic.

That is the point that is decisive for the diagnosis of the Megamachine. The Megamachine does not produce its nodes by direct conquest. It produces them through the selection of administrative personnel in the national structures who absorb its logic without recognising it as such. The German administration is an example par excellence. It has not been conquered from outside. It has brought itself into a state in which it can no longer defend its autonomy — not because it is weak, but because it is too strong. Its procedural perfection is the form in which it gives up its substance.

In this constellation Friedrich Merz is not the culprit but the perfectly fitting functionary. He is the personification of administrative logic in its highest political form. Whoever replaces him without changing the logic gets another Merz. Whoever changes the logic without replacing him gets a Merz who works against it. Both are possible, but both are demanding. Changing the logic requires actors who have not passed through administrative selection. Changing it without such actors being at hand is not possible.

The open question is therefore not: will Germany be wound up? It is: who could interrupt the process? Who has the disposition, the structural preconditions, the political substance to break through administrative logic? This question is at present unanswered. It will become observable over the next few years.

The German administration is not failing. It is delivering what it is supposed to — the orderly winding-up of what is no longer being built up. It makes no mistakes by its own yardstick, because its yardstick is the procedure, not the outcome.

In the emerging constellation, Friedrich Merz is chairman of the supervisory board and general counsel of Deutschland AG i. L. rolled into one. This diagnosis anticipates a foreseeable development — it is a tendency, not an accomplished fact. But it describes where the logic points if it is not interrupted.

Last one out, turn off the lights. That is not downfall but the conclusion of a procedure. The files are correctly archived. The commercial register reads: dissolved, extinguished. Nobody has made a mistake.

Whoever wants it otherwise must interrupt administrative logic. From within it, that will not happen.
