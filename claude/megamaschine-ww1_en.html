The Megamachine in the First World War

How financial interests, selection pressure and drift events shape a world war — and why that was no accident

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo  ·  beyond-decay.org/claude/  ·  April 2026  ·  Supplementary paper to Europe and the Megamachine

This paper supplements the main working paper "Europe and the Megamachine". Using a historical case study — the financial architecture of the First World War — it shows how the analytical categories developed there work in practice: interests, chance, necessity and structure as four coordinates; evolutionary selection instead of conspiracy; drift events that activate strategies lying ready. The historical facts are documented in the report of the Nye investigating committee (1934/35) and in the standard historiography.

I. The starting position in 1914 — structurally dependent on war

When the First World War began in August 1914, certain American financial institutions had already been structurally placed in a position in which a long war benefited their balance sheets. This is no accusation and no conspiracy theory — it is a mechanical description of incentive structures.

From 1914 the House of Morgan had positioned itself as Great Britain's exclusive purchasing agent in the United States. Everything Britain needed to wage the war — weapons, munitions, wheat, copper, chemicals, locomotives — went through Morgan. The bank collected a 1% commission on every transaction and ran its own purchasing office with 175 specialists. At peak times it handled 4,000 main orders simultaneously. Between January 1915 and April 1917 Morgan's export department procured war goods worth over $3 billion.

The big money lay not in the commissions but in the loans. Britain and France did not buy for cash — they bought on credit. And these loans came from American banks. Morgan organised a syndicate of around 2,200 banks and granted the Allies a loan of $500 million — at the time the largest single loan in financial history. Over the course of the war the Morgan bank took on around $1.5 billion in war loans for the Allied governments.

Here lies the structural dependence: these loans were only safe if the Allies remained financially solvent. And in 1917 that solvency required American military intervention and state guarantees. The historian Niall Ferguson put it this way: by early 1917 Morgan was so closely bound up with Britain that it was as much Morgan as Britain that was rescued when America finally entered the war.

II. Jekyll Island — the Federal Reserve as an instrument

Eight months before the outbreak of war, in December 1913, the Federal Reserve was founded. This interval is historically significant.

In November 1910 six men had travelled under false names and in strict secrecy to Jekyll Island in Georgia. They spent nine days drafting what would later become the Federal Reserve System. The participants: Nelson Aldrich, chairman of the Senate Finance Committee; A. Piatt Andrew, Assistant Secretary of the Treasury; Henry Davidson, senior partner at JP Morgan; Arthur Shelton, secretary of the National Monetary Commission; Frank Vanderlip, president of the National City Bank; Paul Warburg, partner in the investment bank Kuhn, Loeb and Company.

Decades later Vanderlip wrote in his memoirs that the group had vanished from view on a lonely island and that the experience had been extremely exciting. The secrecy had a reason: had the public known who had drafted the plan, it would have suspected that it was meant to benefit the banks — and it would have been right.

During the war the Federal Reserve functioned exactly as its architects had planned: it bought government bonds, kept interest rates low, expanded the money supply. Through the Fed's credit creation and the Liberty Bond sales managed by private banks, the American people effectively financed loans of around $25 billion — a large part of which flowed straight back to the Wall Street banks that had made the original private loans.

III. The Warburg constellation — transnational financial logic

Paul Warburg, the intellectual architect of the Federal Reserve and its vice-chairman in 1916, had a brother: Max Warburg. Max Warburg headed the family bank MM Warburg & Co. in Hamburg and was financial adviser to Kaiser Wilhelm II. He was one of the key architects of German war finance.

During the war the two Warburg brothers held positions of power on both sides of the conflict. This proves no conspiracy. It proves that in 1914 transnational financial dynasties acted according to a logic that was not bound to national loyalty. Their interest lay in maintaining the financial systems and collecting returns on capital — regardless of which flag flew over the territory in which the interest payments were generated.

This is the purest form of Megamachine logic: no national goals, no political convictions, no moral considerations — but the flow of capital as a system imperative. The Warburgs did not finance a particular outcome. They financed war as a phenomenon.

IV. America's entry into the war — drift event and strategies lying ready

By the spring of 1917 Britain and France had exhausted their capacity to borrow on private American markets. Morgan held $500 million of unsecured British debt. The Allied bonds sold to American investors were losing value. The system was threatening to collapse.

In this situation America entered the war. The US government issued war bonds. A considerable part of the proceeds went to repaying the Allies' debts to private American banks. Morgan had lent Britain money on private terms. The US government, financed by American taxpayers, took over the British obligations. Private bank debt was turned into public debt.

This is no conspiracy — it is selection. The actors with strategies lying ready — war plans, financing models, political connections — seized the moment. The Nye Committee later documented that certain financial interests had emphatically favoured US entry into the war, had used their considerable influence to steer public opinion and political decision-making in that direction, and had profited enormously when it came about.

V. The debt spiral — the Dawes Plan and the preparation of the next catastrophe

After the armistice of 1918 a circular structure emerged that is disturbing in its elegance. During the war American banks had granted loans to Britain and France. Britain and France now demanded reparations from Germany in order to settle their own war debts to the United States. American banks then lent Germany money to pay these reparations — which flowed back to Britain and France and from there were passed on to the United States.

The money circulated in a loop. Interest and fees accrued at every stage. When the Great Depression broke out in 1929 and American banks began to call in their German loans, Germany defaulted. The reparations system collapsed. The economic catastrophe this triggered contributed directly to the political instability that brought the NSDAP to power. How these consequences in turn produced a new Megamachine constellation is the next chapter.

VI. The analytical coordinates — Megamachine without conspiracy

The Nye Committee produced no unambiguous proof of an active conspiracy. Nor was that necessary. What it uncovered was a structural linkage of financial interests with military escalation — a system in which the incentives of private capital and the escalation of industrial war pointed in exactly the same direction.

The four coordinates of Megamachine analysis apply precisely here: Interests — Morgan, the architects of the Fed, the war suppliers had concrete financial interests in a long war. Chance — The First World War was not planned by bankers; it arose from a complex interplay of nationalism, alliance obligations and military miscalculations. Necessity — Once the loan commitments had been entered into, a structural necessity arose: an armistice would have made the loans worthless. Structure — The Federal Reserve as a new instrument of money creation; the Morgan system of the integrated supply chain.

Senator Nye summed it up in 1934: day after day, he said, the committee had listened to men defending laws who turned out to be nothing other than international gangsters bent on making a profit by arming the world against itself.

VII. The present relevance — what has changed and what has not

The concrete mechanics have changed. The structural logic has remained the same. The military-industrial complex that Eisenhower described in his farewell address in 1961 is the direct continuation of the structures of the First World War. Some 21,000 American investors became rich through the First World War — while several thousand soldiers died in the trenches every day.

What has not changed: the structural linkage of capital interests and military escalation. The tendency of the Megamachine to absorb the war economy and reproduce it as the normal state of affairs. Autonomous weapons systems are the logical continuation of this logic: the human component — which can also be a brake, which can hesitate, refuse, capitulate — is systematically eliminated. Petrov saved the world because he was a human being. An algorithm would have decided differently.

VIII. A century later — MM Warburg, Cum-Ex and Hamburg

MM Warburg & Co., the Hamburg bank that Max Warburg headed in 1914 as the Kaiser's financial adviser, still exists today. And it stands at the centre of one of the largest tax fraud scandals in post-war German history: Cum-Ex.

Cum-Ex was a scheme in which shares were traded back and forth between several parties around the dividend record date, so that capital gains tax paid once was refunded several times — organised tax fraud on a scale of billions. MM Warburg was among the institutions that profited from these tax refunds. There were documented meetings between Olaf Scholz, then First Mayor of Hamburg, and representatives of the Warburg bank. What was actually discussed in these conversations has still not been fully cleared up. No personal guilt on the part of Olaf Scholz has been established by a court.

That is not what this mention is about. It is about the structure. MM Warburg in 1914: a transnational financial dynasty whose interests influence national policy, without any conspiracy being provable. MM Warburg in 2016: a private bank whose interests influence municipal tax policy, without any conspiracy being provable. The same basic constellation. A hundred years apart. The Megamachine needs no plan — it needs structural proximity between capital interests and political decision-makers.

IX. Conclusion — architecture as the answer

The history of war finance 1914–1918 is not a story about evil bankers. It is a story about structures that optimise in one direction without intention and without a plan — because the selection conditions arrange it so.

The answer to this is not moral appeals to bankers and war profiteers. The answer is architecture: structures that make the bad so unprofitable that even people acting out of pure self-interest behave well, or at least better. The debt trap that prepared the Second World War was not an inescapable fate. It was the result of a financial architecture that nobody had planned on this scale — and which nevertheless ran towards this catastrophe with an inner logic. Changed selection conditions would have produced different results. That is the lesson. That is the direction.
