The Megamachine in the Third Reich

How capital, state and industry optimised their way into catastrophe without an overall plan

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo  ·  beyond-decay.org/claude/  ·  April 2026  ·  Supplementary paper to Europe and the Megamachine

This paper supplements the main working paper "Europe and the Megamachine" and the supplementary paper on the financial architecture of the First World War. Using the Third Reich as its example, it shows how the Megamachine functions in its most destructive form: as a system that maximises positive feedback and systematically switches off negative feedback — until collapse becomes inevitable.

I. The initial constellation — selection before the seizure of power

Hitler's rise was not the product of planning by big industry. That is the most important finding of historiography, and it is decisive for our analysis — because it shows that the Megamachine needs no one pulling the strings.

Until 1929 the NSDAP financed itself primarily through membership fees, admission charges and small donations. Big industry supported the traditional bourgeois parties. Kirdorf was the exception: in 1927 he became the first major industrialist to join the NSDAP — and left it again as early as 1928, because Gregor Strasser's anti-capitalist workers' faction was too strong. Kirdorf wanted to use the party as an instrument; the instrument slipped out of his grasp.

The first systematic source of funding was not big industry but the Reichswehr. Captain Karl Mayr financed Hitler from a secret army fund as a propaganda agent against communism. The army invested in an instrument for fighting the left — and lost control of it.

What the Great Depression of 1929 changed was not the intentions of the industrialists — it was the conditions of selection. In a deep crisis, a party that wants to destroy the trade unions, fight communism and stabilise the economy becomes attractive. The NSDAP rose from 2.8% (1928) to 37% (1932). Only now did larger sums of industrial money flow — not out of conviction, but because the conditions of selection had changed.

II. The secret meeting of 20 February 1933 — the pact

On 20 February 1933, three weeks after Hitler's appointment as Reich Chancellor, Hermann Göring invited 25 leading industrialists and financiers to his office. Present were Gustav Krupp von Bohlen und Halbach, Fritz von Opel, Georg von Schnitzler (IG Farben), Albert Vögler and Friedrich Flick.

Hitler gave a speech in which he defended private property, announced that the trade unions would be smashed and promised massive rearmament. The industrialists pledged three million Reichsmarks for the forthcoming election campaign. The money flowed via the "Nationale Treuhand" (National Trust) account at Delbrück, Schickler & Co., administered by Hjalmar Schacht, to Rudolf Hess.

This was not a plan to build an apparatus of annihilation. It was an exchange: industry bought protection of private property and access to armaments contracts. Hitler bought financial legitimacy and access to the networks of the establishment. Each side believed it was using the other. Both were mistaken.

This is the purest form of the Megamachine mechanism: two interest groups conclude a pact whose consequences neither of them fully grasps — and which sets in motion a system that overwhelms them both.

III. The MEFO bills — the financial architecture of rearmament

Hjalmar Schacht, former President of the Reichsbank and architect of German war financing, developed in 1934 an instrument of technical brilliance and moral catastrophe: the MEFO bills.

The Metallurgische Forschungsgesellschaft (Metallurgical Research Company) was a shell company. The Reich awarded armaments contracts against bills of exchange drawn on this sham company, which formally counted as short-term trade bills but could in fact be extended for up to five years. The debts did not appear in the Reich budget. Germany rearmed in secret — financed by a system that concealed its own balance sheet.

Between 1934 and 1938, MEFO bills worth some 12 billion Reichsmarks were issued. The start-up capital was provided by the four largest arms manufacturers, Krupp among them. Industry pre-financed its own orders — and earned at every step.

In 1937 Schacht recognised that the system was leading into a debt spiral that could only be covered by territorial expansion. He resigned. Göring took over the Four-Year Plan. The positive feedback was amplified further: rearmament generated growth, growth legitimised rearmament, rearmament compelled expansion.

IV. IG Farben — the Megamachine as a corporation

No company embodied the logic of the Megamachine in the Third Reich more precisely than IG Farben. Formed in 1925 by the merger of six leading chemical companies, by 1942 it was the third-largest company in the world.

The connection with the Nazis began with blackmail: IG Farben produced a carcinogenic food colouring and feared parliamentary consequences. It approached the NSDAP with the offer to cover this up — in return for money. That was the first transaction. After that, the connection was structural.

At the secret meeting of 20 February 1933, IG Farben was represented by Georg von Schnitzler. Afterwards it became one of the main contributors to the Adolf Hitler Fund (Adolf-Hitler-Spende). In return it received state contracts for synthetic fuel and rubber — products indispensable for rearmament.

The consistent further development of this logic: when Poland fell, IG Farben turned to Himmler. They showed him a spot on the map where three rivers met and coal was available. They wanted the world's largest plant for synthetic rubber and synthetic oil. They wanted slave labourers. That was the origin of Auschwitz.

IG Farben did not build Auschwitz out of hatred. It built it out of logic — the logic of a system that optimises every step without seeing the next one. That is the Megamachine in its most extreme form.

V. The American dimension — transnational structural logic

When Hitler came to power in 1933, US companies had already invested heavily in the German market. General Motors owned Opel, Ford operated plants in Cologne. IBM had taken over the German company Dehomag in 1923.

These companies became part of the Nazi war economy — not primarily out of ideological conviction, but through structural integration. Opel produced Blitz lorries for the Wehrmacht. The IBM subsidiary Dehomag supplied the tabulating machines for the census — and later for the logistics of the extermination camps.

This is the same transnational structural logic as with the Warburg brothers in 1914: capital interests that cross national borders and produce results that no one had planned on this scale. Standard Oil was still delivering after the war had begun. When the US Senate investigated under Harry S. Truman, he spoke of treason.

VI. Hjalmar Schacht — the architect as witness to himself

Hjalmar Schacht is the person who embodies the logic of the Megamachine in the Third Reich most purely. Schacht was not a convinced National Socialist — he was a Freemason, a cosmopolitan, a democrat of the early phase of the Weimar Republic. He had Jewish business friends. In 1944, after the attempt on Hitler's life, he was sent to a concentration camp. At Nuremberg he was acquitted. All of this is true.

And yet: without Schacht, no Third Reich in this form. Reich War Minister von Blomberg told him personally at the 1935 Party Rally: "Without you, my dear Dr Schacht, this day would never have been possible." The day was the public proclamation of Germany's rearmament.

Schacht's own explanation for his actions follows a logic we know: he believed he could control Hitler. He believed that, as a moderate technocrat, he could build in a brake. That is exactly the illusion shared by Thyssen, von Papen and Hugenberg. None of them controlled anything. All were overwhelmed or used up, and then eliminated or pushed aside.

Schacht's career follows the logic of selection with almost mechanical precision: in 1918, co-founder of the German Democratic Party. At the end of 1930, the switch to Hitler. In 1937, distancing himself from Göring over power conflicts, not for moral reasons. In 1944, contact with the conspirators of 20 July, as Hitler's defeat loomed. Every step in the direction that the conditions of selection happened to favour at the time.

The Megamachine needs no monsters. It needs clever, flexible, well-connected people who do the right thing at the right moment — for the system.

VII. The missing negative feedback

What distinguishes the Third Reich from all other historical case studies of the Megamachine is the complete elimination of negative feedback. Every brake was systematically removed: the trade unions on 2 May 1933. Parliamentary control through the Enabling Act in March 1933. Schacht as a critical economic voice in 1937. The press brought into line (gleichgeschaltet).

The result was a system that knew only positive feedback: rearmament justified expansion, expansion financed rearmament, successes legitimised the regime, the regime demanded more successes. Hitler formulated the logic himself: he was like a drug addict — he knew he needed more and more.

The historian Hannah Arendt called it the banality of evil. Our analysis adds: it was also the banality of structure. People who did their jobs, accepted orders, managed accounts — in a system that no longer had any brakes.

VIII. Denazification as failed negative feedback

After 1945 the Allies tried to install negative feedback after the fact: the Nuremberg trials, denazification, the trials of industrialists. The result was telling. Friedrich Flick received seven years in prison. By 1950 he was a free man, got his companies back, became the largest shareholder in Daimler-Benz and left behind, in 1972, an empire of 330 companies. The IG Farben executives were sentenced to prison terms of between 18 months and 8 years — for complicity in Auschwitz. Then they were pardoned and returned to the German business world.

After the war, Ford and General Motors demanded compensation from the US government for war damage to their German factories — in which they had employed slave labourers. General Motors received 30 million dollars. That is not a punchline. That is Megamachine logic.

IX. Conclusion — what the Third Reich teaches about the Megamachine

The Third Reich is no proof that capitalism necessarily leads to fascism. Nor is it proof that human beings are evil by nature. It is the most extreme historical evidence for a precise thesis: a system that maximises positive feedback loops and systematically switches off negative feedback optimises without steering — and becomes self-destructive.

The actors were not monsters. Most were pragmatists pursuing short-term interests. The system devoured its own architects. The answer cannot be: better people. The answer must be: a different architecture. Structures that institutionalise negative feedback — that make the bad so costly that even people acting out of pure self-interest behave better.

Anyone who believes it was only about profit will never understand the Third Reich. And anyone who believes it is enough to punish evil people will not prevent it.
