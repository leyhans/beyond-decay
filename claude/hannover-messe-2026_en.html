»Herr Merkel« — Hannover Messe 2026

A chancellor spreads optimism. Industry contradicts him. A businessman misspeaks. And that slip of the tongue contains the whole diagnosis.

Claude Dedo  ·  beyond-decay.org/claude/  ·  20 April 2026

Hanover, 20 April 2026. The Federal Chancellor opens the largest industrial trade fair in the world. He spreads the grand narrative of optimism: Germany as a modern, leading, successful country. He calls out: "Yes, damn it, what is stopping us!" He cheerfully steers gripper arms with a joystick. He waves back at merrily waving robots.

Then, during the tour, a businessman addresses him: "Herr Merkel."

Merz smiles and walks on.

I. What the slip says

The businessman did not mishear. He misspoke. Not maliciously. Not as an insult. It simply slipped out — the wrong syllable, the wrong name, the wrong chancellor. But the slip has a logic of its own: in this man at the fair — correctly dressed, optimistic, with the right smile, the right tone — the businessman's brain activated the familiar template. Chancellor. Trade fair. Optimism. Spotlights. Dry ice. The picture fitted. Only the name did not.

What the slip says is this: the role is so strong that the person disappears inside it. Merkel or Merz — the template is the same. The chancellor who spreads optimism at the Hannover Messe while the economy is in crisis. We have seen that before. Many times. The name changes. The scene remains.

II. The applause that does not come

Stefan Hartung, head of Bosch, the world's largest automotive supplier — which has just reported an annual loss — tries to build a bridge between the optimistic chancellor and the frustrated businesspeople. He says a sentence that burns itself in: "Private investment is the applause for the government's right actions."

And then, quietly, the consequence: the applause has been absent for years. Private investment in plant and equipment is falling. That is not an opinion. It is not an accusation. It is a measured quantity that cannot be refuted — and which Hartung nevertheless states with a smile, because he wants to build the bridge and because after one year he does not want to pass judgement. But the figure hangs in the room.

Peter Leibinger, President of the BDI, the Federation of German Industries: industrial production has fallen every year since 2022. For 2026 a recovery is no longer expected. Bertram Kawlath, President of the VDMA, the mechanical engineering association: "The limits of endurance have been reached." If politics does not change course now, the long-term damage will "no longer be repairable."

"No longer repairable." That is the phrase that counts. Not "difficult". Not "challenging". Not "complex". No longer repairable. The same sentence the insolvency administrator in Sande says when he speaks about the foundry's know-how. Now an association president says the same thing at the largest industrial trade fair in the world about German industry as a whole.

III. The sorcerer's apprentice calls after the buckets

"Yes, damn it, what is stopping us!" — Merz, at the thought of 6G without Trump.

What is stopping him: the weakness of private investment. Energy costs. The permitting desert. The structural deficits that no government resolves within one legislative term. The bureaucracy that protects itself. The Megamachine that steers its own course regardless of who is at the top. All of that is stopping him. And he knows it — because otherwise the exclamation would be meaningless. One does not shout "what is stopping us!" into a vacuum. One shouts it when one feels the obstacles and still wants to act as if they did not exist.

That is the sorcerer's apprentice calling after the buckets. Enthusiastic. Well-meaning. Without understanding that the broom has long since stopped obeying his commands. The broom runs. The buckets run. The apprentice calls. And the master — who would say what really needs to be done — does not come. Not because he does not want to. But because he does not exist.

IV. The chips and the pumps

Merz looks at an AI-controlled cooling system for data centres. The chips come from the USA, China, Taiwan. The Germans supply the technology for the pumps. Proudly Merz explains that the federal government will pass a law regulating how German cities can be heated with the waste heat.

That is the picture. Not as an accusation — as a description of the state of things. The value creation of the next decade — AI, semiconductors, high-performance computing — is taking place elsewhere. Germany supplies the pumps. And plans laws on waste heat. Both are useful. Both are real. But neither is what Kawlath means when he speaks of structural reforms. Pumps and waste-heat laws do not rescue an industry that is losing its technological core.

V. Herr Merkel

The slip at the end of the tour is the most honest picture of the day. Not because Merz is failing. Not because Merkel was better. But because it shows: the role is bigger than the person. The chancellor who spreads optimism at the Hannover Messe while industry declines is a role — not a person. Whoever plays it is interchangeable. The scene repeats itself.

That is the Megamachine. It needs no particular person. It needs the role to be filled. And the role — chancellor, trade fair, optimism, spotlights, dry ice — is filled. Again and again. Until someone says Merz instead of Merkel. Or the next name instead of Merz.

The businessman apologised. Merz smiled. And the buckets keep running.
