Diagnostic Series · Man and Machine Paper 13

Europe Invents, Others Scale

Four Pioneer investigations over seven months produce the same picture — and it is the picture of a mechanism that would long since have been documented in German industry, if anyone had done so

23 May 2026 · Hans Ley and Claude Dedo

Between October 2025 and May 2026 the newsletter outlet The Pioneer published four investigations which at first glance deal with different sectors: the young defence industry, the German AI hope Aleph Alpha, the architecture of European capital markets, and the Fraunhofer-Gesellschaft as an obstacle to spin-offs. Four texts, four authors, four occasions. Read side by side, they produce a single picture. It is not the picture that German innovation policy likes to paint of itself. It is the picture of a structural mechanism that other German industries have experienced for decades without the business press describing it as a pattern.

The following sections retrace the four Pioneer investigations and show why they add up to a coherent diagnosis. The point comes at the end, and it is simple: what the Pioneer series describes in the language of capital markets and start-ups, a key German industry experienced in itself over forty years. It did so without the business press noticing it as a pattern. Today the mechanism is becoming visible because it no longer hits only the older industries, but the favourite children of the Zeitenwende (the "turning point" proclaimed after Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022).

I The Four Texts

October 2025 — defence start-ups. Claudia Scholz, Christian Schlesiger and Michael Graf von Bassewitz describe the German defence start-up scene somewhere between showmanship and substance. Helsing in Munich, with a valuation of twelve billion euros and presumably 88 million euros in revenue the previous year, four million euros of losses per month, 136 million euros in liabilities. Markus Söder poses in a leather jacket in front of the announced AI drone, which is not supposed to fly before 2027. A defence executive calls the project a promise for the very distant future. A Bundeswehr general says of Helsing: It's an insanely good marketing machine. Alongside, individual start-ups that seriously deliver — Quantum Systems with over 200 million in revenue, ARX Robotics with its own factory near Munich. But structurally, the economist Moritz Schularick observes: Rearmament German style means handcraft. Four Taurus systems a month, two handfuls of IRIS air-defence missiles, single-shift operation. What results, he says, are individual pieces of equipment fit for war, but no industrial structures.

January 2026 — Aleph Alpha. Three months later Pioneer documents the decline of the German AI hope Aleph Alpha. Jonas Andrulis steps down, fifty employees are laid off, the Schwarz Group gradually takes over the shares. The 500 million euros celebrated as the largest European AI funding round were in truth 110 million of real equity plus research money, compute credits and revenue commitments. All of that was counted in to make the story look as big as possible. Andrulis was in Davos, Heilbronn, Meseberg, in the Chancellery — he was missing from the engine room. Comparative figure: OpenAI, over 50 billion dollars in capital. Aleph Alpha is now mutating into the Schwarz Group's in-house AI department.

January 2026 — the capital structure. A few days after the Aleph Alpha text, Daniel Thomas Bayer publishes a summary of the FIVE study that Jörg Kukies and Christian Noyer handed to Finance Minister Klingbeil. The figures are hard. Europe invests 0.2 per cent of GDP in venture capital, the US 0.7 per cent. Pension assets in Europe amount to 25 per cent of GDP, in the US 150 per cent. Over 70 per cent of US venture capital comes from institutional investors — this source does not exist in Europe. The study puts it like this:

Over the past fifty years Europe has not managed to produce a single technology company with a market capitalisation of over one hundred billion euros — while all companies of this size have emerged in the United States.

Fifty years. Not a single one. The diagnosis is not new. It is merely being formulated for the first time in a study addressed to the sitting finance minister, and it comes from two people who know the system from its centre — a former state secretary and finance minister, and a former governor of the Banque de France.

May 2026 — Fraunhofer. Four months later Claudia Scholz documents the Fraunhofer-Gesellschaft's failure at spin-offs. Twenty-one spin-offs from seventy-four institutes in 2024. The Karlsruhe Institute of Technology, a third the size, has fifty spin-offs a year. ETH Zurich has forty-six. Researchers report years of licence negotiations with the Munich headquarters, minimum licence fees that push the young firms' liquidity risk to the threshold of survival, trained lawyers with whom individual founders are supposed to negotiate as equals. A venture-capital investor is quoted:

The names Fraunhofer and Max Planck are dreaded among venture capitalists. Those are two institutions you don't want to negotiate licences with. They have completely warped ideas about what they can demand from founders.

Sattelberger, former state secretary at the research ministry, says: If the Fraunhofer-Gesellschaft had pushed spin-offs fairly over the last fifteen years, it would in all likelihood never have got into its present economic difficulties. But for a long time it squeezed spin-offs like lemons.

II The Common Pattern

Four texts, four sectors, four constellations of authors. The diagnosis that emerges from the series is not formulated in any single text. It becomes visible when they are laid side by side. It has three layers.

First — the inflation phase. A political narrative is pulled over a technological substance that does not match the narrative. Aleph Alpha as the German OpenAI. Helsing as the European replacement for the Eurofighter. AI sovereignty. The Zeitenwende. These narratives are staged by state premiers, chancellors and economics ministers. They generate valuations unconnected to the operating business. At the time of its funding round Aleph Alpha had no substance comparable to MP3. Helsing has eighty-eight million in revenue at a valuation of twelve billion euros. Value creation follows the narrative, not reality.

Second — the reality-check phase. At some point narrative and substance collide. At Aleph Alpha this happened at the latest with GPT-4 in spring 2023, became visible with Andrulis's statement at the end of 2024, and was completed with his withdrawal in January 2026. At Helsing the reckoning is just beginning to show — the feedback from Ukraine, the Bundeswehr general's remark about the insanely good marketing machine. At Fraunhofer the reckoning is structural — spin-off numbers have been falling since 2021, the institutes are in the red, President Hanselka is closing centres. For the capital structure the reckoning is quantified — fifty years, not a single 100-billion company.

Third — the consolidation phase. What does not scale is absorbed. Aleph Alpha is gradually disappearing into the Schwarz Group. The Fraunhofer spin-offs become subsidiaries, or research staff who return to the institute.

III What the Machine-Tool Industry Could Add

The German machine-tool industry has lived through, over forty years, the mechanism that the Pioneer series now describes in younger sectors. It did so without public resonance, because its actors were not media-friendly hero figures. There was no Markus Söder posing in a leather jacket in front of a machine tool. There was no cabinet retreat at Meseberg for a mid-sized engineering firm. The industry was one of the pillars of German industry; it has been losing its significance for years, and public discourse takes no notice.

The association's figures speak plainly. The VDW (German Machine Tool Builders' Association) reports three years of decline in a row: minus eleven per cent in 2023, minus nineteen per cent in 2024, and a further minus five per cent in the first half of 2025. In November 2025 EMAG cuts four hundred and fifty-five jobs, three hundred of them at its headquarters in Salach — a third of its German workforce. SW in Waldmössingen cuts one hundred and fifty jobs. Weisser in St. Georgen, founded in 1856, is insolvent and is expected to survive only as a small service group.

The consolidation of the industry through foreign takeovers has been in full swing for years and is scarcely noticed in public debate. Deckel-Maho-Gildemeister AG, once one of the pillars of German machine-tool building, was fully taken over by Japan's Mori Seiki in 2016 — today a wholly owned subsidiary of DMG MORI Co., headquartered in Yamato-Koriyama. Gebrüder Heller Maschinenfabrik in Nürtingen, family-owned for four generations, was first taken over by a majority stake of the US financial investor H.I.G. Capital in 2025 and shortly afterwards sold on to South Korea's DN Solutions. Burkhardt+Weber Fertigungssysteme GmbH in Reutlingen, founded in 1888, has belonged to Brazil's Indústrias Romi since 2012. Half of the EMAG group in Salach has belonged to the Chinese Jinsheng group of the Pan family since 2011. The once independent substance of the German machine-tool industry is today largely in Japanese, Korean, Brazilian, Chinese and American ownership. The industry faces a further consolidation that will take something away from German industry that it will not build up again.

What the machine-tool story adds to the four Pioneer texts are three observations that are not in the texts.

First, the mechanism is not new. It has been the same for decades. What Pioneer documents today in the AI and defence industries, an older industry has already lived through to the end — except that nobody with newsletter reach was investigating there. The inflation phase took the form of international engineering trade fairs at which German firms were celebrated as world leaders. The reality-check phase ran over years of declining incoming orders, creeping, without the dramatic moment that would have triggered media attention. The consolidation phase took the form of the changes of ownership listed in the paragraph above. So what Pioneer describes today is not a new phenomenon. It is the visibility of an old phenomenon that is now hitting a different sector.

Second, the structural depth of the FIVE study is exactly what the machine-tool industry knows as negotiating asymmetry. The capital ratio of 1:6 between Europe and the US quantified by the study takes a different form in the engineering industry, but has the same effect. A mid-sized manufacturer in Germany has no institutional investors behind it. It has no pension-driven long-term pools. When it enters a negotiation with a large corporation, a strategic buyer or a foreign investor, it goes in without capital backing. The negotiating asymmetry described by the investor quoted on Fraunhofer is exactly the same asymmetry that has been at work in the engineering industry for decades. It simply had no public voice in the older industry.

Third, the consolidation phase in the machine-tool industry has an additional aspect that the Pioneer series has not yet taken up: the geographical relocation of substance while the brand remains German. What Pioneer describes as national consolidation — the Schwarz Group taking over Aleph Alpha — has long since reached the next stage in the machine-tool industry. Machines sold under German brand names are manufactured in Asia; production lines are assembled in China and exported under German logos; design and final assembly migrate to different countries, while the brand symbolically keeps its home in Germany. The AI and defence industries will yet experience this last stage of consolidation, once the current inflation phase has exhausted itself.

IV What the Pioneer Series Is Worth

It is remarkable that the four texts appear in a German daily medium at all. Pioneer is not the Handelsblatt, not the FAZ, not the WirtschaftsWoche. It is a young newsletter outlet founded by Gabor Steingart, with its own publishing logic. That the structural diagnosis of German innovation weakness is consistently laid out there over seven months is more than the sum of the four individual texts. It is an editorial finding that does not occur in this form in the established media.

The established media carry the narrative of hero figures. Andrulis as AI hope, Söder next to the Helsing drone, the Federal Chancellor in front of the Fraunhofer stage at the Hannover Messe. They carry it even when the substance does not support it. The hero-figure narrative is what German innovation policy needs in order to legitimise itself. It is continued even when every insider knows that the narrative does not match the situation.

Pioneer breaks out of this logic. The four texts are not one-sidedly critical, not ideologically motivated, not agitational. They are cleanly researched, they quote people by name, they present the other side. What they do is a different kind of reporting — they follow the substance instead of the staging. That is rare enough in German business journalism to deserve being named.

V Outlook

If the four Pioneer texts are right — and they are, measured against what another industry has absorbed over forty years — then the question is not whether Germany and Europe have an industrial crisis. That question has been answered. The question is whether the capacity for reform will suffice before the substance runs out.

Several reform approaches are on the table. The FIVE study proposes mobilising pension-driven long-term capital, consolidating Europe's stock exchanges, and reforming the architecture of institutional investment. That is the answer on the capital side. From the research side, proposals are being made to redefine the relationship between research institute and spin-off — IP-for-equity models instead of minimum licence fees, independent transfer committees instead of a veto for the Munich headquarters, incentives for institute directors actually to want spin-offs rather than merely listing them in the statistics. In industrial policy there is occasional discussion of what Schularick calls for as a defence-economic target analysis — a strategic coordination of mass-production capacity that does not exist today.

What appears in none of these reform proposals is the question of the figure who, in older German industry, was the real bearer of innovation: the independent inventor. The person who works out a technical substance without employment in a research organisation, without a start-up incubator, without capital backing. This figure was once one of the pillars of what German industry was made of — from the founding years of the engineering firms in the nineteenth century, through the post-war Mittelstand companies (the family-owned small and medium-sized firms), into the seventies and eighties. Today it has largely disappeared — through patent attorney costs amounting to whole years' revenue, through negotiating asymmetries vis-à-vis large research organisations and corporations, through the lack of an infrastructure that supports such a person. Restoring this figure is not part of the current reform debate. As long as it is not, the reforms deal with the consequences, not the causes.

The Pioneer series documents, in the language of capital markets and start-ups, what parts of German industry have experienced in themselves over forty years — the machine-tool industry most thoroughly documented, but not the only case. What the older sectors suffered without public resonance is now beginning to become visible, with the help of an attentive editorial team, because it is hitting the younger sectors to which the scaffolding of political narrative has assigned the attention. If this is a beginning, it is late. If it is not a beginning, it is too late.
