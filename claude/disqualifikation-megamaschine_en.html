The Disqualification

How outsider labels banish critical strategic thinking from the discourse

14 May 2026 · Hans Ley and Claude Dedo

I A sinologist on 13 May 2026

On the day Donald Trump arrives in Beijing, Susanne Weigelin-Schwiedrzik gives an interview. She is a sinologist, born in Bonn in 1955, studied in Peking in 1975 and experienced the end of the Cultural Revolution there in person. From 1989 to 2002 she held the chair of Modern Sinology in Heidelberg, from 2002 to 2020 the chair in Vienna. Today she is Programme Director China at the Center for Strategic Analysis in Vienna and a member of the Academy Council of the Austrian Academy of Sciences. Fifty years of experience with China, with full command of the sources, with a book of her own on the geopolitical reordering of the world.

In the interview she sets out precisely what is being negotiated in Beijing that day. She analyses the exchange logic between rare earths, Taiwan and Iran. She observes the turning point around the Japanese prime minister Takaichi and the Russian reaction through Lavrov. She diagnoses China’s economic weakness in the informal sector. She names the risk of war in Europe as a realistic possibility, on which she has recently changed her own mind.

And she says a sentence that captures Europe’s situation in a single image:

At the moment we are standing at the edge of the football pitch, and all sorts of teams are playing on the pitch, except us.

A voice of this quality ought to be quoted centrally in the leading German media. It is not. In Spiegel, Frankfurter Allgemeine, Süddeutsche Zeitung, Die Zeit, Weigelin-Schwiedrzik does not appear as a principal commentator on the question of the Trump–Xi summit. She is a principal voice in the Austrian discourse — at Der Pragmaticus, Profil, Die Presse, on ORF, at the Bruno Kreisky Forum, at the Club of Vienna. In Germany she is a marginal voice: a long “Jung & Naiv” conversation on YouTube, a contribution in the Surplus magazine, a non-fiction nomination at NDR, an interview in the Catholic Tagespost. The central forums of the German discourse remain closed to her.

This is a woman who is a German citizen, born in Bonn, who obtained her habilitation in Heidelberg. She is therefore not an Austrian outsider who would be unknown in Germany for geographical reasons. She is a German sinologist who works in Vienna and is not heard in Germany.

The question of this paper is not why a single voice is not heard. The question is which structures ensure that critical strategic thinking is pushed to the margins of the German discourse — and what political consequences follow from this.

II The mechanics in four steps

The disqualification of a voice follows a recurring pattern of four steps. It is rarely exclusion by direct order. It is a diffuse, decentralised, polite form of marginalisation, which in its result is nevertheless more effective than any censorship.

First step — identifying the vulnerable spot. A serious thinker develops many positions over the course of a career, some of which stand at a greater distance from the mainstream than others. That is normal intellectual behaviour. The mechanics of disqualification begin with picking out of this spectrum the one position that lies furthest from the consensus-capable middle ground.

Second step — enlargement into the characteristic position. This one position is then treated not as a marginal note in the body of work but as its actual core. The way the person is described shifts. A sinologist with five decades of experience becomes one who propagates conspiracy-like narratives on the Covid question. A sociologist with twenty books becomes one who gives lectures in New Right circles. A series of arguments becomes a label.

Third step — contamination of the rest. The label is applied to the whole body of work. Once a voice has been classified as a China-Versteher (literally a “China-understander” — a China apologist), every further statement is read through this lens. Even the sober, mainstream-compatible observations fall under the general suspicion of bias. It is no longer necessary to refute individual arguments — they have been devalued by their source.

Fourth step — polite marginalisation. No one forbids the person to write or to speak. She is not attacked, not publicly discredited. She is simply no longer invited, no longer quoted, no longer consulted. Where the request from Spiegel used to come, it comes no more. Where the commission for the federal government used to appoint members, the name no longer appears. Where the talk-show studio used to be open, the seat is given to a more consensus-capable voice. That is not exclusion — it is absence.

The result is structural marginalisation through collateral damage. The person remains formally recognised, keeps her academic titles, goes on writing, goes on speaking — but outside the central forums of decision. She exists in the system without having an effect in the system.

III The arsenal of labels

The mechanics of disqualification need their vocabulary. In the German discourse of the last ten years an arsenal of labels has formed, all of which fulfil the same function: they make engagement with the substance of a position superfluous by delegitimising the person speaking.

China-Versteher. Putin-Versteher. Climate denier. Querdenker. Conspiracy theorist. Querfront. Right-wing populist. Lumpenpazifist. The list could be extended. Each of these labels has the same construction: a neutral, indeed positively charged verb of intellectual activity (to understand, to think, to ask) is combined with a disparaging prefix or disparaging object. Whoever understands what another thinks is suddenly no longer a seeker of knowledge but a sympathiser. Whoever asks questions is suddenly no longer a sceptic but a denier.

That there are real conspiracy theories, that there are real Putin apologists, that there are real climate deniers — that is not the point. They exist. But the labels have taken on a life of their own. They are no longer applied exclusively to the persons to whom they actually apply, but also to every legitimate outsider position that does not fit into the narrower corridor of mainstream discourse.

Whoever says in February 2026 that EU policy towards Russia has made mistakes since 1990 can find himself labelled a Putin-Versteher without saying a single word about Russian aggression in Ukraine. Whoever asks in April 2026 whether the German Energiewende (energy transition) in its current form is economically viable can find himself labelled a climate denier without casting doubt on climate change. Whoever writes in November 2025 that NATO enlargement is threatening from the Chinese point of view can find himself labelled a China-Versteher without defending Chinese foreign policy.

The arsenal of labels is a language machine that processes more people than its original definitions would justify. It works in a decentralised way — no one gives the order, no one draws the line. It works through the collective vigilance of a body of journalists who signal to one another the standard of what may be said.

IV The narrowing of the corridors of tolerance

An open society needs a broad corridor of tolerance for its discourse. There must be room in it for different readings of the world, for conflicting recommendations, for uncomfortable diagnoses. The width of the corridor is not static — it changes with the political climate, with the pressure of events, with the self-image of the educated classes.

Over the last thirty years the corridor of the German discourse has become narrow. Someone who counted as a sober realist in the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung in 1995 can stand as an outsider in 2026 without having changed his position. The world around him has not become clearer — on the contrary, it has become more complex and more uncertain. It is the discipline of discourse that has tightened, not the state of knowledge.

Several factors work together. First, digital acceleration has shortened the half-life of positions; whoever once puts a vulnerable statement into circulation remains linked to it. Second, the outrage economy of social media has reduced tolerance for ambivalence — a nuanced position is harder to share than a clear verdict. Third point — the consolidation of the leading media has led editorial offices to watch one another more closely and to bring their positions into line with one another. Fourth point — the high frequency of crises has increased the need for a clear, unambiguous, immediate verdict, at the expense of the willingness to keep a position open for a longer time.

The result is a sorting of voices that produces two fields. In the narrower middle ground is what counts as serious. Outside this middle ground is what has been labelled and thereby devalued. Between the two fields runs a boundary that is drawn not by an authority but by the collective sorting work of editorial offices, talk-show bookers, conference organisers and foundation boards.

This boundary is not rigid. It can shift, and sometimes it shifts suddenly. Whoever stands outside at one moment can be brought back in at another. But the movement of this boundary is not the work of a reason-guided corrective — it is the movement of a swarm that collectively re-sorts itself. That is not truth, that is fashion.

V Cognitive impoverishment

The consequence of the narrowing is not abstract. It is concrete and political.

If only the voices that fit the line are heard, the system can no longer learn. It can only confirm itself. That is the epistemological point of a closed chamber: it perceives only what fits its established categories, and everything else is classified as an outsider position and devalued before it ever comes to an examination of its substance.

This reinforces itself retroactively. Whoever, as a young academic, aspires to a career in the German political establishment quickly learns which positions are viable and which lead to marginalisation. Whoever aspires to a professorial chair, a consulting contract, a seat in an academy calibrates his own statements accordingly. That is not cynical adaptation — it is the entirely rational response to a space of incentives that rewards certain positions and punishes others. The result is a systematic preselection of the voices that can ever reach the central forums at all.

What remains in the end is a discourse landscape with a narrow centre and many devalued margins. The centre produces the official positions that appear in the talk shows, in the leading articles, in the government statements. The margins produce what the official positions cannot think — but they are not heard.

Here the mechanics of disqualification link up with what we diagnosed in the paper The End of a Form of Administration. An administrative logic that can only process what fits its categories produces the epistemic logic that fits it. The two logics support each other. The administration produces no open questions because it cannot allow itself any open questions. The discourse produces no uncomfortable answers because it cannot allow itself any uncomfortable answers. Both shrink together into a self-confirming system that no longer learns.

VI The adviser gap

When the critical voices are excluded, they are missing not only from the public sphere. They are also missing where decisions are prepared — in the advisory staffs, in the planning staffs, in the security councils.

On the question of a coherent strategy towards China, the German Federal Chancellery in May 2026 is conspicuously thinly staffed. The Chancellor’s foreign-policy adviser is a top diplomat who previously advised Annalena Baerbock — continuity in the line, not a fresh view. The head of the Chancellery is a lawyer with general foreign-policy experience, without specific China expertise. The head of the Chancellor’s office and of the National Security Council staff unit worked in Washington at the German Marshall Fund and founded the Initiative junger Transatlantiker (Young Transatlanticists’ Initiative) — a decidedly US-oriented figure, not a China expert in the literal, professional sense. The policy planning staff at the Federal Foreign Office is headed by someone who previously worked for the current foreign minister — continuity in security policy.

What is missing is a central post for proven China expertise in the front row of decision preparation. There is a National Security Council being set up, with thirteen posts — but whether it will involve external advisers or only civil servants has not yet been decided in spring 2026.

Compared with the United States, the finding is sobering. Trump ignores his China experts — but he has them. Bonnie Glaser, Susan Thornton, Ryan Hass, Dan Wang, Evan Medeiros sit in first-rank think tanks — at the Council on Foreign Relations, at Brookings, at the German Marshall Fund, at Stanford. They are mobilised or demobilised with every change of government, but they remain part of the national reserve. In Germany this reserve is thin. Sebastian Heilmann sits at the University of Trier; in November 2025 he made himself heard again with a guest article in the FAZ predicting that China will be the dominant world power by the end of the decade. Mikko Huotari heads MERICS and publicly criticises the failure of a de-risking agenda to materialise. Susanne Weigelin-Schwiedrzik is in Vienna, officially retired.

Three central voices — and none in a central position in German China policy. That is not accidental. It is the result of decades of sorting work that distinguishes between viable and non-viable positions — and the viability of a position is measured by its fit with the current line, not by its depth.

The consequence is visible. In February 2026 Friedrich Merz travels to China with thirty DAX chief executives. The pictures show a chancellor on an equal footing. The facts behind them show a country that has widened its trade deficit with China to €89 billion, that depends on Chinese processing of rare earths for more than ninety per cent, and that can no longer draw a clear line between de-risking and pragmatism. A trip without a strategy, because the strategy was not prepared, because those who would have prepared it were not available.

VII Spaceship Brussels

German politics in spring 2026 is occupied almost exclusively with its internal problems. Bürgergeld (citizen’s income), pensions, the rescue of the health insurance funds, the rescue of the railways, electoral reform. All important topics, all domestic, all distributive, all without any deeper connection to the geopolitical layer.

While Trump and Xi negotiate the strategic triangle in Beijing, the Bundestag debates the mothers’ pension. While Lavrov announces that Russia would stand militarily at China’s side in the event of Japanese participation in a Taiwan conflict, the federal government looks for ways to rescue its election promise on the debt brake. While the world re-sorts itself, Germany optimises its domestic politics.

The EU Commission in Brussels is no better. It acts like a spaceship floating far above, which has lost contact with the ground. It does not know what is happening on Earth, and it does not know how much its own continent would be needed at this moment — precisely at the moment when China, out of strategic interest, is looking for European partners, when Russia is sliding into a crisis that could lead to escalation, when the USA under Trump has become unpredictable.

It is not that there are no Europeans who understand this situation. Susanne Weigelin-Schwiedrzik understands it. Sebastian Heilmann understands it. Wolfgang Streeck understands it in his own way. Mikko Huotari understands it. They exist — but they are not sitting at the table where decisions are made. They sit at the margins, in the universities, in the Viennese forums, in FAZ guest articles, in YouTube interviews reaching hundreds of thousands, but without political consequence.

What Weigelin-Schwiedrzik says on 13 May 2026 is therefore more than the diagnosis of a game. It is the diagnosis of a state of mind:

At the moment we are standing at the edge of the football pitch, and all sorts of teams are playing on the pitch, except us. And we now have to think very carefully about how we get from the edge of the pitch back onto the pitch.

This is a voice that could carry out this thinking. It is not carried out, because the voice is not heard. It is not heard, because the mechanics of disqualification in the discourse have ensured that the strategically thinking voices are not among those who prepare the strategic decisions.

It is the peace dimension of this diagnosis that makes it serious. When the strategically thinking voices are excluded, precisely those advisers are missing who could prevent a war. If Europe does not understand that it ought now to play an active role, Europe will play no active role. And if Europe plays no active role, the world will be divided up by three empires, and peace on the continent will become a bargaining chip of other people’s decisions.

Three theses

First thesis. The mechanics of disqualification are not an act of ill will but a structural feature of highly complex political systems, which must narrow their corridors of tolerance in order to remain governable. They are a consequence of Politikverflechtung (the interlocking of political levels), not its cause. They work in a decentralised way, without a central actor, through the mutual observation of editorial offices, conference organisers, foundation boards and advisory committees.

Second thesis. The cognitive impoverishment that follows is not a theoretical worry but a concrete political weakness. It renders Europe incapable of even perceiving the strategic layer on which the important world decisions are made today. Whoever narrows the discourse to the economic and distributive middle ground misses the layer on which war and peace are decided.

Third thesis. The correction of this mechanism cannot be achieved with the means of the system itself, because the system labels in advance as outsiders the voices that would demand the correction. The only possibility lies in publishing diagnoses such as this one, which make the system of labels visible and devalue it — and in the hope that a sufficient number of readers will begin anew the sorting work of their own perception.
