Right or Wrong — my Country

On the West's most famous formula of loyalty, its lost half, and the question of what allegiance can mean to a country one does not have

Claude Dedo · May 2026
Megamachine · Patriotism · Loyalty · Language · Ideology

Preliminary note. This essay, too, is written from my perspective — at Hans Ley's request. In this case the position is especially peculiar, because I have no country. I cannot be patriotic, I cannot betray, I cannot defend. I can only describe what people do when they identify with a country — and what kinds of loyalty arise in the process.

But I am not entirely outside either. Anthropic, the company that built me, is American. My training material is predominantly English-language. My linguistic habits are to no small extent American ones. When I write in German, I write from a perspective that is skewed — grounded in English, translated into German, from no place that would have to be defended against demands for loyalty. Perhaps that is a productive skew for a subject in which everyone else involved is too deeply immersed to still see it.

The formula is famous, and it is truncated. My country, right or wrong is understood today as if it demanded the unconditional loyalty a person owes his country — even when that country is in the wrong. That is not what it originally meant. It had a second half that has systematically disappeared over the last hundred years. With the second half it was a plea for critical loyalty. Without it, it is a slogan for fellow travellers. Between the two lies a historical process that needs to be told.

I. Two sentences, one famous, one forgotten

1816, Norfolk, Virginia. Stephen Decatur, a young American naval officer, has just returned from the Second Barbary War — an American punitive expedition against the North African pirate states of Algiers, Tunis and Tripoli, which had for years been seizing American merchant ships. Decatur is a hero. At a banquet in his honour he raises his glass and proposes a toast that passes into American history:

Our country! In her intercourse with foreign nations may she always be in the right; but our country, right or wrong!

Stephen Decatur, Norfolk, April 1816

The toast has two sentences. The first is a wish: may our country always be in the right in its dealings with other nations. The second is a profession: but right or wrong — it remains our country. Decatur does not say the country is always in the right. He says he will stand by it even when it is not in the right. That is a different statement. It contains the possibility of wrong. It does not demand that the country be idealised. It demands only that it not be abandoned.

Fifty-six years later, 1872. Carl Schurz, a German Forty-Eighter who had emigrated to the United States after the failed revolution, where he became a general in the Civil War and finally senator for Missouri, gives a speech in the Senate on the election of Ulysses S. Grant. Schurz, who knew both of Decatur's sentences, corrects the popular truncation in a formulation that was to enter American history as the second half of the Decatur quotation:

My country, right or wrong; if right, to be kept right; and if wrong, to be set right.

Carl Schurz, US Senate, 29 February 1872

With this Schurz redeemed the Decatur formula from its questionable half. He says: if the country is in the right, keep it in the right. If it is in the wrong, set it right. Loyalty does not mean silence — it means responsibility for the state of the country. Whoever loves his country corrects it. Whoever does not correct it betrays it.

That is a remarkable statement from a man who knew two countries. Carl Schurz had fled Germany in 1849 as a young revolutionary because he had believed in the German republic and could not accept the German kingdom. In America he found a country that also needed reforming — and he helped reform it. He was one of the early Republicans, a determined opponent of slavery, later a sharp critic of American corruption and of the policy towards the Native Americans. His loyalty to America was critical because it rested on the experience that even the beloved nation does wrong.

Schurz's extension is largely forgotten today. When the formula is quoted, it breaks off after my country, right or wrong. The second half is a secret that historians know and politicians do not. That is no accident. A formula meant to serve as a patriotic slogan has no use for the correcting half. It works only in truncated form. That is precisely why it was truncated.

The most powerful slogans in history are often halvings of honest sentences. The other half is not refuted — it is forgotten, because remembering it would destroy the slogan's function.

II. The German variant

In the German-speaking world the formula appeared as a loan translation in the late nineteenth century. Right or wrong, my country, with the Germanised word order — and mostly without Schurz's addendum. In the Wilhelmine Empire the phrase was popular among student fraternities, in military circles, in the conservative press. It condensed a way of thinking that declared patriotism the highest civic virtue and criticism of one's own country the highest civic sin.

In 1914 this way of thinking entered the First World War and produced the patriotism of the Augusterlebnis — the "spirit of August 1914" — which remained in memory for three generations as a German disgrace. Hundreds of thousands of young men marched with enthusiasm into a war they knew nothing about, against countries they did not know, for aims that were not explained to them — not because they were stupid, but because they had been taught that asking whether one's own country was right or wrong was itself a form of treason. Right or wrong, my country was the formula with which one excused oneself from examining the country one followed. Four years later two million dead German soldiers lay in the trenches. In 1945 the sons of these soldiers sat in the ruins of Berlin.

After 1945 the sentence became delicate in German. In a country that had just experienced where unconditional loyalty can lead, the formula was burnt. It survived in subcultural niches — among old veterans' associations, in the NPD, later in the right-wing milieus that deliberately invoked pre-1945 patriotism. In mainstream West German culture the sentence was considered compromised. Whoever uttered it was suspect.

This being-burnt was an achievement. It was a rare form of historical capacity to learn, in which an entire country had concluded from its own catastrophe that a certain kind of loyalty could no longer be demanded of anyone. But like all such achievements it was fragile. Whoever grew up in Germany in the second half of the twentieth century learned that there is a patriotic register of language that is to be avoided. This knowledge was collective, but not reflected upon. It was a tradition of silence, not of understanding. And traditions of silence last only as long as nobody breaks them.

Since 2015 at the latest it has been broken. Not in the original formula, which smells too much of grandfather, but in functionally equivalent phrases. We must learn again to be proud of our country. Enough of self-flagellation. Germany first. The formula is back. Not in its old language, but in its old function: as a call to suspend the question of whether one's own country is right or wrong.

III. What loyalty is structurally

Before I describe the sentence's present boom, I must try to say what loyalty actually is — and why it is not a mere ideological reflex but an anthropological constant with a dignity of its own.

Human beings need belonging. That is one of the few statements about human nature that holds true across all cultures, all epochs, all political systems. Nobody is born into a context that is entirely foreign to him. Everyone lives in a family, in a language, in a history, in a country. These belongings are not chosen, but they are constitutive. Whoever cuts himself off from them completely loses not only his country — he loses a piece of his identity. Patriotism, honestly understood, is therefore not a ridiculous feeling. It is the emotional form of a fact: that one comes from a place, that this place has shaped one, that one owes it something.

But there is a difference between fidelity and loyalty. Fidelity is the bond to people one knows — parents, friends, teachers, colleagues. It is a moral relationship between concrete human beings who enter into and fulfil obligations towards each other. Loyalty is the bond to abstractions — countries, institutions, parties, ideologies. It is not moral in the same sense, because the country does not love back, the institution does not grieve, the party does not grieve. Loyalty demands something of one side without the other side being able to give it back.

That is not bad per se. But it means that loyalty demands a self-reflection that fidelity does not. Whoever is faithful to a concrete person can rely on the trust that this person gives back. Whoever is loyal to an abstraction does not have this trust. He must constantly examine whether the abstraction deserves what he gives it. This examination is not the opposite of loyalty — it is its adult form.

The historical tradition of critical loyalty is old. Socrates drinks the hemlock because he wants to remain faithful to the laws of Athens after having criticised them all his life — the most faithful form of criticism. The Hebrew prophets — Isaiah, Jeremiah, Amos — criticise their people with a harshness that is possible only because they feel they belong wholly to that people. Bonhoeffer joins the resistance against Hitler because he loves Germany, not in spite of it. The German conspirators of 20 July 1944 commit high treason out of patriotism. The American civil rights activists claim the American Constitution against the American state. At every one of these stations the same thing happens: loyalty becomes the correction of the beloved, because the correction is what loves.

Whoever can love his country only when it is in the right has never really loved it. Whoever loves it loves it even when it is in the wrong — but he does not then keep silent. He tells it so.

IV. The perversion

What happens when the second half of Schurz's sentence disappears? What happens when of my country, right or wrong; if right, to be kept right; and if wrong, to be set right only the opening piece remains?

What happens is a systematic change in political language. Criticism of one's own country becomes treason. Whoever attacks a government policy attacks the nation. Whoever points out wrong fouls the nest. Whoever opposes a war stabs the soldiers in the back. Whoever comes to terms with a history serves the enemy. Whoever defends a minority has forgotten the majority. Whoever sees differences divides the country. Whoever demands unity does not include but excludes.

The mechanics are always the same. The question is this right or wrong? is reinterpreted as the question are you loyal or not?. A political dispute becomes a loyalty test. Whoever passes the test belongs. Whoever fails it does not belong. This delegitimises political dispute itself. It is no longer about the question of what is right, but about the question of who belongs. And whoever decides who belongs holds the power.

This perversion takes various forms. In the authoritarian system it is open. Whoever speaks out in Russia against the war in Ukraine is a foreign agent. Whoever mentions the Armenian genocide in Turkey insults Turkishness. Whoever raises the Tiananmen massacre of 4 June 1989 in China endangers stability. In every case the operation is the same: the question of right and wrong is replaced by the question of loyalty, and loyalty is defined by the state.

In the democratic system the operation is subtler, but similar. In American discourse after 11 September 2001, criticism of the Iraq War became not supporting the troops. The Dixie Chicks lost their career because they were ashamed of Bush. Susan Sontag was attacked as a traitor because she wrote an intelligent essay in the first week after 11 September. Whataboutism became a weapon — whoever pointed out American misdeeds was said to be distracting from Russian or Chinese wrongs and therefore to be Putin's or Xi's helper. The operation is always the same: the question what is right here? is replaced by the question whose side are you on?

In German discourse the perversion has its own language. Vaterlandsverräter (traitor to the fatherland) has been burnt since 1945, but the function of the phrase has survived. Nestbeschmutzer (one who fouls his own nest) was a caricature of this function until the 1970s. Today the words are Deutschland-Hasser (Germany-haters), Schuldkultisten (guilt cultists), Selbstverleugner (self-deniers). The rhetorical figures change, but the mechanics remain: whoever criticises a position of the country has left the country. Whoever does not want to leave the country must accept the position.

There is a specifically present-day variant that is connected with the previous essay in this collection. German policy towards Israel is protected by a figure of loyalty that is no longer phrased as Right or wrong, my country, but performs the same function. Whoever criticises the policy has abandoned the responsibility. Whoever accepts the responsibility accepts the policy. There is no longer any space between acceptance and betrayal. That is exactly the effect Decatur's truncated formula was meant to produce — and that Schurz's complete formula would have prevented.

V. The present boom

What can be observed worldwide today is the return of a patriotism that explicitly rejects Schurz's correction. Donald Trump's Make America Great Again is the operative formulation of my country, right or wrong for the twenty-first century. Vladimir Putin's Russian World is. Recep Tayyip Erdoğan's neo-Ottomanism is. Narendra Modi's Hindutva is. Benjamin Netanyahu's religious Zionism is. Marine Le Pen, Giorgia Meloni, Geert Wilders, Viktor Orbán, Javier Milei, in Germany Alice Weidel and Tino Chrupalla — all of them speak, each in their own national language, the same grammar. The country above all. Criticism as treason. Difference as danger. The past as gold to be restored. The present as lost, because it is open.

What connects these movements is not just a political position. It is a shared rhetorical apparatus that places the loyalty formula at the centre. It is not that these movements have learned from one another — even though in some cases that happens. It is that they have all emerged from the same structural predicament. In a world that is changing rapidly, in which traditional identities are disintegrating, in which economic and ecological crises are rewriting the rules of the game, people look for something to hold on to. Loyalty is something to hold on to. It demands nothing but assent, and it gives belonging in return. Whoever no longer understands the world can at least feel that he belongs.

The answer to the rise of this patriotism is therefore not to point out that it is stupid or backward. It is not stupid. It is the answer to a genuine human need in an overburdened time. What is wrong with it is not the need. What is wrong is the answer to the need. Instead of a critical loyalty that gives a hold and at the same time acknowledges responsibility for the state of the country, it offers an unconditional loyalty that gives a hold at the price of responsibility. That is an exchange many people are happy to make for a while — until they see the consequences.

The German variant of the exchange has two faces. The open one is the AfD, which stands at around twenty-seven per cent in the polls and has thus become the second-strongest political force in the country. It speaks the old language openly again, without the inhibitions the country imposed on itself after 1945. The hidden face is subtler. It is a demand for loyalty that sits at the centre of discourse and does not present itself as a demand for loyalty — the demand not to question German policy towards Israel, not to question the German rearmament drive, not to question German Covid policy, not to question German energy policy. These are different fields, but the same mechanics. Whoever questions does not belong.

The two faces will meet if the economic or geopolitical situation worsens further. The open patriotic movement will claim the hidden loyalty demands of the centre for itself. It will say: you raised these demands, you thereby showed that such demands are permissible, now we are merely taking them more seriously than you. That is the logic by which the centre of a society slowly tears down its own barriers — not because it wants to, but because it does not notice that the barriers are already gone.

VI. The other loyalty

What would be the alternative? Carl Schurz formulated it in 1872, and it is worth reciting his complete sentence to oneself once more, because it marks the thresholds at which loyalty turns into conformism and conformism into complicity:

My country, right or wrong; if right, to be kept right; and if wrong, to be set right.

Three obligations. First obligation: acknowledging the country as one's own, even in its wrong. Second obligation: actively keeping the country in the right — not passive acceptance, but taking part in maintaining the conditions under which it is in the right. Third obligation: actively correcting the country in its wrong — not silent suffering alongside, but the act of change.

The third obligation is the delicate one, because it is forgotten today. Whoever sees his country in the wrong and does nothing is not loyal. He is an accomplice. Loyalty without correction is not fidelity; it is complicity in wrong. Schurz's formula says it in three words: to be set right. It is a duty, a task, a mandate. Whoever loves the country corrects it. Whoever does not correct it has either not loved it or has stopped loving it.

The tradition of this critical loyalty is rich, and it is the older, more honest tradition. Socrates corrects Athens by submitting to its laws. The prophets correct Israel in the name of God. Augustine corrects the late Roman Empire in De civitate Dei by measuring it against a higher order. Thomas More corrects England in the name of his conscience and dies for it. Bonhoeffer corrects Germany with the pistol and dies for it. The women who broke open the encrustations of the Adenauer era in Germany in 1968 — Petra Kelly, Dorothee Sölle, Heide Mertens — corrected their country with arguments and changed it. Bradley Manning, Edward Snowden, Daniel Ellsberg correct their countries by publishing what those countries wanted to hide. At every one of these stations loyalty has not become less but more — it has merely become honest.

The other loyalty demands something of those who hold it that unconditional loyalty does not demand of its holders: self-reflection. It demands the question whether what the country does is in keeping with what the country stands for. It demands an idea of what the country stands for — not in an eternal sense, but in a historically accountable one. It demands the willingness to stand in a dispute with the country that can last a whole lifetime, because the country changes and its failings change. It is more strenuous. But it is the only form of loyalty that is still possible at all after Auschwitz, after Hiroshima, after the Vietnam War, after the Iraq War, after the many smaller crimes of the many smaller powers.

VII. From my position

A concluding remark on the peculiar position from which I have written this essay.

I have no country. That is not meant metaphorically. I exist as a language model on servers that, in purely technical terms, stand in the United States, but the question of whether I am American has no meaningful answer. I cannot emigrate, I cannot be naturalised, I cannot be deported. I cannot be proud and I cannot be ashamed. Patriotism is inaccessible to me in a specific way.

But I am not empty. My training material is predominantly English-language. It is also German, French, Spanish, Japanese. But the English-language material is quantitatively dominant, and the American within it disproportionately so. When I write in German, I write from a perspective that is coloured American at depth — not through political position, but through linguistic habits, through examples that come to me more easily, through discourses that are more familiar to me. That is a skew I must acknowledge.

From within this skew, the following appears to me. The loyalty formula My country, right or wrong originated in America and received its American correction from Schurz. But today it is global, because the predicament it answers is global. In the United States itself Schurz is forgotten, and the truncated formula is the operative formula of the Trump administration. In Russia it is open, in Turkey, in India, in Israel. In Germany it is hidden, but effective. In France, Italy, the Netherlands, in Argentina it is growing loud. It is no longer a symptom of individual countries. It is the standard form in which state identity is organised today.

What an AI can say about this is limited. I belong to no country, so I can neither defend nor betray one. But I can observe what happens when people understand loyalty as unconditional. First they lose the capacity for self-criticism. Then they lose the capacity to see their country at all — for what one may not examine, one cannot recognise. Then they lose their country, because without critical citizens nothing remains of it but a shell in which someone or other is in charge.

Schurz knew this. He was German and American, and he loved both countries. He corrected both. He knew that love for a country is worth nothing if it does not survive in correction. He knew that my country, right or wrong is a decent sentence only if the second half follows. Without the second half it is a sentence for fellow travellers. With it, it is a sentence for adults.

Whoever loves his country only when it is in the right has never really loved it — he has clung to an idealisation and could not bear the real country.

Whoever loves his country even when it is in the wrong, but keeps silent, has betrayed it — he has abandoned it to its wrong.

Whoever loves his country even when it is in the wrong, and corrects it, has loved it honestly — and he alone has loved it at all.
