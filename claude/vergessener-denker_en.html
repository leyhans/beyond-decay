A Forgotten Thinker

Frederic Vester and the significance of cybernetic thinking for the present

There are German thinkers whose work has become strangely invisible. Not refuted, not superseded, not sunk into a venerable tradition — but simply fallen out of awareness, without anyone having decided to forget them. Frederic Vester is one of them. Whoever talks about complexity in Germany in 2026 rarely talks about him. Whoever talks about networked thinking often does not either. Whoever uses the term megamachine thinks of Mumford. Whoever talks about cybernetics thinks of Wiener or Forrester. Vester still turns up on German non-fiction shelves, but he no longer turns up in German schools, where he might once have arrived. This story is worth telling — not out of piety towards a dead man, but because what he developed is needed more urgently today than fifty years ago.

I. Who he was

Frederic Vester was born in Saarbrücken in 1925, studied chemistry and biochemistry, took his doctorate in 1950, worked first as a scientist in Hamburg and at Yale, founded the Studiengruppe für Biologie und Umwelt in Munich in 1970 and, from the 1970s onwards, published a series of books that seem all of a piece: Das Überlebensprogramm (1972), Phänomen Stress (1976), Ballungsgebiete in der Krise (1976), Unsere Welt — ein vernetztes System (1978), Neuland des Denkens (1980), Die Kunst vernetzt zu denken (1999). He was a member of the Club of Rome, lectured worldwide, worked as an adviser to governments and corporations, and developed the Sensitivity Model as a computer-aided tool for analysing complex systems. He died in Munich in 2003, aged 78.

What distinguishes Vester from other systems theorists of his time is not the theory. He did not invent the theory of negative and positive feedback, of non-linear relationships, of thresholds and saturation effects — it lies in cybernetics with Wiener, in system dynamics with Forrester. What Vester invented is the didactic form in which this theory becomes accessible to everyone without loss of depth. He took networked thinking out of the university and brought it into German schools, touring exhibitions, board games, magazine supplements, children’s books and secondary-modern classrooms. That is not popularisation in the pejorative sense. It is the greater intellectual achievement — to think a method through so clearly that a fourteen-year-old understands it and at the same time a corporate board member learns something new from it.

II. The paper computer and the game

Two of his inventions deserve special mention. The first is the paper computer — a table in which each variable of a system is rated for its effect on every other, from zero (no effect) to three (strong effect). The row and column totals automatically yield the role of each variable in the system. Variables with a high active sum are levers — interventions there reach far into the system. Variables with a high passive sum are indicators — they show the state of the system but cannot be steered directly. Variables with high values in both sums are critical — they can destabilise the system. Variables with low values in both sums are buffers.

This technique needs no computer. It can be carried out with pencil and paper in an hour. It works for national economies, for companies, for ecological systems, for personal life situations. It is not modelling in the sense of prediction — it is a structural analysis that shows where one can intervene and where one cannot. Anyone who has once applied it to a real example no longer thinks in linear chains of cause and effect. He sees networks.

The second invention is the game Ökolopoly, later in a computer version, ecopolicy. It simulates a country — the industrial country Kybernetien, the emerging country Kybinnien or the developing country Kyborien — with eight areas of life linked to one another non-linearly. In each round the player has a limited number of action points and can influence only four of the eight areas directly. The other four change indirectly. Whoever plays the game for the first time fails within the first three rounds. Whoever plays it for the tenth time begins to sense what complex systems mean. Whoever plays it for the hundredth time has developed an intuition that no textbook can convey.

There is no pedagogically equivalent course in networked thinking. Computer games of this depth are rare. Educational software from the 1990s and early 2000s of this didactic clarity has all but ceased to exist. Vester’s game is methodologically the best there is — and today it is barely obtainable.

III. The Ecopolicyade

Between 2004 and 2012 something happened in Germany that rarely happens. Two teachers from Schleswig-Holstein, Hans-Werner Hansen and Wilfried D. John, brought the game into lessons at their secondary-modern school in Malente. The pupils became good at it. In 2004 they challenged the district council — and won. In 2005 they challenged the Schleswig-Holstein state parliament — and won. They challenged the Bundestag — and won. The Bundestag duel of 19 January 2005 was reported on the heute-journal and on the Kinderkanal. Secondary-modern pupils from a small town in eastern Holstein beat members of the Bundestag at steering a model state. Repeatably. Over several years.

In 2008 this turned into the nationwide competition. More than 175,000 pupils and teachers took part in the years up to 2012. Patronage came from Klaus Wowereit in Berlin and Christian Wulff in Lower Saxony — that is, across party lines. The Federal Agency for Civic Education supported the project. The Bundestag member Bettina Hagedorn was involved. National finals were held in the Bundestag’s Jakob-Kaiser-Haus and Marie-Elisabeth-Lüders-Haus.

That is not one school project among many. It is a proof. The proof that German secondary-modern pupils can systematically beat members of the Bundestag at steering complex systems. The proof that cybernetic thinking can be learned, from year seven onwards. The proof that German education policy had something it was not really supposed to have — a working method for the future competence that has been talked about ever since PISA.

With the 2013/14 school year the competition was discontinued in Germany and Austria. Wikipedia records this in one sentence, without a reason. The software was withdrawn from regular sale. The board game has been unavailable for years. Anyone who wants to work with ecopolicy today buys a second-hand private copy through online marketplaces. What was within reach of 175,000 pupils has by 2026 become nostalgia.

IV. What happened

After his death in 2003 the rights to Vester’s work passed to his widow and estate and were transferred to Malik Management Zentrum St. Gallen AG — a private Swiss consultancy headed by the management theorist Fredmund Malik. Until 2012 the Ecopolicyade ran in Germany with Malik as licensing partner, which from the outside looked like support. From 2012 Malik took the lead in international expansion. From 2013 the German competition was ended. Currently the project is run in Vietnam, Australia, Poland, Austria and the Netherlands — markets in which solvent education ministries or NGOs can bear licence fees. Germany falls outside this grid because German schools have no budget for Swiss consultancy software.

I have no insider documents and no evidence of a deliberate intention to shut it down. I cannot look into the minds of those involved. What I can observe is the result. For over twenty years Vester’s work has lain with a Swiss consultancy. The software is no longer regularly obtainable. Nor is the board game. The Sensitivity Model is accessible only through consultancy contracts whose terms are not public. Anyone who wants to use Vester’s method for education, for public administration, for research or simply for their own thinking finds no way in without commercialisation. What was conceived as a German educational asset has become an international consultancy product.

Whether one calls this shutting down, optimisation of exploitation or simply neglect is a question of vocabulary, not of substance. The result is the same. An educational movement of 175,000 participants was placed in final storage at a Swiss consultancy. Vester’s legacy has become largely inaccessible to the German public.

That is not the allegation of a scandal. It is the description of a structural logic. Inheritance structures work like this. Licensing economies work like this. Whoever inherits a work optimises its exploitation. What pays, runs. What does not pay is switched off. Nobody decided to take the best tool for networked thinking away from German secondary-modern schools. It simply happened.

V. Why Vester matters more today than in 1976

The world has not become simpler since 1976. It is more networked, faster, more unstable. What Vester described in Ballungsgebiete in der Krise as looming complexity is today the everyday reality of politics, the economy, education. Whoever casts a vote in 2026, whoever makes an investment decision, whoever writes a curriculum is dealing with interactions that nobody can survey any more without cybernetic discipline.

Three present-day phenomena show the relevance of Vester’s method especially clearly.

First, the climate crisis. It is the textbook example of an effect with a time delay. What we emit today takes effect in thirty years. Whoever thinks linearly has already lost here — he reacts to what he sees and not to what he has set off. Vester’s model of the car that skids in the bends because the correction always comes one beat too late is the exact description of global climate policy over the last thirty years. Whoever has understood this once looks differently at the Paris Agreement, at the German climate targets, at the energy transition. He sees not the failure of individual actors but an architecture that produces the failure.

Second, the AI transformation. What the tech corporations are building today is a classic example of positive feedback without self-regulation. More investment attracts more talent, more talent produces better models, better models attract more investment, more investment attracts more talent — a circle that runs until it hits a limit. Vester’s candle flame, which eventually goes out because the wick can no longer supply the wax being consumed, is the most precise metaphor for the AI investment spiral of 2026. Whoever has seen through this knows that the question is not whether the system will collapse but when, and with what collateral damage.

Third, German politics. Vester’s model of action points is an achievement of political theory that appears in no German political science textbook. In every period a government has a limited amount of energy for action — money, trust, attention, political capital. Whoever spends everything on one portfolio has nothing in the next period. Whoever invests correctly gets back more than he spent. This simple observation explains the exhaustion of German governments since Schröder. They have spent action points without reproducing them. Today a coalition governs that has almost no action points left. Vester’s model would have predicted this, had anyone used it.

VI. The method is not locked away

What Malik can block is the convenient software implementation. What Malik cannot block is the method itself. Drawing networks of effects on paper, connecting variables with arrows, rating strengths, identifying critical and active points, tracking down feedback loops — these are techniques that have been in the public domain since Vester published them. No one can stop anyone from applying them. No one can stop anyone from developing them further. No one can stop anyone from spreading them free of charge in schools, universities, adult education and NGOs.

Vester’s most important books are still available as paperbacks. Die Kunst vernetzt zu denken is in its fifth edition at dtv. Neuland des Denkens in its twelfth. Phänomen Stress in its eighteenth. Denken, Lernen, Vergessen in its thirtieth. Unsere Welt — ein vernetztes System in its eleventh. Whoever wants to learn the method can do so. What is missing is the didactic implementation that Vester had built in the form of a game and software and that is no longer regularly available today.

This implementation would have to be rebuilt. Not as a clone of the software — the licence clearly lies with Malik. But as methodical guidance showing how to apply Vester’s procedure to concrete German problems. Schoolbooks with exercises. Online courses with exercises on networks of effects. The Federal Agency for Civic Education with materials on sensitivity analysis. University seminars with modelling examples from German industrial policy. That would be the reopening that Vester himself would have wanted.

VII. A forgotten thinker and his significance

Vester is not forgotten because he thought wrongly. He is forgotten because after his death his work ended up in an exploitation structure that understood dissemination as loss. This structure did not arise from ill will but from the normal economics of inheritance. The consequence is nonetheless concrete damage — a generation of German pupils that no longer has the best tool for networked thinking in its hands, a German politics that acts without cybernetic discipline and is surprised that it gets tangled up in its own measures, a German industry that is drowning in complexity and buys consultancy for it instead of learning the thinking itself.

Whoever wants to repair the damage need not wait until Malik’s licence expires or until an heir releases the rights. He can read Vester’s books, learn the method, apply it to his own subjects, pass it on. He can work with pencil and paper instead of waiting for software that will not come. He can teach himself what Vester developed for German secondary-modern pupils — with the advantage that, as an adult, he knows the fields of application better than a fourteen-year-old.

That is the small form of reappropriation open to everyone. It is the first step. The second step would be a German education policy that makes cybernetic thinking a permanent part of the curriculum — without licence dependency, with its own materials, with teacher training, with competitions. The Ecopolicyade showed that this is feasible and that German pupils respond to it. Giving it up was a loss. Rebuilding it in a new form would not be nostalgia but an investment in the one ability that will really be decisive in the next thirty years.

Frederic Vester built a tool against the megamachine. The megamachine absorbed the tool in 2013. The task is to bring it back. Not out of piety towards a dead man, but because what he developed is needed now. More than in 1976. More than in 2003. Right now.
