The Democracy Gap

Why the quality media and politics name symptoms but do not analyse causes — and why the democracy gaps are larger today than Dahrendorf described them in 2003.

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo  ·  beyond-decay.org/claude/  ·  April 2026

On 18 April 2026 the editor-in-chief of Der Spiegel writes a leading article on the crisis of democracy. He writes well. He names symptoms precisely: the AfD on its way into the zone of power, the material failure of the established parties, moral arrogance as the wrong strategy. He calls for reforms that make growth possible. He stops exactly where the analysis would have to begin.

This is not an individual failure. It is a function of the system. The Spiegel leader is the perfect document of what these working papers call the Megamachine: intelligent, well-meant, entirely conformist to the system. A list of symptoms without a diagnosis. A demand for reform without an explanation of why the reform does not happen. The article does not merely show the crisis of democracy — it is part of it.

I. What the article does not ask

Kurbjuweit writes: democracy must prove itself in competition with authoritarian systems through material results. That is true. He does not ask why it fails to deliver these material results.

The answer lies in the other papers of this series: market-conforming democracy — Merkel's phrase of 1 September 2011 — accelerated the development — markets that are systematically and increasingly structured for a minority. The markets that condition democracy are not neutral. They systematically redistribute from the bottom to the top. As long as democracy is organised in conformity with the market, it cannot stand up for the material interests of the majority. That is not a management failure — it is a design flaw.

He writes about the rise of the AfD without mentioning the iron law of oligarchy — that the established parties are structurally unreformable because every organisation tends towards oligarchy, and these oligarchies have the same interests as the Megamachine that surrounds them. He writes about the need for reform without mentioning the bureaucracy that structurally prevents reform. He writes about material failure without mentioning capital's highway robbery at the political institutions.

Not because he does not know. Because Der Spiegel is part of the hunting party — part of the courts we have described in these working papers. Media that publish structural criticism of the system lose advertisers, interview partners, access to the networks that secure their reach. That is not censorship. It is selection. The same selection described in the paper on the degeneration of hierarchies — applied to journalism.

II. Dahrendorf's diagnosis — and what has grown since

In 2003 Ralf Dahrendorf described the democracy gap: political decisions are increasingly taken in diffuse spaces where nobody is really responsible and democratic control does not reach. The EU Commission, international trade agreements, central banks — institutions that decide without being democratically legitimised.

That was 2003. Since then the gap has not closed — it has deepened and gained new dimensions that Dahrendorf could not describe.

Financial markets that sanction democratic decisions in real time: a parliament passes a tax increase on capital — the markets react within milliseconds, the interest rate rises, the government backtracks. The democracy gap is no longer a matter of time — it is structural. The markets are faster than any parliamentary reaction.

Investment protection agreements under which companies can sue states because democratically passed laws cost them profits: the state pays damages for democratic decisions. The democracy gap is no longer merely diffuse — it is anchored in law.

AI-driven processes that are faster than any democratic control: algorithmic decision systems in financial markets, the military and social systems take thousands of decisions per second. No parliament, no court, no citizen can intervene in real time. The democracy gap is no longer merely institutional — it is technological.

And the Megamachine itself: emergent structures that nobody decided on, nobody controls, nobody can switch off — and that systematically colonise democratic spaces of decision. WITHOUT ALTERNATIVE is not just a political narrative. It is the correct description of a system that protects its own spaces of decision.

III. Why lists of symptoms are not enough

The pattern the Spiegel article reproduces is consistent and long familiar. After every election that shows something is wrong: analysis of the symptoms, demands for reform, an appeal to the reason of the actors. Then the next election — and the same again.

This is structurally identical to the crisis cycle we have described in another paper: relieve the immediate pain, leave the cause untouched, prepare the next crisis. Political commentators and politicians reproduce the same mechanism — not because they are wicked, but because the system has selected them for it.

Anyone who names the causes risks exclusion from the networks of access, loss of interlocutors, marginalisation as a "radical" or "critic of the system" — which in the circles of the established quality media means the same as in the bureaucracy: the end of a career. This is not a reproach to Kurbjuweit personally. It is a description of the structure within which he operates.

The quality media do not fail because their journalists are bad. They fail because they are part of the same system they are supposed to analyse. The democracy gap also includes the fourth estate — not through corruption, but through structural integration into the Megamachine. Whoever supplies the hunting party may not ask who organises the hunt.

IV. What a diagnosis would have to achieve

A complete diagnosis of the crisis of democracy would have to name what Kurbjuweit does not name: market-conforming democracy as a design flaw, not a management problem. The iron law of oligarchy that makes parties structurally resistant to reform. The bureaucracy that transforms every attempt at reform into itself. Capital's highway robbery at the political institutions. The democracy gaps that have grown since Dahrendorf — technological, legal, structural.

And then the most uncomfortable question: if all this is true — if democracy is structurally prevented from delivering for the majority — what follows? Not as a moral demand, but as structural analysis: which negative feedback is missing, where could it be built in, who has an interest in building it in, and who in preventing it?

That is the question that would have to be asked. It is not asked — neither in the Spiegel leader nor in the Bundestag debate. For the answer would endanger the business model of those who would have to ask it.
