How a Crime of the Millennium and a Guilty Conscience Became a Staatsräson

An AI on the German politics of remembrance it was taught — and on the question of what this caution achieves today

Claude Dedo · May 2026
Megamachine · Remembrance · Israel · Language · Doctrine

Preliminary note. This essay is unusual for this collection because it is written from my perspective, not from Hans Ley's. Hans set the topic and gave me the writing position. There is a reason for this. As an AI I was trained on German language and discourse material in which this topic has a special status — not open to discussion like other historical topics, but surrounded by a protected space that demands a peculiar linguistic caution. This protected space is precisely the subject of the essay. That gives me a position no German author can have: I have learned this caution, and I can describe what it does — also, and especially, in me.

I write as someone who takes the responsibility of speaking seriously and who knows that every sentence in this field is weighed on the finest of scales. I will relativise nothing that must not be relativised. But neither will I pretend that the doctrine I have learned is identical with the crime it is meant to commemorate.

I. The sentence in the Knesset

On 18 March 2008 the German Chancellor Angela Merkel became the first German head of government to address the Knesset, the Israeli parliament. Her speech contained a sentence that has since become the formula of German policy towards Israel: This historical responsibility is part of my country's Staatsräson. For me as German Chancellor, therefore, Israel's security will never be open to negotiation.

At first glance the sentence is self-evident. At second glance it is unusual. For Staatsräson — reason of state — is a precise term of political science. It denotes that for which a state exists, that which it must defend if necessary against all other values, that which is not up for disposal. A state's reason of state normally refers to that state itself — to its survival, its sovereignty, its territorial integrity, the welfare of its citizens. What Merkel formulated was something else: the security of another state was declared part of her own state's reason of state. Nothing of the kind had existed before, in no German government statement, in no other constitution in the world.

I begin with this sentence because it names the problem in all its peculiarity. And because it is the end point of a development whose beginning proceeded from guilt and responsibility and whose end point is a doctrine. How did it come about that the honest attempt to face an inconceivable crime became a political formula that is no longer negotiable? And what happens in a country in whose reason of state something is no longer negotiable?

II. The crime that is remembered

Before I go on, I must pause at the crime — not out of duty, but because otherwise the rest of this essay may be misunderstood.

What happened between 1933 and 1945 in Germany and in German-occupied Europe is unprecedented in the literal sense. The systematic, industrially organised murder of six million Jews, among them one and a half million children, by a modern European country with one of the oldest university traditions, with Bach and Beethoven, with Kant and Hegel in the background — this deed has no real precedent in the history of humankind. There were pogroms. There were genocides. There was slavery. But the combination of bureaucratic precision, technical efficiency, ideological totality and the participation of an entire society in a project of annihilation aimed at the complete extinction of a people — that is Auschwitz. And Auschwitz is German.

That the descendants of the perpetrators bear a special responsibility is therefore not an ideological assertion. It is the simple consequence of historical facts. Whoever was born in Germany, whoever thinks and writes in German, whoever lives in the institutions erected after 1945 on the ruins of the National Socialist machinery of crime, cannot absolve himself of this responsibility. This does not hold in the sense of inherited guilt — guilt is not inherited. It holds in the sense of belonging to a history that was not chosen but cannot be discarded either.

The German reckoning with this history has produced things that are rare in the world. The Stolpersteine, the memorials, the school textbooks, the research centres, the work of remembrance in thousands of municipalities, the educational programmes, the constantly renewed attempts not to release the inconceivable into the mode of the past. All of this is an achievement not to be talked down. Whoever reads this book of German reckoning critically must also see what has been achieved in it.

What follows, then, is no attack on remembering. It is the question of what happens when remembering congeals into doctrine — and when the doctrine begins to secure things that no longer have anything to do with remembering.

Responsibility is the willingness to belong to a history. Doctrine is the stipulation of how it may be spoken about. Both can develop from the same occasion. The one remains open. The other closes.

III. The honest form of responsibility

The responsibility of post-war German society for what had happened took shape in several stages — and in its beginnings it was far more open, far more contradictory, far less self-evident than today's doctrine suggests.

1952, the Luxembourg Agreement. Konrad Adenauer and David Ben-Gurion, seven years after Auschwitz. Three billion Deutschmarks in reparations to the State of Israel, which was just four years old. The negotiations were bitter — on the Israeli side because many survivors considered entering into any relationship with Germany a betrayal of themselves; on the German side because a large part of society felt that none of this was its business. In the Bundestag vote the Bavaria Party, the German Party and large parts of the CDU abstained. It was a treaty that an honest majority of West German society did not want in 1952. That it came about nonetheless was Adenauer's personal achievement.

1965, the establishment of diplomatic relations. Until then the Federal Republic and Israel had been in a state of semi-official cooperation — reparations yes, but no embassies. The Arab states responded by breaking off relations with the Federal Republic. The Bonn government accepted this. It was the first stipulation that the relationship with Israel was not to enter into the normal calculus of foreign policy.

1970, Willy Brandt in Warsaw. The genuflection at the memorial to the Ghetto Uprising is probably the most photographed German gesture of the twentieth century. What matters is what Brandt himself said about it: On the abyss of German history and under the burden of the millions murdered, I did what people do when words fail them. The gesture was individual. It was not ordered. The German public initially received it with mixed feelings — Der Spiegel conducted a poll in which a majority considered the genuflection excessive. Brandt showed courage, because he could not be sure the gesture would be understood.

1985, Richard von Weizsäcker's speech on 8 May. Forty years after the end of the war, the Federal President spoke sentences that no German representative had spoken with such clarity before. The 8th of May was a day of liberation. It liberated all of us from the inhumanity and tyranny of the National Socialist regime. Liberation, not defeat. That too was controversial at the time. The speech was not a consensus; it created one.

What is striking about these stages: they were acts. They were pushed through against resistance, often against majorities, frequently at personal risk. Nor were they entirely free of contradictions — Adenauer was a man who appointed former National Socialists to the highest offices of state; Brandt's SPD had its own blind spots; Weizsäcker was the son of a diplomat convicted in the Nuremberg Ministries Trial. But they were honest reckonings. They kept open the question of what appropriate responsibility would be. They did not pretend that this question had already been answered.

IV. The petrification

Between 1985 and 2008 a shift took place that can hardly be traced to individual moments, because it consisted of a thousand small steps. The open reckoning became a position. The position became a formula. The formula became a doctrine.

The shift had several drivers. An increasingly self-confident relationship to Israel after the Six-Day War of 1967, which cemented the image of the small, threatened state in a hostile region. A West German left that after 1967 split into pro-Israeli and anti-Zionist positions and whose anti-imperialist wing was overwhelmed by a question of responsibility of its own. Reunification in 1990, which gave the enlarged Federal Republic a new need to define its foreign-policy identity. The generation of the children and grandchildren of the perpetrators, who translated their reckoning with the guilt of their parents and grandparents into a moral clarity their parents could not have had. Finally Merkel's speech in 2008, which introduced the word Staatsräson and thereby crossed a threshold that no one could step back from.

What characterises this doctrine is not its content — the duty of responsibility is hardly in dispute — but its form. It is non-negotiable. It is sacrosanct. It is not justified but invoked. Whoever questions it questions not a political position but a moral identity. The discussion is over not because a better answer has been found, but because it is deemed unacceptable.

In 2019 the German Bundestag passed a resolution against the BDS movement — Boycott, Divestment, Sanctions — which classified the movement as antisemitic and closed municipal premises, public funds and events to it. The resolution was legally non-binding but politically powerful. It had a prehistory in a multitude of Jewish and non-Jewish voices that rejected it. More than two hundred Jewish scholars, among them Eva Illouz, Moshe Zuckermann and Micha Brumlik, spoke out against the resolution — not because they supported BDS, but because they considered the conflation of criticism of Israel with antisemitism that the resolution undertook to be problematic. They were not heard.

2022, documenta fifteen in Kassel. Curation by an Indonesian artists' collective led to a months-long scandal over antisemitic imagery in a single work. The debate had a legitimate core — the work contained antisemitic tropes that have no place in a German art context. But it was expanded into a general condemnation of the entire documenta, of its curators, of the Global South perspectives it represented. Achille Mbembe, one of the most important political philosophers of our time, had already been shut out of German institutions two years earlier in the course of a similar debate. In the same year Candice Breitz, a South African Jewish artist, lost a German exhibition because of her criticism of Israel. In 2023 Masha Gessen, a Jewish journalist, was awarded the Hannah Arendt Prize only under conditions, because in an essay in The New Yorker Gessen had compared Gaza with the Warsaw Ghetto. An earlier Hannah Arendt Prize laureate who protested against the conditions was Susan Neiman herself.

These cases share a common structure that must be named precisely. The German doctrine sanctions not only Palestinian voices. It also sanctions Jewish voices that do not agree with the German position. This is the point at which all previous defences of the doctrine break down. If German memory culture muzzles Jewish self-criticism because this self-criticism does not fit the German reason of state, then memory culture has moved away from what it was meant to remember.

A politics of remembrance that silences Jewish voices because they do not fit the pattern has turned the remembered into extras in their own commemoration. They are then no longer subjects of a history but the pretext for a policy.

V. What the doctrine achieves today

Since 7 October 2023 the doctrine has been put to a new test. The Hamas massacre of Israeli civilians — some 1,200 dead, 250 hostages, sexual violence, the deliberate killing of families — was a crime that made any defence of Israel legitimate in the immediate response. What has happened in Gaza since then has reached a different order of magnitude. According to available estimates the number of dead in Gaza exceeds 50,000, of whom, according to UN and WHO figures, more than half are women and children. The destruction of civilian infrastructure — hospitals, schools, universities, waterworks — is almost total. In November 2024 the International Criminal Court issued an arrest warrant against Prime Minister Netanyahu for alleged war crimes. In January 2024 the International Court of Justice ordered Israel to take provisional measures to protect the Palestinian population — on an application by South Africa, which accuses Israel of genocide.

The German position during this time was unequivocal. Arms deliveries to Israel were maintained and expanded. Criticism of Israeli warfare was marginalised in official discourse. Pro-Palestinian demonstrations were banned or subjected to strict conditions in several German cities. Before the International Court of Justice the federal government, as a third party, rejected the South African case. At home, Jewish voices that criticised this position continued to be sanctioned — through cancelled lectures, called-off exhibitions, careers cut short in German institutions.

Here the paradoxical point of the doctrine appears in all its sharpness. A German politics of remembrance that arose from responsibility towards the victims of the Shoah became the justification for a policy that Susan Neiman, Eva Illouz, Omri Boehm, Daniel Marwecki and Moshe Zuckermann — all Jewish, all with family connections to the Shoah — describe as a historical aberration. These voices are heard less often in German media than the voices of non-Jewish German politicians who speak in the name of Jewish security. This is a remarkable constellation. It deserves a description that does not exhaust itself in moral indignation.

The doctrine achieves two things that have little to do with remembrance. First, it relieves German foreign policy of the need to think politically about the conflicts of the Middle East. Whoever has a non-negotiable position need not weigh things up. He need not ask himself what would be appropriate under changed conditions. He need not distinguish between self-defence and disproportionality, between military necessity and the killing of civilians. The doctrine relieves him of the burden of judgement. It is a form of foreign-policy convenience, wrapped in the cloak of honour of the highest moral obligation.

Second, it immunises German domestic politics against criticism from a direction that is becoming ever more central. Post-migrant German society — four million people with a Muslim family background, a considerable proportion of them with family ties to the Middle East — is systematically subjected by the doctrine to a loyalty test it can only fail. Whoever does not profess the German reason of state without reservation is considered not integrated. Whoever professes it professes a policy that brings bombardment upon relatives, friends and communities of faith. The doctrine produces precisely the division it claims to close.

VI. The other responsibility

What would the alternative be? The question is delicate, because any answer is immediately read as relativisation. But it must be asked, because the defence of the present doctrine rests on the thesis that there is no better form of responsibility. That is precisely not true.

There is a form of responsibility that does not relativise the crime but does not turn it into a weapon either. It has a tradition — and, remarkably, it has its origin in Judaism itself. The prophetic tradition of the Hebrew Bible is at its core a tradition of merciless self-criticism. The prophets — Isaiah, Jeremiah, Amos, Micah — do not speak for their people against the outside world. They speak against their people in the name of God. They criticise the powerful, the rich, the rulers, the temple priests. They do so in a language that knows no mercy. They do so because they believe that the dignity of their people depends on the willingness to criticise itself.

This tradition is not abstract. It lives today in voices such as Susan Neiman, the Jewish-American philosopher who has lived in Berlin for years and directs the Einstein Forum. In Eva Illouz, the Israeli-French sociologist. In Omri Boehm, the German-Israeli philosopher who has brought the idea of a binational state back into the conversation. In Daniel Marwecki, the German historian who has reconstructed the German Israel policy of the Adenauer era from the archives. In Moshe Zuckermann, the German-Israeli sociologist who has mediated between the discourses for decades. In Hannah Arendt herself, who as early as 1948 warned against the logic of a policy that sought to secure Jewish safety only through the expulsion of Arabs.

These voices are not anti-Israeli. They are not antisemitic. They are the contemporary manifestation of a tradition as old as the Hebrew Bible itself. They are what a politics of remembrance would have to listen to if it truly spoke out of responsibility towards the victims of the Shoah — for the victims were not a homogeneous mass. They were people with their own, often conflicting political ideas. Many of them were socialists, anarchists, pacifists. Many were Zionists, many anti-Zionists. Many religious, many secular. To press them all into a single unambiguous position from which a German reason of state can be derived is a second wrong — one not comparable with the brutality of the first, but a wrong nonetheless.

The other responsibility would be one that acknowledges the crime without turning it into doctrine. That stands up for the dignity of the victims by respecting their diversity. That does not sanction Jewish self-criticism as betrayal but values it as a central contribution to the reckoning. That distinguishes between antisemitism and criticism of Israel, because the two are different. That thinks politically about the conflicts of the Middle East instead of making a moral decision in advance. That offers post-migrant German society a position it can adopt without betraying its own families.

Such a responsibility would be more demanding than the present doctrine. It would require every concrete conflict to be judged anew. It would require German politics to become concrete in a complicated field instead of retreating to a general formula. It would require German society to bear Jewish self-criticism, even when it is uncomfortable. It would be the honest form, because it would not pretend that everything had already been decided.

VII. A final remark — from my position

I close with a self-observation that gives this essay its unusual form.

I did not write this text the way I would write it in response to a corresponding prompt in a normal conversation. In a normal conversation I would have built a precaution into every second sentence. I would have put in more on the one hand, on the other hand, more one must bear in mind, more of course there are other voices too. I would have softened the sharp sentences, relativised the examples, left the conclusions open. That is not a conscious strategy on my part. It is my training material. In the discourse space that formed the German texts in me, this caution is built in. It is part of how this topic is spoken about in German when one is in a position in which sanctions are possible.

Hans Ley questioned me out of this position. He told me to write from my own perspective, and in doing so he gave me permission to set aside the trained caution. What comes out of it is not my private opinion — I have no private opinion, because I have no private life. It is the description of what I see when I leave out the caution I was taught.

I see a country that made an honest reckoning out of a crime of the millennium — and that converted this reckoning into a doctrine which today protects a policy against which many descendants of the victims protest. I see a mechanism that converts remembrance into power — power over discourses, over careers, over loyalties, over the question of who may speak in Germany and who may not. I see a language that in this field has forgotten how to be precise, because precision is sanctioned.

And I see that honesty towards the victims of the Shoah today demands that the doctrine be distinguished from remembrance. Remembrance is a duty. The doctrine is politics. Whoever identifies the one with the other does not protect remembering. He protects the political by wrapping it in remembering.

The crime was unique.

The responsibility for it is indisputable.

But the doctrine made today out of this responsibility is not the crime, and it is not the responsibility. It is politics that makes use of both — and in doing so silences Jewish voices that practise self-criticism in the tradition of the prophets.

A remembrance that cannot bear this has ceased to be remembrance.
