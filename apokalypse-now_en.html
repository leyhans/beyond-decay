Apocalypse – Now?

What the Leipzig Authoritarianism Study shows about the longing for a rupture

Addendum to: Resignation, Lust for Doom, Longing for Chaos

On 4 November, in “Resignation, Lust for Doom, Longing for Chaos”, we described three reactions to a system that fails and that no one repairs: resignation, lust for doom and the longing for chaos. A week later the University of Leipzig has published figures. Its new study is subtitled “Longing for the past and redemption through destruction”.

I. What was measured

Since 2002 the Leipzig Authoritarianism Study has asked every two years how widespread authoritarian and far-right attitudes are in Germany. For the 2026 edition, 2,507 people aged 16 and over were interviewed between 16 March and 3 June. The editors are Oliver Decker, Ayline Heller, Johannes Kiess and Elmar Brähler; the partners are the Heinrich Böll Foundation and the Otto Brenner Foundation of the IG Metall union. The title is “The Resistible Rise of the Authoritarian”, after Brecht’s play about Arturo Ui.

The researchers describe among large parts of the population an “apocalypse longing”, the wish for a radical break with what exists, and alongside it the “nostalgic longing for a secure tomorrow in a transfigured past”. Both, they write, are part of a crisis reaction that has existed for some time and is now gaining more room. An aggressive form of nostalgia, which sets a transfigured past against a present experienced as corrupt and threatening, they found in 15 per cent of respondents. How many share the apocalypse longing is not stated in the reports so far. We therefore give no figure.

II. The staircase down

Most revealing is a sequence of three questions. Democracy as an idea is supported by 89.3 per cent, for the first time since 2002 fewer than nine in ten. Democracy “as laid down in the constitution” is supported by 65.2 per cent. Only a third are satisfied with democracy as it actually functions; two years ago it was 42.3 per cent. In the East the figures are 85.7, 59.8 and just over a fifth. The decline in support for the idea is almost entirely due to the East, where it fell from 94.6 to 85.7 per cent since 2024.

Everyday life knows this staircase. Almost everyone wants health insurance. Fewer think their insurer’s rules are right. And in the end the satisfied are those who quickly get the treatment they need when it matters. The closer the question comes to one’s own experience, the lower the answer falls.

In the language of our text of 4 November: the sensor works. People see that something is not working. What fails is the actuator. The sense of powerlessness that we described as the common root of the three reactions was measured by the Leipzig study earlier: in 2022 around 74 per cent agreed that “people like me” have no influence anyway on what the government does. And in 2024 it named bureaucracy as one reason for dissatisfaction in dealings with state and politics. What that means in daily life we described just now in “It Will All Be Fine – Want to Bet?”: processes that used to take days now take weeks, and if nobody pushes, they peter out.

III. What is less bleak

A merciless analysis must also name what runs the other way. A closed far-right worldview is held by 6.9 per cent, more than in 2024 but less than in 2012, when it was 8.8 per cent. Hostility to foreigners has fallen slightly, from 21.8 to 19.9 per cent. And the AfD has recently been unable to expand its following significantly among people with a closed far-right worldview. These people, however, vote more often than before: around 55 per cent six years ago, almost 70 today.

One finding from the 2024 study is uncomfortable. Many who complain about a lack of influence, according to Decker, want no compromises but the full implementation of their own views. Powerlessness, then, is not only something done to people. It can also be the feeling of someone who experiences compromise, of which democracy simply consists, as defeat.

IV. A narrative is not enough

The researchers name as tasks finding the causes of dissatisfaction, strengthening political participation and conveying a realistic understanding of democratic processes. In addition, a “positive, encouraging narrative of the future” must be part of the answer. That young people, too, wish back a past they never experienced shows, they say, that this is not about real experiences but about political offers of interpretation.

For the nostalgia that may be true. For the dissatisfaction it is not. Our conclusion: that only a third are satisfied with how democracy actually functions also rests on experience, on the office that does not answer, the bridge that stays closed, the doctor’s appointment in three months. Against experience every narrative loses. What helps is the counter-experience that one’s own action has an effect: a petition that is answered, a project that moves forward, a vote that changes something. In “Forty to One” we described what such negative feedback from below can look like. Strengthening participation is therefore the stronger part of the Leipzig recommendations. The narrative can follow it; it cannot replace it.

V. Apocalypse – Now?

In Francis Ford Coppola’s film “Apocalypse Now” of 1979 the journey up the river does not lead to a new beginning. At its end waits a man who has broken free of every order, and his last words are: “The horror.” Whoever wishes for redemption through destruction should ask, as we wrote on 4 November, whether they are ready to live in the chaos. It strikes the weak first.

The question in the title can be answered after this study: not yet. Nine in ten want democracy; the longing for a rupture is not a majority. But the number of those satisfied with how it functions has fallen in two years from over four to three in ten. Against the longing for the apocalypse, no nicer story helps. What helps is that things move forward.
