Poor for Six Days

How Mathias Döpfner was given a billion and the tax on it was waived

In September 2020 Friede Springer gives the chief executive of her publishing house shares worth one billion euros. The gift tax would come to around 500 million. Internal documents of Deutsche Bank now show how it almost entirely disappeared through a purchase six days earlier. An addendum to “The Fiscal Privilege of the Underperformers”.

I. The gift

In “The Fiscal Privilege of the Underperformers” we cited a figure: in 2025, 3.7 billion euros of inheritance and gift tax were waived in Germany through the so-called needs-based exemption test (Verschonungsbedarfsprüfung). A figure from the statistics does not show how this works in a single case. Since 17 November 2026 there is an example with every step.

It comes from the “Inside Deutsche Bank” leak, which more than fifty newsrooms have analysed. In February 2021, bank staff circulated six internal documents under the code name “Project Heritage”. They describe how Friede Springer, widow of the publishing house’s founder, made her confidant Mathias Döpfner a co-owner of Axel Springer. The Vienna newspaper Der Standard has analysed them.

On 24 September 2020, Döpfner buys 4.1 per cent of the company from Friede Springer or her foundation for 276 million euros. He does not have to pay until mid-2021. Six days later, on 30 September, Friede Springer gives him around 15 per cent, worth about one billion euros. Together with the just over three per cent he already owned, Döpfner now holds 22 per cent, exactly as much as Friede Springer and her foundations. Value at the time: around one and a half billion euros.

A gift of this size would normally be taxed in Germany at around 50 per cent, in this case about 500 million euros.

II. Poor on the key date

The needs-based exemption test is set out in section 28a of the German Inheritance and Gift Tax Act. It applies when someone inherits or is given privileged business assets worth more than 26 million euros. The tax on them is waived to the extent that the recipient proves he cannot pay it from his “available assets”. Half of what he owns outside the privileged business assets on the day of the gift counts as available. The idea behind it is reasonable: someone who inherits a business should not have to sell it to pay the tax.

The key date decides. What Döpfner owned outside the privileged assets on 30 September 2020 counted at half its value. Six days earlier he had committed himself to paying 276 million euros, for shares in the same company. According to Der Standard, this purchase is the trick: on paper it turns the future billionaire into a man who cannot pay the tax. The tax is then waived. Not deferred, not spread over instalments, but waived.

A tax adviser who has looked after wealthy clients for decades and read the 15-page gift contract for Der Standard says the contract is “clearly tailored to tax avoidance, there is no other way to put it. It is the central point.” Before the contracts were signed, the parties obtained binding rulings from four tax offices.

III. What Springer says

When Manager Magazin reported in autumn 2020 that the gift had probably remained “largely tax-free”, a spokesman said tax avoidance had not been the aim. Today the company states on behalf of Döpfner, Friede Springer and itself that everything was done “in strictest compliance with tax law” and after a binding ruling from the tax office. The accusation of improper tax optimisation is “absurd”, and the gift tax was “correctly assessed and paid”. And: “One may criticise applicable tax law, but not those who abide by it.”

Whether Döpfner ultimately paid any tax of any kind, and how much, cannot be established, according to Der Standard. We do not accuse him of breaking the law. Four tax offices agreed in advance.

Incidentally, Deutsche Bank chief Christian Sewing wanted to grant the loan for the 276 million. He could not prevail internally, partly because Döpfner already owed the bank tens of millions. The money eventually came from the Swiss bank Julius Baer.

IV. How the state treats others

We take the company at its word and criticise the applicable law. The comparison makes it clearest.

A student transferred 7,000 euros to her mother two weeks before applying for BAföG, Germany’s state student grant. The money was still counted as her own assets. The Chemnitz Administrative Court ruled so, and the Higher Administrative Court of Saxony upheld it in 2010. Administrative courts have ruled this way for decades. In 1983 the Federal Administrative Court held that assets someone gives away abusively before applying continue to be attributed to them. The courts treat the mere closeness in time between transfer and application as a weighty indication. With student grants, the state therefore looks not only at the key date but also at what someone did shortly before to look poorer on that day.

Döpfner gave nothing away, he bought, and legally his case is different. But the question is the same: may someone arrange his assets shortly before the key date so that the state asks less of him? For a student with 7,000 euros the answer is no, and the authorities investigate. For a chief executive with a billion the answer is yes, and four tax offices confirm it in writing beforehand.

V. The objection, tested on a case

In the original essay we took the strongest objection to our thesis seriously: the exemption is meant to prevent a family business from being sold or broken up merely so that the heirs can pay the tax. The Döpfner case allows this objection to be tested against reality.

Döpfner is not an heir taking over a family business. He had been the company’s salaried chief executive for almost twenty years and received a gift from its owner. Without the waiver he would have had to sell some of the shares or finance the tax another way. For the company itself, its jobs and its newspapers, that would have changed nothing. Nor was his neediness on the key date caused by fate. It was the result of a purchase he had concluded himself six days earlier.

Our conclusion: here the law does not protect a business from being sold. It protects a private fortune from tax, and the neediness it requires can be manufactured.

VI. The ruling is pending

On 13 October 2026 the Federal Constitutional Court heard arguments on the exemption of business assets. In 2014 it had already declared parts of the then rules unconstitutional because they spared large fortunes too generously. The needs-based exemption test was the legislature’s answer to that ruling in 2016. It was meant to ensure that only those who need relief are spared.

The Döpfner case shows what has become of that intention. In need of relief is whoever signed the right contracts on the right day.
