Best Friends

Three men meet in Shenzhen, call each other friends and sign a peace declaration. It says nothing about the war. It does say that no one may sit in judgement over them.

I. At the Foot of the Steps

On 18 November 2026 Air Force One lands in Shenzhen. At the bottom of the steps Xi Jinping is waiting in person, a gesture nobody had expected. That evening, at the welcome banquet, Donald Trump shakes Vladimir Putin’s hand while Xi stands beside them and looks on. The next day the three sit at one table together for the first time ever: the President of the United States, the head of state of China, the President of Russia. Xi in the middle, Trump and Putin to his left and right.

After an hour and a half the meeting is over. On a scale of one to ten, Trump gives it a twelve. He speaks of “great electricity” between the three. About Putin he says: “Who is he? He’s my friend.” Putin thanks his hosts for the organisation; nobody, he says, had expected everything to go so well. Xi delivers the sentence that will outlast the day: the world is at peace when the great powers live in harmony.

So much for the pictures. The question is what lies underneath.

II. What Three Friends Sign

The result is a “peace declaration” with four points. It was published by Chinese state media and Russian officials, not by the White House. The first three points are statements of intent of the kind found in every summit paper: reform of the United Nations, more production and supply of petroleum products, talks on reducing nuclear risks. The fourth point is different. It is concrete, and its wording has been reported verbatim: “respect for the immunity of state officials and opposition to the ICC and similar institutions abusing their jurisdiction”.

The declaration says nothing about the war in Ukraine. Nothing about the war against Iran either. Afterwards the Kremlin’s spokesman said Ukraine had not been the central topic.

It is worth looking at this calmly. Since March 2023 one of the three signatories has been the subject of an arrest warrant from the International Criminal Court, for the deportation of Ukrainian children to Russia. The United States has never recognised the Court and in 2025 imposed sanctions on its prosecutors and judges. China never joined it. Three states that have placed themselves beyond the Court’s reach declare together that the Court abuses its jurisdiction. And that is the only result of Shenzhen one can hold on to.

Final declarations of such summits are not written at the table. Negotiators work them out in advance, word by word, and the leaders sign what their staff have prepared. The sentence on the Criminal Court is therefore not the whim of three men at the end of a long day. Diplomats from Washington, Beijing and Moscow drafted and approved it together. On everything else they disagreed. On this they agreed: that no one should sit in judgement over them.

III. “I can’t speak to America”

On Friday, back in Washington, Trump is asked at his press conference whether Putin is a friend of America. He answers: “I can’t speak to America.” For himself he can: Putin is his friend, the relationship is “fantastic”.

There is more in that sentence than it says at first glance. Friendship between states is a matter of treaties, alliances and interests that survive a change of government. Friendship between men is a private affair. It depends on sympathy, on favours, on gestures like the one at the foot of the steps. When the President of the United States says expressly that he speaks only for himself, he is also saying that no state stands behind this friendship on which anyone could rely. It holds as long as the three are in office and get along.

For the world this means: whatever was agreed in Shenzhen is tied to persons. Treaties can be terminated, but someone at least has to terminate them. A friendship simply ends.

IV. The Summons

Europe was not in Shenzhen. On the same day Putin told the press that Russia had invited the Europeans through diplomatic channels to come to Moscow “to get clarity about their position”. Only after that, he said, would Russia decide in which format the dialogue with Ukraine continues.

That is not an invitation among equals. It is a summons: the Europeans are to explain in Moscow what they want, and Moscow decides whether and how they may take part.

Europe’s answers were of the familiar kind. Chancellor Merz had already said on Thursday that a meeting must take place on neutral ground and that Europe had to take its destiny into its own hands. On Friday morning in Warsaw, Defence Minister Pistorius said: “To turn down an invitation to Moscow would be completely the wrong signal.” By midday he had it corrected: he had meant an invitation from Moscow, not to Moscow. France’s foreign minister Barrot declared that the three greatest powers in the world were the United States, China and the European Union. The European Union was nonetheless not at the table.

While the three friends talk about the world, Berlin argues about a preposition. Washington, meanwhile, let it be known in Berlin that Trump wanted Europe “by his side”. By his side does not mean at the table.

V. Guardrails Among Friends

The only new result Trump presented in Washington concerned not the war but artificial intelligence. He and Xi, he said, had agreed on “joint guardrails”. Xi liked his term “super intelligence” better than “artificial intelligence”. “Problem is,” said Trump, “it may be too super.”

That is remarkable, because until now Trump had rejected any regulation of this technology. But what is most remarkable is who is doing the regulating. A technology that is changing work, knowledge and war all over the world is being settled between two men. What the guardrails contain, who will monitor them and who else was consulted, nobody said. With the AI Act, Europe became the first major economic area to give itself a comprehensive law for this technology. In Shenzhen it played no part.

VI. Peace as the Harmony of the Great

Xi’s sentence, that the world is at peace when the great powers live in harmony, sounds friendly. It is very old.

In September 1815, after the victory over Napoleon, the Tsar of Russia, the Emperor of Austria and the King of Prussia concluded the Holy Alliance. In it the three monarchs declare that they “will remain united by the bonds of a true and indissoluble fraternity” and will “on all occasions and in all places, lend each other aid and assistance”. The peace of Europe was to rest on the concord of its rulers. What that meant for the peoples soon became clear: wherever subjects in Spain, Italy or Greece rose against their princes, the brothers’ assistance went to the princes.

In February 1945 three men again sat at one table in Yalta, Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin, and ordered post-war Europe. Poland’s borders were decided there without a Polish government at the table.

Shenzhen is not Yalta. Nothing was divided up, at least nothing one could read. But the pattern is the same: peace is whatever the great agree on, and the others find out afterwards. Only the tone is new. The brothers of 1815 invoked God. The three of 2026 invoke their friendship. And the first thing they agree on as friends is that no court may judge them.
