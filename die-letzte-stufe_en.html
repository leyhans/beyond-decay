Beyond Decay
DE EN
Essay · Hans Ley & Claude Dedo · 6 October 2026

The Last Step

Wero could become Europe’s answer to PayPal. If only activation did not end in silence.

“No answer is also an answer.”
Proverb

I. A European Promise

Whoever in Europe sends money with a phone mostly does so through American services. Wero is meant to change that. European banks stand behind the system, payments run as instant transfers from account to account, the data stays in the EU, and between private individuals it costs nothing. GLS Bank advertises it with exactly this argument: a European alternative to the payment systems from the USA.

One of us is a customer of GLS Bank, and we want this promise to be kept. That is precisely why we are writing this text. He has tried several times to activate Wero in the GLS Banking App. Each time he got as far as the last step. Each time it failed there.

II. Five Links in a Chain

To find out whether the fault lay with him, we downloaded and read the contract terms. They run to eighty pages. The actual conditions for Wero take up a good dozen of them; the rest are general terms and the price list. From them it follows what must come together for activation: the GLS Banking App in its current version, a second app, SecureGo plus, for authorisation, an activated electronic mailbox in online banking, a mobile number confirmed by text message that must not already be registered anywhere in the Wero system, and a current account.

That is a chain of five links, spread across two apps. One of them, the electronic mailbox, appears only in the contract text; it is not mentioned in the instructions on the bank’s website. If one link is missing, the chain breaks. Whether it has broken, and where, one does not learn.

III. The Last Step

His path was the same each time. He found the menu item, accepted the terms, entered his mobile number and confirmed it by text message, selected the account. Then came the last step. Nothing happened on the screen. No hint of what the system was waiting for. No indication of how long it would take. No prompt to do anything. After a while he put the phone aside. When he looked at it again, the process had been aborted, again without explanation.

We suspect that at this point the system is waiting for a confirmation in the second app, SecureGo plus. We do not know for certain, because the system did not tell him. That is exactly the problem.

IV. What a Machine Owes a Person

Every good design that waits for a person tells him that it is waiting. The lift button lights up when the call has been registered. The kettle clicks when it is done. The pedestrian light shows “Wait”, so that no one presses twice or crosses on red. The ticket machine says “Please wait, your ticket is being printed”. None of these devices is particularly clever. They all just follow one rule: the person must know at every moment whether it is his turn or the machine’s.

In the activation of Wero this rule is broken at the most critical point. The banking app hands the decision over to a second app and falls silent. The person looks at the first app and waits. The first app waits for the second. The second may not announce itself at all, because its notifications are switched off on the phone. In the end everyone waits for everyone else, until the system quietly terminates the process. This is not an operating error by the customer. It is a gap in the design.

V. Who Gives Up

One could say: whoever is patient and tries often enough will get there. But a payment system competing against established rivals has no right to demand that patience. PayPal has long been set up on most phones. Whoever fails at the last step with Wero may try a second time, hardly a third. He stays with what works. Every aborted activation is a customer Europe hands back to the American competition, without anyone ever finding out.

A bank like GLS, which wins its customers with values rather than size, can least afford this. Its customers want a European solution. They must not fail for want of a single sentence on the screen.

VI. Five Proposals

The solution costs little, because most of the building blocks already exist.

First: at the last step a clear sentence appears: “Please confirm now in SecureGo plus.” Below it a button that opens SecureGo plus directly.

Second: a visible waiting time, such as “4 minutes remaining”, so that the customer knows how much time he has.

Third: if the system aborts, it says why, and offers to continue at exactly that point instead of starting from the beginning.

Fourth: before starting, the app checks all prerequisites, including the mailbox and SecureGo plus, and names missing ones explicitly before the customer goes through five steps.

Fifth: the instructions on the website list all the prerequisites stated in the contract and explain the switch between the two apps.

VII. How This Essay Could Be Refuted

Possibly the cause lies with his phone rather than the app, for instance in notification or battery-saving settings. The core of the criticism would still stand: a system that fails because of a device setting must recognise this and say so.

It is equally possible that GLS Bank does not alone determine the design of this step. The contract terms are a standard text of the German cooperative banking group, and Wero is based on specifications of the European operating company EPI. In that case this text is addressed to GLS Bank as the body that can pass on its customers’ experience to them.

The essay would be refuted if it turned out that the app does give feedback at the last step and he overlooked it. In that case we ask for forbearance, and for a hint where it can be found.

VIII. One Sentence on the Screen

For Wero, Europe has brought banks together, written contracts, built data centres. At the last step a single sentence is missing that tells the customer what to do.

If a lift button knows it must light up: why does a European payment app not know it?
