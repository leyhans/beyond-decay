The Third Clock

The Patience of Money

In “The Second Clock” we described why an invention needs two clocks that strike at the same time. There is a third. It runs faster than the other two, and it decides what becomes of the inventor.

I. Three Clocks

The first clock is that of the technology. Polygon turning was shown for the first time in 1985 and, by our reckoning, would have been ready for series production between 1995 and 2000. The second clock is that of the carrier, a machine builder with a sales network and training. The licence agreement ran from 1987 to 1992. Both are set out in “The Second Clock”.

The third clock is that of the money. In this case it started running in 1985, and it struck for the first time after three months.

The technology needed ten years, the carrier lasted five, the money gave a quarter of a year. Anyone who sees these three figures side by side needs no culprit for what follows.

II. The Euphoria

In 1985 the inventor came to Lower Saxony with a patented process. Two of the most respected professors in the field, Manfred Weck in Aachen and Hans Kurt Tönshoff in Hanover, had assessed it favourably. The town of Hamelin took him into its new start-up centre. The state gave a grant of 700,000 marks that did not have to be repaid. A subsidiary of the state bank, Nord/GI, gave a silent participation of 500,000 marks. The local savings bank gave credit.

That is a great deal of money and a great deal of goodwill in a single year. The state cannot be accused of having done nothing.

In the autumn the process stood at the EMO trade fair in Hanover. The response was strong. Not one machine was sold.

III. Cold Feet

The financiers saw correctly that nothing could be sold in 1985. The electronic coupling of the spindles was not yet reliable at high speeds, and a usable controlled axis in the rotating tool did not exist. The invention was ahead of its surroundings.

At this point a second decision would have been needed. It would have read: this thing needs ten years, what follows from that? One could have cut costs, looked for a carrier and waited. One could also have pulled out and taken the loss. Either would have been a strategy.

Neither happened. The instruments went on running as they were built. The silent participation demanded interest. To pay it, the inventor took out loans at the savings bank. The savings bank cancelled the loans at intervals and threatened enforcement. Then he drove to Hamelin and placated the man in charge, who for his part complained that the banking supervisors were breathing down his neck.

The whole matter is in this picture. Everyone had someone above him who pressed, and everyone passed the pressure downwards. At the end of the chain stood the only person who could have moved the technology forward, and he spent his days calming bankers.

Nobody set a trap for him. It was inherent in the system. A company that grants silent participations with running interest and a savings bank that grants loans did what they are built to do. Nobody finances the planting of a forest with an overdraft. Here it was done, and people were surprised that after a quarter of a year there was no timber.

IV. The Source of Ideas

Meanwhile the first clock went on running, and the inventor helped to wind it himself. A maker of control systems wrote the specification for its new numerical control according to the requirements of this process. A toolmaker learned from him the details from which a market-leading controlled tool axis later emerged. The electronics subsidiary of the licensee developed its control further on the same requirements.

That was these firms’ good right, and it is no reproach to them. The finding lies elsewhere. Whoever is servicing interest cannot take a stake anywhere. He cannot co-finance a development or negotiate a contract that secures him a share. What remains to him is to pass on ideas and watch what others make of them. The debt trap of 1985 did not take money from him that he had. It fixed the role in which he spent the following decades: a source of ideas without a share and without a name.

V. The Tuition Fee

His contact at Nord/GI was the managing director Gerd Kastrup, who later headed NORD Holding, the state bank’s investment company. He once told the inventor that they had learned more from this project than from any other.

What was learned can be looked up. From the early 1990s the company shifted its business from silent participations to buying established medium-sized firms. Today it manages around four billion euros. Founders and inventors are not in its programme.

So the lesson was correct: it cannot be done with these tools. It was paid for by the one on whom it was learned.

VI. The Bankrupt

On the treatment of the one who has failed there is a fact that explains why the years afterwards were as they were. Until the end of 1998 German law knew no clean break for a person who could not pay his debts. The old Bankruptcy Code left creditors the unrestricted right to go on claiming after the proceedings, for thirty years. Discharge of residual debt has existed only since 1 January 1999. At first it took seven years, and since October 2020 it takes three.

In the United States there has been a permanent bankruptcy law granting the debtor a discharge since 1898. Germany needed a hundred years longer. Whoever fails there has proceedings behind him. Whoever failed in Germany before 1999 had a life ahead of him in which every income belonged to others first. The inventor was personally liable, and relatives stood surety for part of the loans. He says that he and his family were treated for years like a bankrupt. That was no particular harshness on the part of those involved. It corresponded to a law that knew no clean break for the one who had failed.

VII. And Today

Some things have improved. Discharge takes three years instead of thirty. For the early phase of a start-up there is more money than in 1985.

The third clock still runs fastest. In 2025 Germany invested venture capital amounting to 0.16 per cent of its economic output, France 0.29, Britain 0.63, the United States 0.90. The German start-up association finds that the early phase is making progress and that the gap in the later phases remains. That is the same finding in today’s words: money for the beginning can be found, money for staying the course cannot.

VIII. What Follows

One simple rule would have sufficed in 1985, and it suffices today. Whoever finances something new must ask on the first day which of the three clocks sets the pace. If it is the technology and it needs ten years, the money must not fall due after months. If the money cannot wait, it must not be given to such a thing, not even out of goodwill.

The euphoria is the lesser evil here. What is worse is that nobody is responsible for the day after, on which the first assumption turns out to be wrong. For that day the German system of funding has a form for reclaiming the money and nothing else.
