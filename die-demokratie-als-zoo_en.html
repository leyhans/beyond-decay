Democracy as a Zoo

Is democracy a form of state or a form of society?

The animals sit in their enclosures, the visitors come and go. How often they come and whether they help muck out is up to them. And in theory any one of them can become zoo director.

I. The zoo

A zoo has enclosures, keepers, opening hours and house rules. It has animals that live in the enclosures and visitors who stand at the railings. It has a director. And it has an owner who is rarely seen: the city that runs it.

Democracy as the German Basic Law describes it in Article 20 is such a zoo. “The Federal Republic of Germany is a democratic and social federal state. All state authority is derived from the people.” The enclosures are the institutions: the Bundestag, the state parliaments, the ministries, the party headquarters, the talk-show studios. The animals are the political actors who live in them. The visitors are the citizens. The owner, according to the wording of the constitution, is also the people.

The image answers a question that has been argued over for almost two hundred years: whether democracy is a form of state or a form of society. The zoo is the form of state. Whether it is alive is decided by the visitors.

II. The dispute

In 1831 Alexis de Tocqueville travelled through America to study its prisons. He came back with a different finding. His book on democracy in America opens by saying that nothing had struck him more there than the “equality of conditions”. He did not mean the constitution but everyday life: a country in which the servant greeted the master at eye level. For Tocqueville the democratic society came first. The democratic state followed from it.

John Dewey sharpened this in 1916: a democracy is more than a form of government; it is primarily a mode of associated living.

In Germany the dispute was fought in the open a little over fifty years ago. On 28 October 1969 Willy Brandt said in his first government statement: “We want to dare more democracy.” He meant universities, schools and workplaces too. In 1970 the Freiburg political scientist Wilhelm Hennis replied with a polemic on “democratisation”. Democracy, he argued, is a form of state. Whoever extends it to every sphere of life confuses having a say with having competence, and in the end destroys what he wanted to spread.

Hennis had a point, and we take it seriously. The operating theatre does not vote on an operation, nor the building site on a bridge. Some things are decided by skill, not by majority. In the image: Hennis is the zoo director who insists that not every visitor may enter the predator enclosure. That is not contempt for the visitors. It is house rules.

What Hennis did not answer is where the visitors are to come from when nobody outside knows animals any more.

III. The animals

Whoever lives in an enclosure for long adapts to it. He knows when feeding time is, where the audience stands and how to move so that it stays. What he unlearns is life outside.

In the Bundestag of 1949, according to an analysis by “Die Welt”, 18 per cent of members were workers. In today’s Bundestag it is three per cent. Of the 630 members elected in 2025, the Bundestag’s own statistics assign 459 to the field of “business organisation, law, administration”. A typical path today leads from the party youth organisation through a post as an MP’s assistant to a seat of one’s own. How this path filters we described in “The Long March through the Party”.

This is no reproach to any individual. An animal born in the zoo cannot help it. But it knows the wild only by hearsay, and it decides about it.

Feeding times are fixed too. The animals are liveliest when there is an audience: on the evening talk show, in the plenary when the cameras are running, on the platform where outrage brings applause fastest. The enclosure does not reward the animal that can do the most, but the one that is seen the best.

IV. The sponsors

In many zoos a small sign hangs on the enclosure: the name of the sponsor who pays for this animal’s food.

Since 2022 the Bundestag has kept such a register. According to the first report of the register office in March 2025, around 6,000 active interest representatives were listed, with almost 27,000 named persons. That makes a good forty people for each of the 630 members whose job it is to talk to him. For 2024 those registered reported spending more than 910 million euros on their work. Trade unions, employers’ associations and churches are exempt from registration and do not appear.

Fairness requires saying this: not every sponsor wants to steer the animal. Many supply knowledge that the enclosures lack, and the register itself is progress, because before it there were no signs at all. But a sponsor comes every day. The visitor comes when he has time.

V. The visitors

The visitors come. In the 2025 federal election 82.5 per cent of those entitled cast their vote, more than at any time since reunification. On that Sunday the zoo is full.

How often they come otherwise is up to them. Nobody forbids them to go to an MP’s surgery on an ordinary Tuesday, attend a council meeting or join a party. The parties in the Bundestag together again have just over 1.2 million members. With 59 million eligible voters that is two in a hundred, and since 1990 the total number of party members has roughly halved. In the CDU more than half the members are over sixty.

A zoo whose season tickets are held almost only by pensioners is not dead. But one can calculate when it will fall silent.

VI. Mucking out

The most interesting part of a zoo is never seen by the visitor. In the morning, before it opens, the enclosures are mucked out, the animals fed, the fences repaired.

Democracy has this work too, and most of it is done by volunteers. At the 2025 federal election, according to the Federal Returning Officer, around 675,000 poll workers sat in the polling stations and counted the votes by hand. In the town and municipal councils of the thirteen non-city states there are about 170,000 seats, almost all of them honorary, alongside a job, after hours.

That is exactly where it is thinning out. In the 2024 local elections in Baden-Württemberg, 172 municipalities had only one list or none at all, so that 1,866 councillors had to be chosen by simple majority vote. These are enclosures for which no keeper applies any more.

VII. Becoming director

In theory any citizen can become zoo director. The word “theory” deserves a closer look.

The way into the municipal council is open. One needs a list, a few signatures, evenings and a thick skin. The way into the state parliament and the Bundestag is ajar. The door is not locked, but behind it lies the long march through the party: local branch, district association, list selection, and at every stage those already inside decide. In 2025, 587 of 733 sitting members stood again. Whoever does not come from the enclosure must apply against those who live in it.

So there are two truths, and both hold. Nobody prevents the visitor from becoming director. And hardly any visitor does.

VIII. Outside

The real question lies outside the gate. Ernst-Wolfgang Böckenförde put it in 1967 into a sentence that has been quoted ever since: “The liberal, secularised state lives on preconditions that it cannot itself guarantee.” In the image: a zoo can feed its animals, but it cannot breed them if the species has died out in the wild.

Outside, an adult spends most of his waking time at work. According to the IAB establishment panel for 2024, about 43 per cent of all employees had a works council. The majority therefore work without any elected representation. Whoever spends eight hours a day in an order where decisions are made at the top and carried out at the bottom brings little practice to election Sunday.

That the species can survive in the wild is shown by a few stubborn examples. In the Swiss cantons of Glarus and Appenzell Innerrhoden the citizens gather once a year in the open air and decide laws and taxes by show of hands. In Barquisimeto, Venezuela, the cooperative federation Cecosesola has worked without bosses since 1967. Decisions are made not by vote but by consensus, and according to the Right Livelihood Foundation, which honoured it in 2022, its weekly markets serve around 100,000 families. That is democracy as a way of life, in a state whose form of state is hardly democratic any more. It is the counterpart of the Federal Republic, which has a democratic form of state and a society that practises it little.

IX. Who owns the zoo

What follows is our conclusion from the evidence.

Democracy is legally a form of state and lives only as a form of society. The zoo with its enclosures, house rules and opening hours is the form of state. Whether visitors come, how often, whether they help muck out and whether one of them one day becomes director, that is the form of society.

Hennis was right that not everyone may enter the predator enclosure. He overlooked that a zoo withers when nobody outside knows how to handle animals any more. Brandt was right that democracy must be practised. How far that has succeeded is shown by the figures above.

A zoo without visitors does not close at once. First it falls silent, then it becomes expensive, and then its sponsors take it over. The signs on the enclosures grow larger, feeding times follow the donors, and at some point nobody asks any more who actually owns the zoo. The constitution answers that question. Whether the answer holds is decided by the visitors.
