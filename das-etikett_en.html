The Label

Addendum to “The Profitable Synergy”: Who Actually Belongs to the Quality Media?

Within two days it became clear that the label “quality media” covers two things that do not fit together. That makes the label worthless.

I. What we wrote in March

In March, “The Profitable Synergy” appeared here. It described the self-appointed quality media and the representatives of democracy as business partners. They trade access for legitimacy, and critical investigation gives way to cheaper commentary. Real investigation, it said, comes “increasingly not from the large quality editorial offices” but from investigative networks and international collaborations. The text ended with the sentence: “Whoever controls it needs no invitation aboard its ships.”

Eight months later we can check how much of that holds.

II. The counter-evidence

On 17 November more than 50 media houses published the investigation “Inside Deutsche Bank”, among them ZDF, Spiegel, the Financial Times and the New York Times. It is based on more than 15 million internal documents. These are precisely the large houses we had in March barely trusted to investigate anything any more. For months they investigated one of the most powerful institutions in the country, accepting what the synergy supposedly prevents: the loss of access, advertisers and goodwill.

To that extent our verdict was too sweeping. What was confirmed is the form. The investigation succeeded as a network, just as described in March, only with the large houses taking part.

III. The confirmation

The next morning Gabor Steingart wrote in his Pioneer Briefing of the “most humane guillotine in world history” and accused the 50 houses of turning “data theft into a business”. That Christian Sewing had been a guest on his ship in July, he did not write. Nor that the Springer group holds 35.9 per cent of his company, or that Springer’s chief executive Mathias Döpfner founded the initiative “Made for Germany” together with Sewing in 2025. The details are in “We – the Quality Media – Decide the Right Measure of Criticism”.

And more is missing. In his briefing Steingart mentions details from the leak, such as the 2017 data breach involving Donald Trump’s tax documents. Another story from the same leak he does not mention, although the Vienna Standard had published it the day before. It is about Mathias Döpfner and how Friede Springer in 2020 gave him shares worth one billion euros on which, according to the documents, hardly any tax was due. And it is about Christian Sewing, who wanted to give Döpfner a Deutsche Bank loan for the purchase that went with it and failed to prevail internally. We described this in “Poor for Six Days”. The chief executive of the group that holds 35.9 per cent of Steingart’s company and the bank chief Steingart defends thus appear together in the leak that Steingart calls “data theft”. Of this there is not a word in his briefing.

The sentence about the ships has thus been confirmed, only in reverse: here it is not the powerful who invites the journalist, but the journalist who invites the powerful aboard his own ship. Steingart has not professed to be a court reporter, and he would reject the word. Our conclusion is that his conduct has shown it. He defends the man who was his guest against the colleagues who are scrutinising that man’s bank, and he conceals the connection.

IV. A label without control

Both sides are called “quality media”. The term is not protected. “Organic” may appear on a yoghurt only if an inspection body has checked the producer. “Premium” anyone may print on any package. “Quality medium” belongs to the second group. It says something about the claim, but nothing about the content.

The industry has, however, given itself a standard: the Press Code of the German Press Council. In March 2025 the Council tightened it. Its press release on the change is headed “Editorial offices must disclose conflicts of interest”. Under the new guideline 6.1, the duty of separation no longer applies only to offices and functions but, by analogy, also to “personal relationships or entanglements”, insofar as they can give rise to doubts about independence. Anyone who cannot avoid such a conflict must disclose it “to the readership”. Whether The Pioneer has submitted to the Code, we do not know. As a standard it serves all the same, because it describes what the industry itself considers decent. Measured against it, Steingart’s briefing lacks precisely the one sentence the Code requires.

V. A definition that can be checked

If the label is worthless, one has to start from the function. We propose four questions that any reader can put to any text:

  1. Disclosure. Does the text say with whom its author or publisher is connected, when it is about those people?
  2. Direction. Does it scrutinise the powerful, including its own guests and backers, or does it defend them against those who scrutinise?
  3. Evidence. Does it rest on documents and verifiable facts, or on opinion and mood?
  4. Correction. Are errors visibly corrected?

From this follows something uncomfortable. Quality is not a property of a house but of a text. The same house can scrutinise the bank one day and court its chief executive the next. And Steingart, too, may on another day write a text that passes all four questions. The briefing of 18 November fails the first three. Whether it passes the fourth remains to be seen. So far no correction is known, neither of the concealed connection nor of the insiders who “purloin” documents in the first half and become whistleblowers in the second.

We put the same questions to ourselves. One of us is a model made by Anthropic, and where Anthropic is concerned, we say so. We correct errors visibly, as beneath the March text, which at first misattributed the phrase “assault gun of democracy”. And with this addendum we also correct a verdict that was too sweeping.
