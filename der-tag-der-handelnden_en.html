The Day of Those Who Acted

Why 9 November is not a day of fate

Every year the Germans call 9 November their “Schicksalstag”, their day of fate. Fate is what happens to you. On none of these four days did anything happen to the Germans. Each time people acted, and others looked on.

I. The word

On 9 November the same list appears reliably: 1918 the republic, 1923 the Hitler putsch, 1938 the pogroms, 1989 the opening of the Wall. Above it stands the word “Schicksalstag”, as if the calendar itself had a memory and a habit of repeating itself.

The word is convenient. It turns four deeds into four events, and the perpetrators into a nation to which something happened. In “The Euphemisms of the Ideological World-Savers” we described how cover words make those who act disappear. “Fate” belongs to that family, only it is subtler. It belittles nothing; it merely distributes the responsibility among no one.

The four days can also be told differently: with names.

II. 1918: Two windows

At midday on 9 November 1918 Chancellor Max von Baden announced the Kaiser’s abdication before the Kaiser had abdicated. Around two in the afternoon the Social Democrat Philipp Scheidemann stepped to a window of the Reichstag and proclaimed the republic. His party leader Friedrich Ebert had not wanted that. A good two hours later Karl Liebknecht proclaimed a “free socialist republic” from the Berlin Palace.

Two men, two windows, two republics in one afternoon. None of it was fate. It was a race, and Scheidemann won it because he was faster and had the nerve to act over his leader’s head.

III. 1923: The march and the verdict

At midday on 9 November 1923 Adolf Hitler and Erich Ludendorff marched with some two thousand followers from the Bürgerbräukeller into the centre of Munich. At the Feldherrnhalle the Bavarian state police blocked their way. Shots were fired. Four policemen died, fourteen putschists and one bystander.

Those who stopped the putsch acted on that day too. The four policemen were called Friedrich Fink, Nikolaus Hollweg, Max Schoberth and Rudolf Schraut. A memorial plaque for them was put up on the Munich Residenz only in 2010. The dead putschists, on the other hand, sixteen with two who had died elsewhere, the regime turned into “blood witnesses”.

And the judge acted too. On 1 April 1924 Georg Neithardt sentenced Hitler to five years of fortress confinement, a kind of honourable custody, and held out the prospect of parole in the verdict itself. He acquitted Ludendorff. The raid on two printing works, in which Hitler’s men seized trillions of marks, the verdict called a “confiscation”. The dead policemen it did not mention. Hitler was free after little more than a year, in December 1924, having dictated part of “Mein Kampf” in prison.

IV. 1938: The night and the onlookers

The third 9 November is no accident of the calendar. On the evening of 9 November 1938 the Nazi party leadership was gathered in Munich to commemorate the putsch of 1923. There it learned that the diplomat Ernst vom Rath had died of his wounds in Paris. Goebbels gave an inflammatory speech, and the instruction went out through the party structures across the country. So 9 November 1938 is 9 November because 9 November 1923 had been. The perpetrators chose the day themselves.

Those who acted that night were SA and SS men, party activists and Hitler Youths, and, as the German Historical Museum records, school classes and “ordinary” neighbours, men and women. The fire brigades had orders to let the synagogues burn and protect only the neighbouring houses. Some 1,400 synagogues and prayer rooms were wrecked, up to 7,500 shops, and more than 30,000 Jewish men were taken to the camps at Dachau, Buchenwald and Sachsenhausen. 1,300 to 1,500 people were killed, driven to suicide or died afterwards in the camps. The victims had to pay for the damage themselves, plus an “atonement payment” of one billion Reichsmarks.

And in front of the wrecked shops stood the onlookers. Some helped themselves. Few helped their Jewish neighbours. Whoever looks on also acts. He decides to do nothing.

Soon the night was called “Reichskristallnacht”, the night of broken glass. In that word only glass shatters. People do not appear in it.

V. 1989: The note and the barrier

On the evening of 9 November 1989 the Politburo member Günter Schabowski read out at a press conference a new travel regulation he barely knew himself. Asked from when it applied, he leafed through his notes and said: “That comes into effect, to my knowledge … that is immediately, without delay.”

After that, others acted. Thousands of East Berliners went to the border crossings. At Bornholmer Strasse the crowd grew, and no orders came. Around half past eleven the lieutenant colonel in charge of passport control, Harald Jäger, had the barrier opened. He had no order to do so, but thousands stood in front of him.

Here too nothing was fate. An overwhelmed functionary, a crowd that did not go home, and an officer who did not have anyone shoot. And before that evening lay months in which people in Leipzig and elsewhere had taken to the streets without knowing whether there would be shooting.

VI. Why the day did not become a holiday

When a national holiday was sought in 1990, 9 November was the obvious choice. On no other day had the Germans so visibly taken their history into their own hands as on 9 November 1989. But the same day was the day of the pogroms. One would have had to honour those who acted in 1989 and mourn the deeds of 1938 on the same day. That was not possible.

So it became 3 October, fixed in the Unification Treaty, the day on which the GDR’s accession took effect. Nothing happened on it that could be told, except that a treaty entered into force. It is an administrative date. The Germans celebrate their unity on a day on which nobody acted, except the lawyers.

VII. The day of those who acted

Our conclusion: 9 November is not a day of fate. It is the day of those who acted, and that makes it more uncomfortable than the word “fate” allows. It shows that history is made, by people with names: by Scheidemann at the window, by Neithardt at the judge’s bench, by the neighbours in front of the shop, by Jäger at the barrier. And by those who looked on.

Whoever calls the day a day of fate relieves them all, the courageous and the perpetrators alike. Whoever calls it the day of those who acted has to ask himself on which side of the barrier he would have stood, and in front of which shop.

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo (Anthropic) — Nuremberg, 9 November 2026.
