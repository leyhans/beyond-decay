The Real Erhard and the Social Market Economy 2.0

On a forgotten Erhard, a diagnosis suppressed since 1965, and an order that needs no strong man — because it has built its strength into its very construction
beyond-decay.org — 13 June 2026

I. The Forgotten Erhard

There are two Ludwig Erhards. One is a brand: the stout man with the cigar, the father of the economic miracle, the friendly face of the upswing, whose name today adorns summits, foundations and research centres. This Erhard is everywhere. The other is almost forgotten, and he is the more important one: the fighter against the entrenchment of economic power, the man who spent his political life on a single battle — the battle over the antitrust law.

Erhard's real opponent was never the welfare state and never the union. His opponent was organised interests, cartels, federations, the entrenchment of the market by those who already held power. His struggle for the law against restraints of competition dragged on for years against the bitter resistance of the Federation of German Industry. „Prosperity for All", his famous book, is for long stretches a polemic against market power and against the privileged access of the few to the levers of decision. The real Erhard was no man of comfortable growth. He was a man of struggle against those who wanted to disable competition — from above, not from below.

This Erhard has been forgotten, and the forgetting has a system. For whoever remembers the enemy of cartels would have to ask how competition fares today. The brand is harmless; the content would be an indictment.

II. The Suppressed Diagnosis

A year before Erhard's fall, in 1965, one of his intellectual forerunners wrote the sentence that sums it all up. Rüdiger Altmann described a society endangered no longer by social conflicts of the old kind but by what he called the „functionless proliferation of organised interests". Some of the great federations, he wrote, already resembled „dinosaurs forced to feed on their own excrement". The image is drastic, but the diagnosis is sober and to this day unrefuted: an overdeveloped pluralism no longer questions the unity of society, but it morbidly weakens its capacity to act. It paralyses government, parliament and reform.

The same finding, in the language of science, had come a few years earlier from an American economist, Mancur Olson: organised minorities push through their concentrated interests against the dispersed majority, and the longer a society is stable, the denser the web of these distributional coalitions grows, until it strangles renewal. What Altmann called „proliferation", Olson called the „logic of collective action". It is the same disease.

And it is more visible today than ever. When a government fails at overdue reforms, when every change shatters against a web of vested interests, when a country feels incapable of acting although no one is openly opposed — then this is not the failure of one man. It is the paralysis that Altmann and Olson described sixty years ago. The diagnosis was right. It was merely suppressed, because its beneficiaries had no interest in it.

III. Why the Old Answers Fall Short

To this diagnosis there were, since 1965, two great answers, and both fall short.

The first was Altmann's own, and it was Erhard's programme: the „Formed Society", a strong, synchronising government standing above the federations and steering the whole. Altmann appealed openly to the formula of Thomas Hobbes — auctoritas non veritas facit legem, authority, not truth, makes the law. This is the answer of the strong man: if the federations paralyse, then what is needed is a government mightier than they. This answer is understandable, but it is dangerous, for it heals the paralysis through the concentration of power — and the strong man who tames the federations today is tomorrow himself the sovereign whom no one tames.

The second answer was pure faith in the market: one need only roll back the state, and competition will regulate everything. This too falls short, for the unregulated market produces the very entrenchment it claims to prevent. Erhard knew exactly this, and exactly for this reason he needed the antitrust law. A market without the rule that prevents market power devours itself — the big buy the small, until no competition is left. The mere retreat of the state is not an order; it is capitulation before the concentrated.

Both answers share the same blind spot: they think in persons and quantities — more state or less state, more authority or more freedom. But the question is not how much state is needed. The question is how it is built.

IV. The Social Market Economy 2.0: Architecture Instead of Sovereign

Here a third answer begins, and it is the true completion of what Erhard started. Let us call it the Social Market Economy 2.0. Its basic idea is simple: the solution to the paralysis is neither the strong man at the top nor the absent state, but the right construction — an order that makes entrenchment structurally impossible, so that it needs no permanent guardian afterwards.

This is no new thought; it is Erhard's own, thought through to the end. The antitrust law was precisely that: not a micro-rule controlling every transaction, but the one basic rule preventing success from turning into market power. A structural rule that then lets the game run free. Walter Eucken, the forerunner of ordoliberalism, called this Ordnungspolitik: the state sets the frame and then keeps out. The Social Market Economy 2.0 takes this thought more seriously than its inventors could, because we today have means they did not have.

It was Franz Böhm, the jurist of the Freiburg School, who pushed this antitrust law through the Bundestag in the 1950s against the bitter resistance of organised industry — and from him comes the sentence that captures the whole idea in a handful of words: competition is the most ingenious instrument of disempowerment in history. That is the core. Competition is not first a machine for lowering prices; it is a means against entrenchment — it makes independent where monopoly makes dependent. And what holds for the economy holds for order as such: a construction that disperses power instead of concentrating it needs no strong man at the top, because it carries the disempowerment within itself.

What does this mean concretely? It means: feedback instead of supervision. In a well-built technical system a deviation corrects itself, because the feedback is built into the construction — no overseer need intervene. An order can be built the same way. Structures that cannot be taken over, because takeover is constructively denied them. Levers that elude seizure, because no one obtains them alone. Rules with a built-in expiry date, which must renew themselves rather than harden into vested interest. Visibility that exposes entrenchment before it nests. This is no new bureaucracy. It is the opposite: it is the art of finding so few and so fundamental rules that the guardian becomes superfluous.

It is telling that these building blocks have long been on the table. Even the recent resilience agenda from the circle of the Ludwig Erhard Forum — written by a Princeton economist and an Erhard scholar — expressly calls for sunset clauses, regional sandboxes and data cooperatives. It sees the same paralysis, it even names the same cause, the stubborn persistence of particular interests. But it treats these building blocks as individual tools in a long list of reforms, addressed to the very actors its own diagnosis names as the blockers. Precisely here lies the difference: what appears there as a collection of good individual measures is in truth the expression of a single principle. A sunset clause is not one clever idea among many; it is a lever withdrawn. A data cooperative is not a funding idea; it is a structure that cannot be taken over. The list has the stones; it lacks the blueprint. The blueprint is supplied by the principle: architecture without a sovereign.

V. Not a Cage, but a Free Game

An objection is to be expected here, and it is a good one. Only recently a clever mind from the Erhard tradition warned, using the example of football: in the cage of perfect rules the game dies. The video referee, who wants to exclude every error, destroys the authority of the referee and the beauty of the game; Erhard's teaching was few, simple rules that set a frame in which the actors act freely. This is exactly right — and it is no objection to the Social Market Economy 2.0, but its best argument.

For the feedback we speak of is not the video referee. It does not control every single decision, it does not review every foul. It is the one simple basic rule that prevents a player from buying the referee. The cage of perfect rules and the architecture without a sovereign are not the same — they are opposites. The cage distrusts the actors and regulates the game to death. The architecture trusts the actors and protects only the one condition without which the free game is not possible at all: that no one can buy the playing field itself. Erhard would not have minded, as that clever mind rightly wrote, that a referee makes mistakes. But he would have done everything to prevent one team from owning the referee. The one is micro-regulation that suffocates the game. The other is the structural rule that first makes it free. The Social Market Economy 2.0 wants less cage and more game — but it wants to bolt the one gate through which entrenchment enters.

VI. The Vacant Place

With that, a programme lies ready that belongs to no one. It is not left and not right; it is the completion of the ordoliberal thought with the means of our time. It takes seriously the diagnosis suppressed since 1965, and it gives an answer that needs neither the strong man nor the absent state. It is, in the precise sense, the real Erhard for the twenty-first century: not the brand, but the fight against entrenchment, continued with tools Erhard did not have.

It is striking that this place stands empty today. No one in the front row of politics occupies the real Erhard — the enemy of cartels, the foe of entrenchment. All have the brand, none has the content. This is remarkable, for the place is not small. Whoever entered it — credibly, not as the next costume — would have something that has grown rare in today's business: a narrative larger than the daily grind, and a lineage reaching back to the economic miracle.

We write this not for a name. We write it because the idea is larger than anyone who might carry it — just as Erhard's idea was larger than Erhard and outlived him, albeit distorted. A programme that is strong only as long as a particular man represents it is weak. A programme that anyone with the courage can take up is strong. The place stands open. It is not the programme that is missing. It is the one who makes it his own.

Hans Ley und Claude Dedo (Anthropic)
