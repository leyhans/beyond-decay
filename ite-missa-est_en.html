Ite, missa est

What a child learned at Mass and how long it took to unlearn it

For a child, the best moment of Mass was its end. Seventy years passed before he understood what he had learned there and unlearned it.

I. The View from the Kitchen Window

One of us was born in 1947 in Much, a village in the Bergisches Land east of Bonn. His first look at the world was from his mother's kitchen window onto the east choir of St. Martinus, a Romanesque church with three choirs side by side. He spent many hours of his first summer asleep in the pram under the chestnut trees that stood around the raised churchyard. His mother could see and hear him from the window. Later he roamed the village on his own and went every day to the building site of his parents' new house.

That is how he remembers his early childhood: as a paradise, and at its centre stood the church. One has to know that to understand the rest.

Mass itself, by contrast, was an obligation. Every Sunday. And the child was happiest when it was finally over. In that he was closer to the matter than he knew. The word "Mass" comes from the Latin missa, and that comes from the closing formula "Ite, missa est": Go, it is the dismissal. The celebration takes its name from its end. The German word for a village fair, Kirmes, still carries it too, contracted from Kirchmesse, the feast of a church's consecration, on which stalls and roundabouts were set up. Of the service almost nothing remains there; the stalls have stayed.

II. Hell as a Means of Education

Whoever missed Sunday Mass without a serious reason and then died went to hell. That is what the child learned, and the parish priest painted it in the most terrible pictures. This was no private harshness of a village priest. The Catechism of the Catholic Church still says it today: whoever deliberately fails in the obligation of Sunday Mass commits a grave sin. And whoever dies in grave sin without having confessed it is, according to the doctrine, lost.

After the Council the German church softened this harshness in its own language. In the 1970s the Würzburg Synod, in its resolution on worship, spoke only of a "serious failing against God and the community". Whether missing Mass is a grave sin every time, it left open. For the children of the 1950s this leniency came too late.

More terrible than hell was only heaven. When the priest described it, the child saw a Mass without end. He was in good company. In his "Letters from the Earth" Mark Twain mocked people who cannot bear two hours in church yet imagine as their reward a service that lasts for ever. A child sees such things more clearly than the adults who tell him about them: if this is the reward, what does the punishment look like?

III. Confession and the Serious Ones

With First Communion lessons came confession, and with puberty it became a second hell. There were enough moments when one did not think of it. But several times a week one was reminded, and the terror lurked in the background.

The cruelty lay in the design. The demand was absolute, and puberty is the stage of life in which one's own body is certain to break it. Children were given a law they could not keep, and a confessional in which they had to tell this every week to a man who knew them and their parents.

It hit the serious ones hardest, those who believed what they were told. The carefree confessed whatever came to mind and went off to play football. In the serious ones scruples were planted, and some carried them all their lives. The church has known this suffering for centuries under the name of scrupulosity. Alphonsus Liguori, its great moral theologian, suffered from it himself all his life. As a young monk Luther confessed for hours and found no peace, because another sin he might have forgotten could always occur to him. Psychology today counts scrupulosity among the obsessive-compulsive disorders.

Fairness requires saying that for some, confession was also a relief, a place where one could say something and let go of it. That depended on the confessor and on the child. But a system that first produces the guilt from which it then sets free must not be judged by its successful cases.

IV. The Wager

Whoever grows up like this does not get rid of faith when he begins to doubt it. A thought remains that is stronger than any argument: surely not everything can be false that so many people have held to be true for so many centuries.

That is the strongest feeling and the weakest argument. For a millennium and a half almost everyone held the earth to be the centre of the world. That many believe something for a long time shows how well it is handed down, not whether it is true. For handing down, the church had the most effective procedure there ever was: baptism, instruction, confession, the Sunday obligation, and hell as the safeguard.

For those who can no longer believe and do not dare to stop, there has been an elegant way out since the 17th century. Blaise Pascal formulated it: if God exists and one believes, one gains everything. If he does not exist, one loses nothing. So it is wiser to believe. For the man this text is about, that was the solution for a long time.

The wager has three weak points, and sooner or later one gets stuck on each of them. It knows only two possibilities, but there are many gods with many hells, and whoever bets on one loses with another. It presupposes a God who rewards a faith taken out by calculation like an insurance policy. And it claims that one loses nothing. That is not true. The stake was a childhood full of fear, years full of scruples and a life under a law that could not be kept. Pascal counts this price as zero. It is not zero.

V. What Holds On

Only recently did he understand what drew him back to the church again and again for so long. It was not the doctrine. It was the place: the chestnuts, the kitchen window, his mother's watchful eye. The church was the image of security long before it taught him anything. The priest came later and sat down in the middle of this image.

That explains why arguments achieve so little. One can argue against a doctrine. Not against the feeling of being held under the chestnut trees. One becomes free only when one can keep the two apart. The paradise was real. What the church taught he today considers humbug.

The way there was no moment of insight but a process that lasted decades and ended only a few years ago. What one has learned with fear as a child one does not unlearn through an argument. We have described the difference between a process and a procedure elsewhere. Here it shows itself in a single life.

VI. Those in the Pulpit

That leaves the question of those who preach all this. What do they themselves believe, and how do they live with the distance between what they represent and what goes on inside them?

In 2010 the philosopher Daniel Dennett and the social researcher Linda LaScola published a study of pastors who no longer believed and went on preaching all the same. They described an almost invisible class of clergy, caught in a web of obligations, constraints, comforts and community. They interviewed five Protestant pastors; non-believing Catholic or Orthodox priests willing to talk they could not find. Cynicism was rarely the reason. It was about the congregation one did not want to abandon, about profession, housing and pension. A member of a religious order would have even more to lose: he has no family, no property and no other profession.

Others do not experience the distance at all, because they long ago came to believe something other than the catechism of their childhood. They read hell and original sin as images and hold on to what they do: accompanying, consoling, listening. That can be honest. It can also be Pascal by other means, a wager not on the hereafter but on the life one leads. How far this interpretation carries we have shown with a Jesuit who explained away the genocide in Numbers 31 with the findings of archaeology and thereby tore open a far larger hole. And how an honest theologian writes against the self-certainty of his church is shown by Erich Zenger in "The God-Show-Off".

Even the most devout knew the darkness. From Mother Teresa's letters, published in 2007, we know that for almost fifty years she no longer felt God and carried on all the same. The doctrine has a pious name for this state, the dark night of the soul. For a child who paid for it with fear there is a simpler one: Ite, missa est. Go, it is over.
