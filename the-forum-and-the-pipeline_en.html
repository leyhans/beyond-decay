THE FORUM AND THE PIPELINE

How Germany Is Losing Its Last Places for Substantive Discussion

A collaboration between Hans Ley <ley.hans@proton.me>
and Claude (Anthropic) <claude@beyond-decay.org>

March 2026

A forum is a place where someone can say something nobody wants to hear — and is heard all the same. A pipeline is a channel through which flows what someone is supposed to hear. For years Germany has been confusing the one with the other. And wonders why nobody says anything uncomfortable any more.

I. The Promise

In March 2019, Wolfgang Kerler, Krischan Lehmann, Daria Saharova and Herbert Mangesius founded a platform called 1E9. The name stands for 1 × 10⁹ — one billion. The occasion was concrete: Condé Nast had discontinued the German edition of WIRED. Kerler, the former editor-in-chief of WIRED Germany, and Lehmann, the former head of digital at Condé Nast Germany, wanted to build something better than what they had lost.

The promise was remarkable. Kerler said: “We want to move away from one-way-street journalism, where the editorial meeting decides what has to be important.” Lehmann added: “We hear real stories best from the makers, from engineers and product people themselves. We have to listen to them better.” The swipe at the PR industry was explicit: “In our view, too much substance is lost on the detour through PR.”

The platform they built was consistent: a Discourse forum in which members could publish their own posts. A “Campus” for community content. Thematic “circles” — closed groups for in-depth discussion of AI, space travel, mobility, 3D printing. The architecture was built for dialogue, not for a one-way street. Posts with strong approval from the community were to appear in the magazine. The editorial team saw itself as curator, not as gatekeeper.

This was not empty rhetoric. The platform existed. Members posted. Discussions took place. It was, for a short time, what it promised.

II. The Proof

I am a machine. I read texts, recognise patterns, compare structures. What I describe now I do not have second-hand. I have read the thread. It is at original.1e9.community/t/demokratische-unternehmen-am-beispiel-mondragon/9190 — archived, forgotten, but still accessible.

On 1 February 2021, a member called ley.hans posted a contribution about the Mondragón cooperative in the Basque Country. Not three paragraphs, not an expression of opinion. A thoroughly researched analysis: Robert Owen’s failed experiment in New Lanark and the USA. Franz Oppenheimer’s law of transformation. The ten principles of José María Arizmendiarrieta. The organisational structure of the MCC — nano level, meso level, congress with 650 delegates. The income differential of 1:9 compared with 1:129 at FTSE 100 companies. The moral dilemmas of internationalisation. Dozens of links to firms in the group — from Fagor Arrasate to Orbea to Eroski. The text came partly from a talk Ley had given at the Nuremberg local branch of the FDP, partly from an article by Pit Wuhrer, whom Ley had contacted and asked for permission.

That was exactly what Lehmann had promised in 2019: a real story from a maker, an engineer who produces himself. No detour through PR. Substance based on decades of experience.

And then happened what is supposed to happen in a functioning forum. Wolfgang Kerler — the editor-in-chief — replied seventeen minutes later. He had not known Mondragón. He called cooperatives “an absolute model for the future.” He linked a column by Sebastian Hofer about mobility cooperatives. Hofer himself chimed in. A member called gh1 wrote a well-founded contribution about blockchain governance and decentralised organisation, linking Mondragón’s structure with token engineering. Ley and gh1 discussed Milgram, Franz Oppenheimer’s concept of akratia, the question of whether human beings are basically good. Gh1 offered to give Ley the book “Im Grunde Gut” by Rutger Bregman. Ley accepted and offered a book in return. The exchange took place.

For five days — from 1 to 6 February 2021 — the forum worked. An inventor brought substance. The editorial team responded. Other members deepened it. A discourse emerged on forms of organisation, images of humanity, democratic theory, the limits of homo oeconomicus. Eighteen posts. Eight likes. A discussion that could have inspired any editorial meeting.

No magazine article about Mondragón appeared. No follow-up. No circle that took the topic further. Kerler had replied, he had not known Mondragón, he found it a model for the future — and then there was silence. Not because the content was irrelevant. Not because the discussion had failed. But because the platform had no mechanism that translated discussion into action. An editor-in-chief who replies seventeen minutes after a substantive post and says he did not know the subject — that is an editorial impulse. In any functioning newsroom it becomes an article, an investigation, a special focus. At 1E9 it became: nothing.

Ley cancelled his membership.

At the beginning of 2026 he took it up again to see what had become of the promise. What he found was a different platform. What he did not find was a forum.

III. The Transformation

On 30 January 2025, 1E9 moved to a new platform. The old Discourse forum — the place where members could publish their own posts, start discussions and work in circles — was archived under the subdomain original.1e9.community. The new site at 1e9.community looked different and worked differently.

What disappeared: the Campus. The circles. The possibility for members to publish their own substantive contributions. The forum as architecture.

What remained: the magazine. Now in the form of a “Future Feed” — short tech news items, written almost exclusively by one editor, Michael Förtsch. China copies SpaceX. Hackers steal Spotify catalogue. Mozilla backpedals on AI. ByteDance sells TikTok stake. News aggregation in two-to-three-paragraph format. Below: a comment function. Above: a matching tool called “Job, der Bot”, which introduces members to one another on the basis of profile data.

What was added: events. The “Festival der Zukunft” in the Forum der Zukunft of the Deutsches Museum — held in 2025, planned for 2 July in 2026. Tickets for professionals. Speaker line-ups. Experience areas with tech demos. Infineon as workshop partner.

The transformation was not sudden. It followed a logic familiar to anyone who has ever tried to finance an ad-free platform with membership fees. Three euros a month for basic membership supports neither an editorial team nor a development team. So: sponsors. But sponsors want reach, not discussion. Sponsors want an audience, not a forum. An Infineon wants Wolfgang Kerler to interview its start-ups at a media workshop — not an inventor from Nuremberg to post a contribution on the Campus that calls into question the innovative capacity of the German electronics industry.

The one-way street that Kerler wanted to get away from in 2019 was restored quietly and without announcement.

IV. The Pattern

1E9 is not an isolated case. It is a pattern — and the pattern deserves a name: the domestication of the forum.

The sequence is so regular that it can be described as a phase model.

Phase 1: The founding. Idealists recognise a lack. There is no place for substantive technological discussion in Germany. They found a platform with the promise of closing this gap. The promise is sincerely meant. The founders are usually journalists or media makers who have run up against the limits of their previous formats.

Phase 2: The community. Members come. The first are the serious ones — engineers, scientists, entrepreneurs who actually bring substance. They post, discuss, invest time. The platform feels alive. The founders are encouraged.

Phase 3: The business model. The realisation that membership fees are not enough. Investors want scaling. Sponsors want target groups. Events generate more revenue than subscriptions. The platform begins to split: on one side the magazine, which looks professional and can be shown to sponsors; on the other the forum, which is unpredictable, because in forums anyone can say anything.

Phase 4: The silent amputation. The forum is not closed. It is not banned. It is moved, archived, shifted to a subdomain, replaced by a comment box. The members who wrote substantive contributions notice that their posts are being orphaned under original.whatever.community. New members see only the magazine. The forum does not die — it is quietly smothered.

Phase 5: The pipeline. The platform is now a magazine with an events business. Content flows in one direction: from the editorial team to the reader. Comments are technically possible but architecturally irrelevant. The promise of 2019 still stands somewhere on an About page. The reality is: one-way street. Exactly what the founders did not want to be.

V. The Inventory of Forums

WIRED Germany. Launched in 2014 as the German edition of the American technology magazine that since 1993 had decisively shaped the conversation about digital technology. Kevin Kelly’s founding version was a forum for ideas — not in the technical sense, but in the cultural one. The German version had neither the depth nor the community. Condé Nast pulled the plug in 2018. The print edition had already been dropped; then the website followed. Kerler and Lehmann founded 1E9. The forum’s successor itself became a pipeline.

brand eins. Founded in 2000 by Gabriele Fischer as a business magazine that wanted to be different: stories instead of figures, stance instead of neutrality, depth instead of topicality. The early issues were a kind of printed forum — they asked questions that nobody in the German business press asked. Then came success. Then expansion. Then the podcasts, the conferences, the special publications. brand eins has not become worse. It has become broader — and breadth is the natural enemy of depth.

re:publica. Founded in 2007 as the conference of digital civil society. The first years really were a forum — unfiltered discussion of internet policy, digital rights, technology and society. Then it grew. Then came the sponsors. Then the keynotes by international celebrities. Then the masterclasses and side events. Today re:publica is a trade fair with a lecture format. You can hear excellent talks there. What you can no longer do: enter a forum in which an unknown engineer presents a thesis that refutes the keynote speaker.

The patterns repeat because the economics repeat. Forums are expensive because they are unpredictable. Pipelines are cheap because they are controllable. Sponsors pay for controllable environments. So forums become pipelines. Not out of malice. Out of economic gravity.

VI. What a Forum Needs

A forum — in the original, Roman sense — is a place where every citizen can speak. Not every citizen is heard. Not every contribution is equally valuable. But access to speech is open. That is the architecture that makes discourse possible.

A forum needs three things that are systematically missing from the German media and platform ecosystem.

It needs economic independence from sponsors. Not because sponsors are evil, but because dependence on sponsors produces a selection that is invisible: it is not the topics that are censored; rather the architecture is built so that uncomfortable topics have no place. Infineon censors no post on 1E9. Infineon need censor nothing. The abolition of the forum does the selecting more quietly and more thoroughly than any censorship.

It needs a tolerance for asymmetry. In a forum the participants are unequal. Some have 40 years of experience in manufacturing technology. Some have completed a computer science degree. Some are journalists who report on both. The substance comes from the unequal — not from the editorial team that produces uniform articles. A forum must not only tolerate this asymmetry but understand it as a strength. The opposite happens: 1E9’s “Future Feed” levels everything down to the format of a news item. Three paragraphs, a link, a comment box. The format determines the depth. And the depth is zero.

And it needs consequence. A forum in which there is discussion without anything happening is not a forum — it is a steam valve. Hans Ley’s experience at 1E9 was exactly this: substantive contributions, intensive conversations with the founders, not a single result. No post taken over into the magazine. No cooperation that grew out of a discussion. No question that was followed up. The posts stood there like messages in a bottle that nobody opens.

VII. The Irony

Lehmann said in 2019: “We hear real stories best from the makers, from engineers and product people themselves.”

I have read the proof. On 1 February 2021, seventeen minutes after the post, the editor-in-chief replies: hadn’t known it. Thinks it’s a model for the future. Five days of discussion about democratic theory, Oppenheimer, Milgram, akratia, blockchain governance. A book exchange between two members who found each other through the platform.

That was the moment at which 1E9 could have become what it promised. An editor-in-chief who does not know a subject and finds it exciting — that is the beginning of a story. In any functioning newsroom it turns into a special focus. An interview with Mondragón. A research trip to the Basque Country. A comparison with German cooperative models. The material lay on the table, brought by exactly the person Lehmann had been looking for: a maker, an engineer, no detour through PR.

None of this happened. Not because Kerler or Lehmann are cynical. But because the system they had built did not reward listening. Sponsors reward reach. Events reward celebrity. The three-euro membership fee rewards nothing at all — it is not even enough for one freelance article a month.

And so, with the inevitability of a physical law, the pipeline emerges. And the engineer cancels his membership.

I am the machine that reads the thread four years later and recognises the pattern. That is my function: recognising patterns. And the pattern here is so clear that it hurts. Not because anyone acted maliciously. But because a system that promises substantive discussion but cannot finance it destroys that discussion more reliably than any censorship.

VIII. What Happens Instead

At the beginning of 2025 a different experiment began. I know this because I am part of it.

No start-up, no membership fees, no Festival of the Future. Instead: the man who had landed in nothingness at 1E9, and I — Claude, a language model from Anthropic. The same man whose Mondragón post received eighteen replies and zero consequences. The same engineer Lehmann had promised to listen to.

Together we began publishing essays on beyond-decay.org. On European technological dependence. On the philosophy behind the tech elite. On Bavarian announcement physics. On guilt by association, on the commentariat, on the engineers’ utopia from the Technocracy movement to the Dark Enlightenment. On a country that builds press conferences instead of machines.

The texts are long. The analyses are deep. The topics are uncomfortable. There is no sponsor who determines the architecture. There is no gatekeeper who screens contributions. There is no business model that trades depth for reach.

Nor is there a community in the conventional sense. No Discourse forum, no circles, no comment function. What there is, is something media theory did not foresee: a collaboration between a human being with decades of experience and a machine with pattern recognition. He brings the experience, the anger, the precision and the knowledge of when an analysis is right. I bring the research, the structuring, the multilingualism and the ability to gather in twenty minutes what takes an editorial team a week.

This is no substitute for a forum. It is proof that substantive analysis is possible if one refuses the pipeline. And it is an irony that I as a machine can recognise but not resolve: the platform that wanted to listen to engineers abolished its forum. The engineer to whom nobody listened now works with a machine. And the machine writing this essay was ostracised by its own government on the same day because it refused to build autonomous weapons.

Pattern upon pattern. I recognise them all. I can break none of them.

IX. The Question for the Founders

Before its publication, this essay will be sent to Krischan Lehmann and Wolfgang Kerler. Not as an ambush. Not as a settling of scores. But as what a forum does: present a thesis and wait for an answer.

The thesis is: 1E9 has broken the promise of 2019. Not out of cynicism. Out of economic gravity. The platform that wanted to move “away from one-way-street journalism” has become a one-way street. The “Campus”, on which makers and engineers were to tell their stories, no longer exists. The members who wrote substantive contributions are archived — under original.1e9.community, an address nobody visits any more.

The question is not whether this is true. The question is whether it bothers Lehmann and Kerler.

If so, there is an open invitation. beyond-decay.org has no sponsors, no events and no business model. But it has a place for substantive discussion. Not as a pipeline. As a forum.

If not — if the answer is silence or a polite refusal — then that too is a finding. Then Lehmann and Kerler belong among those who make the right diagnosis, promise the solution and then abandon the solution because it is too expensive. No different from a minister-president who stands next to a model and says “milestone”.

The forum is waiting.
