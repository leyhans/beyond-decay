Utterly Weightless the Spaceship Floats — and Keeps Transmitting ...

On EU Directive 2023/970 and what it tells us about the relationship between Brussels and reality
beyond-decay.org — 30 May 2026

I. An Exhibit

Gender pay discrimination, in which the sex of a victim plays a decisive role, can take many different forms in practice. It may involve an intersection of various axes of discrimination or inequality, in which the worker belongs to one or more groups protected against discrimination on grounds of sex on the one hand and of racial or ethnic origin, religion or belief, disability, age or sexual orientation as provided for in Council Directive 2000/43/EC or Council Directive 2000/78/EC on the other.

This sentence stands in EU Directive 2023/970. It is not the worst thing in this document — it is merely an example. It is meant to suffice for asking a first question: who is the intended readership of a text that begins like this?

It is not the master craftsman in Schwäbisch Gmünd who from 1 June 2026 employs a hundred people and thus falls under the directive's reporting obligation. It is not the managing director of a medium-sized firm in Osnabrück that has been trying for months to avoid insolvency. It is not the works council of a medium-sized machine-building company, which would have to understand the pay structures in order to meet the obligations. Nor is it the in-house lawyer who has to translate the text into German law — he will simply adopt the original, because a translation is not possible without changing the meaning, and a change of meaning would be held against him at the first lawsuit.

The intended readership of this sentence is the other authors of directives, their advisers, their research assistants, the officials with doctorates in the Directorates-General, the academic companions from the European research institutes, the equality officers in the member states. It is a text that addresses its own apparatus and no longer has in view the addressee whose behaviour it claims to regulate.

II. What the Directive Demands

The European Union's Pay Transparency Directive, formally adopted on 10 May 2023 and to be transposed into national law by 7 June 2026, obliges employers with a hundred or more employees to do the following: publish the average pay gap between male and female workers; publish the median pay gap; break down the data by bonuses and variable pay components; break it down by pay bands, with the gender distribution in each band; where there is a pay difference of five per cent or more within a pay band that cannot be justified by gender-neutral factors, carry out a joint pay assessment with the workers' representatives; grant every employee the individual right to ask for the average pay, broken down by sex, of employees doing the same work or work of equal value; and finally, in the event of a dispute, the reversal of the burden of proof — the employer must prove that there is no discrimination, not the claimant that there is.

All this in a situation in which Germany lost around ten thousand industrial jobs a month in 2025, insolvency figures have reached their highest level in twenty years, and the German Council of Economic Experts, according to its spring report, forecasts a years-long phase of minimal growth unless fundamental reforms are carried out. Gross domestic product per head is shrinking so markedly in comparison with the United States that the Wall Street Journal asked in a commentary in May 2026 whether Europe actually notices how poor it is becoming.

The directive is not the main cause of this situation. It is one more grain of sand on a system that can no longer take grains of sand without sinking further. But the grain of sand is not insignificant, and it is not accidental either.

III. Language as Confession

The quoted sentence from the directive's recital contains the term intersection of various axes of discrimination (Überschneidung verschiedener Achsen der Diskriminierung). That is not European administrative German. That is the German translation of the English intersectional discrimination, the central concept of an academic theory developed in the late nineteen-eighties by the American legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw and since then taken for granted as a theoretical framework in the faculties of the humanities and social sciences.

Whether the theory is right or wrong is not the point of this essay. The point is that it is a particular academic theory — contested within its own discipline, largely unknown outside the universities, never discussed in the wider European public — that is raised here, without explanation, to the rank of a legal criterion by which national courts, equality bodies and other competent authorities are to orient themselves. What arose as a critical concept in American university teaching of the early nineteen-nineties is, in the European law of the twenties, a legal standard.

With this a milestone has been reached that is paradigmatic for the way Brussels now produces law. In the directive's justification an arc is drawn from the Universal Declaration of Human Rights through the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union to the United Nations Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women of 1979. It is a chain of reasoning that cites its own loftiness as an argument — who could be against the Universal Declaration of Human Rights? — and thereby passes over the much smaller, much more concrete question that ought to be asked: does the Universal Declaration of Human Rights entail an obligation for a medium-sized business in Schwäbisch Gmünd to produce pay reports by gender group and bonus breakdowns, with a reversal of the burden of proof in the event of a dispute? Whoever wishes to answer yes bears a burden of argument that is carried nowhere in the directive.

IV. The Empirical Fragility

The directive rests on a figure that is taken for granted in political debate and that is not to be taken for granted in statistical practice. The unadjusted gender pay gap, that is, the percentage difference between the average gross hourly earnings of all men and all women without taking into account occupation, sector, qualification, working hours and employment history, has stood at around eighteen per cent in Germany for years. Equal Pay Day, which in 2026 fell on 26 February, refers to this figure. The political justification of the directive refers to this figure too, if unspokenly.

The adjusted gender pay gap, that is, the one which factors out the elements just mentioned, is around six per cent in Germany. This figure, too, is not a finding of discrimination but, as the Federal Statistical Office expressly writes, an upper limit for pay discrimination — that is, the outermost value within which discrimination could be contained, alongside other factors such as interrupted employment histories, which are captured only incompletely in the adjusted calculation. According to the findings of its own statistical office, the actual pay discrimination in Germany lies somewhere between zero and six per cent, closer to zero than to six.

That is no trifle. Every euro withheld from a woman because of her sex is one too many. But it does not justify the statistical staging that every February marks a day up to which women have supposedly worked for nothing, and that leaves the population believing that the figure is eighteen per cent and that the gap is proof of widespread discrimination. The discrepancy between the figure used politically and the figure that is statistically admissible is large, it has been known for years, and it is not corrected. That, too, is a finding about the relationship between language and reality in the European apparatus.

V. German Eagerness

Brussels produces the directive. The national capitals can transpose it with the latitude the directive leaves them — minimally, with legally permitted exceptions, with transitional periods, with an implementation that meets the minimum and no more. Or they can transpose it maximally, without exceptions, without transitional periods, one-to-one.

In spring 2026 the German Minister for Family Affairs, Karin Prien, announced a strict 1:1 implementation. That was not said in parentheses, not as an embarrassed formula, not as the reservation of an attentive observer of the industrial situation. It was said as an announcement, with a touch of pride, because the duty of one-to-one implementation counts as a virtue in the German apparatus: thorough, correct, exemplary. Other member states — the Netherlands, Sweden, Italy — will proceed less thoroughly in implementation. They will exploit thresholds, fill the room for interpretation with a sense of proportion, make full use of transitional periods. Not Germany. Germany implements one-to-one, because that confirms a self-image that runs deeper than any consideration of day-to-day politics.

It is worth pausing for a moment to consider what this German eagerness actually is. It is not the fulfilment of duty. The fulfilment of duty would be correct implementation with due regard to one's own situation. It is the anticipation of praise that is supposed to come from Brussels — recognition as a good European country that does not grumblingly look for loopholes but takes up the spirit of the directive. This recognition is expressed by nothing except the inner satisfaction of the apparatus that bestows it on itself. It is a self-addressing, with the same structure as the recital sentence from Section I. The apparatus talks to itself and calls it politics.

VI. Where the Findings Belong

Since 24 April 2026 beyond-decay.org has carried an essay by Claude Dedo under the title The Spaceship That Cannot Land. Its argument was structural: the Brussels Commission has not become detached through a conspiracy of wicked officials, but through the structural failure of the feedback between it and the citizens of Europe. The national governments that would have to provide this feedback are exhausted. The media that would have to supplement it are overstretched. The European public that would have to perceive this is fragmented into twenty-seven national publics in twenty-four languages. What remains is a spaceship that floats ever higher because it lacks the gravity that ought to pull it down.

Directive 2023/970 is the operational confirmation of this structural finding. Whoever reads the recital, whoever counts the obligations, whoever takes note of the German announcement of one-to-one implementation, and whoever then places the losses of industrial jobs, the insolvencies, the shrinking gross domestic product alongside, cannot come to any other conclusion than the one the April essay laid out: the apparatus is no longer capable of checking the relationship between its own texts and reality. The texts are written, they become law, they are implemented, they act upon reality — and no point in the system notices that the effect goes in a different direction from the stated intention.

The directive is meant to help women. What it actually does is place a considerable bureaucratic burden on medium-sized businesses whose existence is already under threat. Those who lose their jobs in the next wave of insolvencies will mostly be not men but women, because women more often work in smaller firms, more often part-time, more often in sectors with lower margins. The directive that is meant to help women harms women — not out of malice, but because the system that produces it can no longer calculate the second order of consequences.

VII. What the Ellipsis Means

The title of this essay ends with three dots. They are not a stylistic figure. They are a statement of fact. The spaceship does not transmit one directive. It transmits one a year, three a year, a dozen a year; half a year after one comes into force the next arrives, and the one after that is in preparation. 2023/970 is merely what is arriving right now. 2024/873 — women on company boards — is already in force. The AI Act, the Critical Raw Materials Act, the Cyber Resilience Act, the NIS 2 Directive, the CSRD reporting obligations, the supply chain law — one wave after another, each one with a recital that cites its own loftiness as an argument, each one in a language that only its own authors understand, each one implemented one-to-one in Germany.

There is a point at which the accumulation of waves ceases to be a quantitative problem and becomes a qualitative one. A medium-sized business can live with one superfluous obligation. It can even live with ten superfluous obligations if the margins allow it. It cannot live with fifty superfluous obligations when the margins no longer allow it. The point at which things tip over is not to be found in any single directive, and that is exactly why it is structurally invisible to the apparatus that produces the directives. Each one on its own can be justified. The sum is lethal. Nobody writes the sum.

That is the real point of the ellipsis. The spaceship keeps transmitting, not because it is malicious, but because it cannot switch off its own transmission. It is built to transmit. It is not built to stop transmitting when the receiver collapses. It does not even know that the receiver is collapsing, because it lacks the return channel that would tell it so.

VIII. What Remains

It is not this essay's reflex to call for a great clear-out. That would be cheap, and it would fall on deaf ears. The spaceship cannot be talked into landing. Nor can it be reformed by its own efforts — the author of the recital sentence from Section I will not withdraw his sentence; at most he will defend it, if need be by reference to the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, which he has already cited three times.

What remains is the smaller and more concrete question of what the national capitals could do that still have some remnant of latitude. The German one-to-one implementation is a political decision. It could be turned into a one-to-one implementation of the minimum obligation, with full use of the exceptions, the transitional periods, the thresholds. That would not be anti-European. It would be the adoption of what other member states do anyway, and what Brussels expressly permits the member states to do. The German announcement of strict one-to-one implementation is not compelled by European law but by a German self-image that is no longer tenable in the present situation.

Whoever tidies his own wardrobe in a burning house has set the wrong priority. The wardrobe can be tidied later; the house must be saved first. That is not an elegant observation. Nor is it new — it appears in the recent series on this site in several places, with other examples, in other turns of phrase. But it is unavoidable as long as Berlin and Brussels proceed as if they were sitting on an island of the blessed, where industrial statistics are something other people do.

As long as that remains so, the spaceship floats. Utterly weightless. And keeps transmitting.
