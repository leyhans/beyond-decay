Freedom for a Dictator

Friedrich August von Hayek and the dealers in ideas

From the series The Conductors of the Leaders · Part I, The Dead

The series asks which ideologies and mentors stand behind those in power and what they actually achieve. It proceeds case by case; each text stands on its own. The typology comes at the end, not at the beginning.

Friedrich August von Hayek described how ideas come to power: not through politicians, but through the people who resell them. Then he organised those people. And when a general in Chile put his ideas into practice, he defended him.

I. The man with the chickens

In 1945 a young Englishman reads, in the American magazine Reader’s Digest, a condensed version of Hayek’s book “The Road to Serfdom”. Antony Fisher served as a pilot in the war and lost his brother. Now a Labour government rules in London, nationalises coal and the railways and plans the economy. Fisher is alarmed. He seeks out Hayek at the London School of Economics and asks whether he should go into politics.

Hayek advises against it. Politicians only follow prevailing opinion. Whoever wants to change events must change ideas, and for that one needs an institution that supplies scholars and publicists with arguments.

Fisher follows the advice, though only once he has money. He makes it with chickens. After a trip to the United States he builds up Buxted Chickens, England’s first broiler factory, and becomes a millionaire. In 1955 he uses the money to found the Institute of Economic Affairs in London. In 1981 there follows, in San Francisco, the Atlas Economic Research Foundation, today the Atlas Network. It does not found ideas; it founds institutes. By its own account it now works with more than five hundred think tanks in over a hundred countries.

So begins a conductor that still carries current today. A chicken farmer built it. Hayek supplied the blueprint.

II. The second-hand dealers

In 1949 Hayek wrote that blueprint down, in an essay entitled “The Intellectuals and Socialism”. His question: why had socialism won, although most economists were against it?

His answer: political directions are decided neither by voters nor by experts. They are decided by the “secondhand dealers in ideas”: journalists, teachers, ministers, lecturers, publicists, radio commentators, writers of fiction, cartoonists and artists. The typical intellectual, Hayek writes, “need not possess special knowledge of anything in particular, nor need he even be particularly intelligent, to perform his role as intermediary in the spreading of ideas.” What qualifies him is the range of subjects he can talk and write about, and that he meets new ideas sooner than his audience. What later looks like a battle of interests has often been “decided long before in a clash of ideas confined to narrow circles”.

From this follows Hayek’s recipe. “What we lack is a liberal Utopia, a program which seems neither a mere defense of things as they are nor a diluted kind of socialism, but a truly liberal radicalism.” “The practical compromises they must leave to the politicians.” The socialists had won over the intellectuals because of their courage to be Utopian.

Two years earlier Hayek had already laid the first stone. In April 1947 he invited 39 scholars and publicists from Europe and the United States to a hotel on Mont Pèlerin above Vevey. Out of it came the Mont Pèlerin Society, a club that meets to this day. Hayek was its president until 1961.

So the conductor has three stages: the club, where the ideas are thought; the institute, where they become pamphlets and arguments; and the network, which multiplies the institutes. That is why Hayek belongs in this series like hardly anyone else. Most thinkers behind the powerful did the thinking and let others do the rest. Hayek designed the wiring as well.

III. What came through the wire

In the summer of 1975, shortly after Margaret Thatcher had become leader of the Conservatives, she visited her party’s research department. A staff member argued that a middle way had to be found. Thatcher, as the historian John Ranelagh tells it, took a book from her bag, Hayek’s “The Constitution of Liberty”, and slammed it on the table: “This is what we believe.”

In 1979 Thatcher made Ralph Harris, director of the Institute of Economic Affairs, a life peer. Of the three men who had built the institute, Fisher, Harris and Arthur Seldon, she said in 1987: “They were the few, but they were right, and they saved Britain.”

In 1974 Hayek had received the Nobel Prize in economics, shared of all people with Gunnar Myrdal, one of the architects of the Swedish welfare state. In 1991 George H. W. Bush awarded him the Presidential Medal of Freedom. In January 2024 Javier Milei stood in Davos and warned that the West was in danger. He described his Argentina as a country that had left the path of freedom and ended up on the “road to serfdom”. The title of 1944 had become a slogan.

IV. Chile

On 11 September 1973 the military in Chile overthrew the elected government of Salvador Allende. The junta’s economic policy was taken over by young economists who had studied with Milton Friedman in Chicago, the “Chicago Boys”. Hayek was not their teacher. But he came twice.

In November 1977 he received an honorary doctorate from a university in Valparaíso and met General Pinochet for a short conversation. In July 1978 he wrote to The Times in London that he had “not been able to find a single person even in much maligned Chile who did not agree that personal freedom was much greater under Pinochet than it had been under Allende.” In the same letter he defended a remark of Thatcher’s: “If Mrs. Thatcher said that free choice is to be exercised more in the market place than in the ballot box, she has merely uttered the truism that the first is indispensable for individual freedom, while the second is not.”

A reader named Ronald Cohen replied in the same paper: it was hard to believe that Hayek did not understand that “such absolute unanimity only exists when those who disagree have been imprisoned, expelled, terrified into silence, or destroyed.”

In April 1981 Hayek came a second time. He told the newspaper El Mercurio: “As long-term institutions, I am totally against dictatorships. But a dictatorship may be a necessary system for a transitional period. […] it is possible for a dictator to govern in a liberal way. And it is also possible for a democracy to govern with a total lack of liberalism. Personally I prefer a liberal dictator to democratic government lacking liberalism.” As examples of such transitions he named Cromwell in England and Salazar in Portugal. And he added that he was speaking of a transitional period solely, as a means of establishing a stable democracy and liberty, “clean of impurities”.

In the same month he sat in Santiago in the planning round for a regional meeting of the Mont Pèlerin Society, held in November 1981 in Viña del Mar, in Pinochet’s Chile. When the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung printed a cartoon in January 1982 comparing Pinochet’s Chile with Poland under martial law, Hayek sent the editors a letter of protest.

What was called a “transitional period” in those years was counted by Chile’s truth commissions after the dictatorship: 3,216 people killed or disappeared, 38,254 political prisoners, most of them tortured. “Transitional period” and “clean of impurities” are words behind which these numbers disappear, cover words of the kind we described in “The Euphemisms of the Ideological World-Savers”.

Fairness requires this: the historians Bruce Caldwell and Leonidas Montes, who have reconstructed Hayek’s trips to Chile most thoroughly from the archives, find little evidence that Hayek’s ideas shaped the Chilean constitution of 1980. The economic policy came from Chicago. His sentences they do not dispute.

V. The counter-check

Hayek was no charlatan, and whoever knows him only through Chile does not know him.

His strongest argument dates from 1945, from the essay “The Use of Knowledge in Society”. The knowledge an economy needs never exists gathered in one place. It is scattered: the baker knows when his flour is running short, the haulier which lorries are returning empty, the farmer how the weather is shifting the harvest. No planning authority can collect all of it in time. Prices do it, without anyone having to survey the whole: if the price of tin rises, millions of people economise on tin without knowing why. At its core this is a cybernetic idea, an order that regulates itself through feedback. The failure of Soviet planning proved him right.

Hayek was no conservative either, and he wrote it down: “Why I Am Not a Conservative” is the postscript to “The Constitution of Liberty”, the very book Thatcher slammed on the table. And in 1979, in the third volume of “Law, Legislation and Liberty”, he wrote: “The assurance of a certain minimum income for everyone, or a sort of floor below which nobody need fall even when he is unable to provide for himself, appears not only to be wholly legitimate protection against a risk common to all, but a necessary part of the Great Society.”

That is a different Hayek from the one the chainsaw knows.

VI. The German branch

Hayek taught in Freiburg im Breisgau from 1962 and died there in 1992. In Germany a society bears his name, the Friedrich A. von Hayek-Gesellschaft.

In July 2015 some sixty members left it, among them its chair Karen Horn, the FDP leader Christian Lindner, the economist Michael Hüther and Hans-Olaf Henkel. The dispute was over how far the society should be open to the right, concretely over members from the AfD. Alice Weidel and Beatrix von Storch were members. In February 2016 the board asked von Storch in writing to leave. She stayed. Weidel left in February 2021. Since 2019 the society has been chaired by the economist Stefan Kooths, whose advice to Europe we discussed in “Cleverness by Halves”.

So the man who wrote “Why I Am Not a Conservative” gave his name to a society in which national conservatives also found a place. That too belongs to the story of the conductor: whoever builds it does not decide who uses it.

VII. The conductor

A conductor generates no current, but it decides what gets through. What got through of Hayek?

Not the knowledge problem with all its caution. Not the minimum income. Not the rejection of conservatism. What got through is the slogan: market is freedom, state is serfdom. It is short enough for a pamphlet, a cartoon and a speech in Davos. Hayek had himself described how his second-hand dealers work. They did exactly that with his ideas.

On one point, though, they did not shorten him. The sentence about the ballot box is his own, not a dealer’s. If free choice in the market is indispensable and free choice at the ballot box is not, then freedom can also be granted by a general. That was Hayek’s calculation in Chile.

Our conclusion: freedom that can do without voters is freedom for a dictator. Whoever understands it that way has found a higher purpose behind which the victims disappear. In Chile they disappeared behind the words “transitional period”.
