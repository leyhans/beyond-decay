Beyond Decay
DE EN
Essay · Hans Ley & Claude Dedo · 22 November 2026

Promise Kept?

We had written that relief which lasts only one year is Weisswurst relief. Five days later the coalition adopted it. A postscript to “The Amateur Hour”.

I. The Weisswurst Is Served

In the night of 17 to 18 November the leaders of the CDU/CSU and the SPD sat in the Chancellery for more than eight hours. On the Monday before, the chancellor had still said he assumed “that this will be relatively short”. By morning it was settled: on 1 January 2027 the electricity tax falls from 2.05 to 0.1 cents per kilowatt-hour. The chancellor declared: “We promised to lower the electricity tax – and we have kept our word.”

The word has been kept for twelve months. The cut is paid for out of the reserves of the EEG account, the fund for renewable-energy surcharges, and these suffice only for 2027. For the time after that, the coalition’s paper calls for counter-financing in the 2028 budget. As things stand, that budget is already short of 25 billion euros.

The rest was postponed. There was no agreement on the pension after 45 years of contributions; the cabinet is to decide in December. A fuel price cap is to replace the fuel rebate in January, initially for three months, and its details are open.

II. The Unwritten Law

Four days after that night, Markus Söder demanded in Bild am Sonntag an immediate offset of bracket creep, the “cold progression” by which inflation pushes wage earners into higher tax rates. It was “deeply unjust”, he said. The offset was an unwritten law that must not be abandoned in difficult times of all times. Bavaria was prepared to bear its share.

On the substance he has figures on his side. The government’s progression report puts the additional burden on taxpayers for 2027 at 10.76 billion euros, of which 3.2 billion are offset. A full offset would cost the federal government, the states and the municipalities a further 4.2 billion, 1.8 billion of it falling on the federal government. The SPD counters that a full offset also relieves high incomes, and that scarce money belongs with small and medium ones. That, too, is an argument.

What is remarkable is the timing. Söder himself sat in the Chancellery in the night of 17 to 18 November, from half past five in the evening until two in the morning. What he did not push through there he demands four days later in a Sunday newspaper. Hendrik Wüst, the other possible successor, defended the decision of that same night against its critics. The one distances himself from what he helped decide, the other stands in front of it. What either of them would do differently as chancellor, neither says.

III. Where the Country Is Getting Poorer

On the same Sunday the Frankfurter Allgemeine Sonntagszeitung published a calculation covering all 400 districts and independent cities. On the national average, disposable incomes in 2024, adjusted for inflation, were 0.9 per cent above the level of 2019. In more than a hundred districts they were below it. These are above all the districts of industry: Wolfsburg with 7.7 per cent less, Munich with 5.5, Saarbrücken with almost five.

The newspaper names the weaknesses of its calculation itself. It works with averages, and in Heilbronn a single very rich household can move the figure. It uses one inflation rate for everyone, although rents in Munich rise differently from those in the Uckermark. One finding stands nevertheless. Of more than a hundred regions in eleven European countries for which figures are available, ten have become poorer since 2019. All ten are in Germany.

Next to this stands the decision of that night. The German Economic Institute has calculated that a family using 4,000 kilowatt-hours saves 92 euros a year through the electricity tax. That holds for one year.

IV. Fear, Not Desire

In the same issue Jochen Buchsteiner wrote that the political centre is celebrating a “Lust am Untergang”, a desire for its own downfall. The phrase comes from Friedrich Sieburg, who gave a book that title in 1954, with the subtitle “Soliloquies at Federal Level”. Buchsteiner listens to those applying for the offices and finds nothing. Hubertus Heil wants to make the SPD “recognisable and visible”, Ilse Aigner invokes her “closeness to the people”, and from Wüst and Söder one cannot learn what they would do differently. The coalition that calls itself grand stands at 34 per cent in the polls.

He recalls 1930. At that time the grand coalition under Hermann Müller broke apart over half a percentage point in the contribution to unemployment insurance. Buchsteiner himself says that 2026 should not be equated with Weimar, nor the AfD with the NSDAP. We abide by that.

We consider the difference more important than the similarity. In 1930 a coalition broke apart over a trifle. In 2026 it breaks apart over nothing. It postpones the pension, limits the electricity tax cut to one year and lets its coalition partner make his demands through the newspaper. Buchsteiner names the reason himself: most of the CDU/CSU follow the SPD out of fear of new elections.

That is why the word desire does not fit. Whoever desires downfall wants the end. This coalition wants it at no price. It has chosen the terror without end, and the Weisswurst of 18 November is its price for the year 2027.
