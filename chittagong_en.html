Chittagong

Three states of emergency, one port — and the long arcs of history
March 2026 · Authors: Hans Ley & Claude (Anthropic)

1969. 1970. 2026. Three times the port of Chittagong has been plunged into crisis by decisions made and caused elsewhere.

I. Patenga, February – March 1969

The port of Chittagong lies on the Bay of Bengal, where the Karnaphuli river meets the sea. In the district of Patenga, a few kilometres south of the city centre, a factory is being built. Eastern Cables Limited — a complete facility for the manufacture of cables and wires. The general contractor is the Hamburg trading firm Coutinho, Caro & Co.; the cable technology comes from Kabel-Werke Reinshagen of Wuppertal; the extrusion equipment for PVC insulation comes from Reifenhäuser, Troisdorf. With these machines, a young man travels from Much in the Bergisches Land — 22 years old. He is not a fitter in the classical sense — he is a heavy-current electrician from the electrical testing department at Reifenhäuser, where he trained from 1963 to 1967. The weather is pleasant. It is the best time of year for Chittagong.

Outside on the streets, history is in turmoil. Ayub Khan, dictator of Pakistan for eleven years, is losing control. In Dhaka and Chittagong, hundreds of thousands demonstrate — students, workers, farmers, united against a regime that has treated East Pakistan as a colony since independence in 1947. West Pakistan collects the foreign exchange earned by Eastern jute and tea. East Pakistan gets uniforms back. In February 1969, Ayub Khan resigns. Yahya Khan, army chief, takes power.

II. Patenga, Monsoon Season 1970

The electrician is back. The equipment is in a desolate state. The entire technical staff of the plant had let the machines rust and rot — which happens very quickly in the extreme climate. After weeks of laborious work: done. All machines are running. The factory is accepted — technically flawless, according to German standards, VDE specifications, highest quality.

There is only one problem: it had been neglected to order sufficient quantities of copper. Without raw material, no production. The most finished — and only — cable factory in Bangladesh, which will not become Bangladesh for another six months, stands still again.

The weather is terrible. It is monsoon season. The young electrician is glad when he can leave.

Shortly after his departure, on 12–13 November 1970, the Bhola cyclone strikes the coast of East Pakistan. Between 300,000 and 500,000 people die — the most severe tropical storm ever recorded. The response of the central government in West Pakistan is catastrophically inadequate. The outrage in East Pakistan knows no bounds. In December 1970, the Awami League wins elections by a majority that West Pakistan refuses to accept.

On 1 March 1971, Eastern Cables Limited begins commercial production — without the electrician from Much, who has long since been home and has no desire for further dangerous adventures. The copper has finally arrived. The machines were presumably kept in working order this time, because they run. Three weeks later, in the night of 25–26 March, the Pakistani army launches Operation Searchlight. Dhaka is overrun by tanks. The massacre begins, later classified as genocide — between 300,000 and three million dead in nine months of war. In December 1971 India intervenes. The war is over in fourteen days. Bangladesh is free.

The factory in Patenga survives. It is nationalised, becomes part of the Bangladesh Steel and Engineering Corporation. Eastern Cables Limited produces to this day — the largest cable manufacturer in the country.

III. Chittagong, 2026

Fifty-five years later. On the banks of the Karnaphuli, a few kilometres from Eastern Cables, stands Eastern Refinery Limited — Bangladesh’s only oil refinery. Commissioned in 1968. A state enterprise. It looks like one too: rusty pipes and barrels on the site, men in uniforms killing time. From this refinery, 176 million people depend.

On 28 February 2026, Israel and the United States attack Iran. The Strait of Hormuz has been de facto closed ever since. Around 100 tankers lie at anchor off the coast of Bangladesh — to the horizon. The refinery normally processes crude oil from Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates and Iraq, arriving via the Strait of Hormuz in around 16 days. That is over for now.

The problem is also technical. The refinery cannot process Russian crude oil — too viscous for 1960s technology. The same constraint that binds Eastern Cables Limited: technology from that era, which has carried the country to this day — and is now letting it down. Qatar, meanwhile, has halted liquefied gas production following Iranian attacks on its facilities. A third of the gas Bangladesh needed came from there.

Universities have been closed since 9 March. At petrol stations in Dhaka, motorcyclists queue for kilometres, blocking the streets. Four to six weeks of reserves remain. Then the country stops. India has offered 5,000 tonnes of diesel — against a daily requirement of 12,000 tonnes. The new government, in office only since mid-February — the first since Generation Z toppled the autocrat Sheikh Hasina in August 2024 — is running out of time.

Bangladesh is what it has always been: a country that produces what the world needs — nearly one in ten T-shirts worldwide comes from here — but that does not control the basis of that production. Resource-poor, export-oriented, dependent on supply chains interrupted elsewhere. The cables manufactured in the Patenga factory conduct the electricity now being rationed.

IV. What the Three Moments Have in Common

Chittagong has been plunged into crisis three times by decisions made elsewhere. In 1969 in the power struggles of West Pakistan. In 1970 in the barracks of Rawalpindi. In 2026 in the military headquarters of Tel Aviv and Washington.

This is no peculiarity of Chittagong. It is the fate of every place that sits at the end of a long supply chain — and bears the final costs of decisions made at the beginning of that chain. Bangladesh has no atomic bomb, no strategic oil reserves, no veto power in the UN Security Council. It has cables, textiles, and 170 million people who need to cook food.

Global history writes its greatest dramas in the places that do not appear in the main news. Chittagong 1971 was not a subject in German newspapers — until the bodies could be counted. Chittagong 2026 is a footnote in the Iran war — until the textile factories close and the clothes are missing from European shops.

History is not what happens in the capitals. History is what arrives in the ports.

V. The Electrician and the Equipment

An inventor from Nuremberg, 79 years old, reads in March 2026 an article about the blackout in Bangladesh. He remembers a port that was in a state of emergency twice in his lifetime. He remembers machines he installed — in a country that did not yet exist, for a factory that is still producing today.

The equipment is 55 years old. It is still running. The history of the country it serves has taken more turns in that time than most countries manage in a century: genocide, war of liberation, independence, military coup, flood catastrophes, economic miracle, another coup in 2024, now an energy crisis.

The cables hold — and conduct, when there is something to conduct.
