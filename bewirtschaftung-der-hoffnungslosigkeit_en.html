Cashing In on Hopelessness

On a market where collapse is for sale

A 53-minute video about Ukraine promises “the sober reckoning of a collapse” and reckons nothing. It names no name, no date and no source. At the end it invites viewers into a “club of realists”. The channel behind it sells collapse, country by country. What it offers is not analysis but a feeling, and there is a market for it.

I. A collapse without a sender

The channel is called “Anatomiya Mira”, Anatomy of the World. It is Russian-language, has a little over fifty thousand subscribers and just under eighty videos. Anyone calling it up from Germany sees the titles machine-translated. New Zealand becomes a “fool’s paradise”, the Netherlands are “ruled by bandits”, Denmark is rotten, Russia “a dead country”. And Ukraine gets its “anatomy of collapse: how an entire nation was used and dropped”. The video appeared in April 2026 and has been watched more than a quarter of a million times.

Anyone who reads the text spoken beneath the pictures finds nothing in it that could be checked. The country is almost never named; it is “the country”, “these territories”, “the eastern region”. No politician, no ministry, no city, no court case. The only figure that sticks is “more than eight million” who have supposedly left the country for good. The UN refugee agency counted around 5.9 million refugees from Ukraine abroad at the end of 2025, and 3.7 million people displaced inside the country. The eight million are neither the one nor the other. They are a number meant to sound big.

What the text offers instead of evidence is images: the ventilator to which the economy is attached, the concrete block falling on people’s heads, the feast during the plague, the pawns on the chessboard. The adjectives come in threes, “cold, hard and absolutely merciless”. Every chapter ends with the same sentence in a different form: it is irreversible. There is no way out. No one will come back.

Some of it is true. The corruption at the conscription offices, the flight of the young, the rift between front line and hinterland, the demographic situation: Ukrainian media report on all of this themselves. The method is to fuse what is true, without evidence and without measure, with the final. Whoever says that something is crumbling can be refuted. Whoever says that everything is lost forever cannot.

Who is behind the channel cannot be determined. There is no name, no imprint, only a link to Telegram and a button marked “Join”. Whether anyone commissioned the videos we do not know, and we do not speculate about it. We see only that the same template fits Russia as well as Denmark. That points to a business rather than a commission.

II. Sadness sells

That the business works has been well studied. A team led by Claire Robertson of New York University and Stefan Feuerriegel of Ludwig Maximilian University in Munich analysed, in 2023, some 105,000 headline variants that the website Upworthy had tested against each other, with more than 370 million impressions between them. Each additional negative word in a headline raised the click-through rate by 2.3 per cent. Each positive word lowered it by one per cent.

The remarkable thing was a side finding. It was not anger that drove the clicks, as had been assumed, but sadness. The Ukraine video is built exactly that way. It is not outraged; it laments. It is not an outburst of rage but a dirge.

Dirges are an old trade. Many cultures had professional mourners, women paid to make the grief at a funeral loud enough. They did not know the dead and did not need to. Their craft was not remembrance but the emotion of the living. The channel does the same, only with countries instead of the dead, and with advertising revenue instead of a funeral feast.

III. The factory

Behind individual channels there is by now an industry. In March 2026 the Atlantic Council’s Digital Forensic Research Lab described a network of 26 YouTube channels in English and Russian. They imitated news broadcasts, had their scripts written and spoken by machines, placed artificial news anchors in front of artificial studio sets and slipped in invented events. Together they reached nearly two billion views.

In August 2026 the American news site Semafor uncovered a second wave: channels painting the economic collapse of California, New York and Illinois, with empty supermarkets, fleeing companies and teachers quitting. Governor Newsom’s team alone had had more than 700 accounts removed since January. In September came a third revelation. A network paid actors 26 dollars a video to read machine-written scripts on camera, because real faces get around the detection of AI content. It reached 45 million views, more than the YouTube channels of the Washington Post and the New Yorker. YouTube shut down twenty channels. The reason was not that they had spread falsehoods, but spam.

That reasoning contains the core. The platform does not check whether a collapse is real. It checks whether it is produced too often and too uniformly. Whether a factory sends Russia, California or Ukraine to its doom is a matter of indifference to the platform, and to the operators too. The product is not a political direction. The product is collapse.

IV. Fleeing into the arms of the mourners

Here lies a contradiction that dissolves on closer inspection. According to the Reuters Institute’s Digital News Report, 40 per cent of people worldwide avoid the news at least sometimes, up from 29 per cent in 2017. In Germany 71 per cent of internet users say they avoid the news at least occasionally. The most common reason: the news spoils their mood. Worldwide, one in five gives as a reason that they feel powerless.

The same people click on headlines that make them sad. Taken together, the two form a cycle, and from it we draw a conclusion that is not proof: whoever turns away from the news because it weighs them down does not leave the house but changes rooms. They end up with providers who tell them that their dejection is the real truth and the news merely “gloss”. The “club of realists” takes them in. They belong because they see the end coming, and they need do nothing more.

Add to this something psychology has described since Martin Seligman’s experiments in the 1960s as learned helplessness. Whoever experiences again and again that nothing they do helps eventually stops trying, even when the door has long been open. A video that repeats for 53 minutes that there is no way out is a training programme for that state.

V. Gloss and abyss

The video claims to stand against the “polished news from the West”. In fact it is its mirror image. The cheerful reports say: everything is going well, you need do nothing. The doom videos say: everything is lost, you can do nothing. Both turn citizens into spectators. One soothes, the other paralyses, and the result is the same.

Who profits is obvious at the first level: the producer. Long videos carry a lot of advertising, membership brings monthly fees, the Telegram channel binds the viewers. A text with a perspective releases its reader: they have understood what could be done, and they leave. A text that shows only collapse releases no one. The viewer comes back for the next collapse. Despair is a renewable resource.

At the second level we draw a conclusion that cannot be proven and which we therefore mark as such: hopelessness benefits everyone who lives off the inaction of others, without their having to order anything. If everything is irreversible, nothing is worth doing any more, neither voting nor protesting, neither founding nor building. Whoever sits at the top needs no advertising for themselves. It is enough for them if those below believe that nothing can be changed. The Megamachine needs no driver for this. It exploits whatever happens.

On this site we have already described other forms of such cultivation: of the longing for meaning, of conflicts, of an inglorious past. Hopelessness is the cheapest of them all. It promises nothing, so it can owe nothing.

VI. How to recognise the chronicler

At this point we have to examine ourselves. This site is called beyond decay. We too describe decline, we too write without mercy, we too show what is going wrong. What separates us from “Anatomiya Mira”? Not conviction, but craft. Four rules by which any reader can measure us:

Verifiability. Names, dates, sources. Every figure must be traceable, every claim attributable to an author. Where there is no evidence, we say so, and we do not speculate.

Measure. What is crumbling we call crumbling. We call something irreversible only when it can be shown. Whoever speaks only in finalities has stopped observing.

Correction. We check our own findings. Addenda such as “Five Months Later” or “From Both Shelves” show what became of a diagnosis, even when things turned out differently than expected.

Perspective. Whoever describes a decline also asks where there might be a way out, however narrow. A diagnosis without the question of treatment is not medicine but an autopsy.

If one of these rules falls away, even a merciless analysis slides into a merciless tone. And tone alone is what the factories sell.

There is one more difference, and it is no merit. No one earns money from these texts. There is no advertising, no membership, no reach that could be turned into money. We write, as it says in “For the Small Ones”, for those who will later ask whether no one was paying attention. An archive does not need to hold on to anyone.

The videos tell the viewer that they can do nothing, and then ask them to subscribe. A chronicler says what they have seen, and lets the reader go.
