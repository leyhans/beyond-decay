Process and Procedure

Why an established society leaves no room for the new

A process is the journey, a procedure the directions. A society that knows nothing but directions no longer arrives anywhere nobody has been before.

I. The Journey and the Directions

Dough rises, a river carves its bed, a child learns to speak. These are processes: something changes, step by step, from a beginning to a result. A process runs whether anyone has written it down or not.

The recipe for the dough, the pilot's checklist before take-off, the service instruction in a government office are procedures. A procedure is a prescribed sequence of moves. Someone made it so that others would follow it.

Three things follow. A process can run without a procedure; nature has none. A procedure can miss the process: whoever acts strictly by the book can fulfil every rule and still miss the result. That is why "work to rule" is a form of strike. And procedures are written for what is already known. For the new there are none yet.

II. What Procedures Achieve

Procedures have a good reason. They protect against arbitrariness. A stranger's building application is examined by the same rules as that of the mayor's friend. The checklist in the cockpit stops a tired captain from forgetting the flaps. Counting the swabs in the operating theatre stops one from being left inside a patient.

A society without any procedures would not be an open society. It would be a society of connections, in which whoever knows someone gets what they want. Anyone who rails against bureaucracy should know that.

III. When Only Procedures Remain

Things break when there is no room left beside the procedures. Then every new situation is bent until it fits an existing form. Whatever does not fit does not happen. The office does not say the project is bad. It says it is not responsible, and the office that would be responsible does not exist.

In an established society like Germany's this is the normal case. Open processes, whose outcome nobody knows, are a nuisance, because they fall under no one's remit and nobody is liable for them. What changes all the same changes out of accidents and emergencies, slowly, and mostly only once an old procedure has visibly failed.

This is not a moral problem but a cybernetic one. In 1956 William Ross Ashby showed that a system can only regulate as many disturbances as it has responses at its disposal. We have already applied this law of requisite variety to politics and to the enterprise. Here it shows its simplest side: a society that only repeats procedures has only the answers it already had. As long as the world outside keeps still, that works. If it changes quickly, in energy, in industry, in technology, the answer that had not been written down in advance is missing.

IV. Cutting Red Tape by the Book

This shows most clearly where bureaucracy is supposed to shrink itself. Germany's National Regulatory Control Council has existed for twenty years. In its 2026 annual report it states that between July 2025 and June 2026 it examined 437 federal drafts for their costs. The annual burden that laws impose on citizens, businesses and administration fell by 6.8 billion euros in that period, almost entirely through a single law on modernising buildings. Pure bureaucracy costs rose by 1.3 billion euros over the same period.

So procedures are measured with a procedure, the balance is drawn in a report, and another body is set up for relief. It is like trying to drain a swamp with water.

Fairness requires naming the counterpart as well. Since 10 October 2026 Germany's Federal Testing Act has been in force. It allows new technology and new administrative procedures to be tried out in regulatory sandboxes, partly outside current law, but under the supervision of the authorities. It contains a general testing clause, and anyone who runs into a legal hurdle can report it to a portal of the Ministry for Economic Affairs. That is a real step. Transport law has had something similar for a long time: for new kinds of transport, the authority can, on application and case by case, approve deviations for at most five years, provided public transport interests do not stand in the way.

But one also sees what this means. For an open process to be allowed to take place, it takes a law, an application, an examination, a deadline and supervision. The state of exception for the new is itself a procedure. That it had to be created specially shows how closed the normal case is.

V. Why the New Has No Procedure

This hits invention hardest. An invention is by definition something that appears in no procedure. Alexander Fleming would not have found penicillin had he disposed of the mouldy dish as prescribed. A cook who never departs from the recipe cooks well and invents no dish.

Large organisations nevertheless try to capture inventing in procedures: idea management, innovation funnels, phase models with stage gates. What comes out is the administration of the known. The proposal that fits no grid fails at the first gate, not because it is bad but because the form has no field for it. The organisation then calls it immature. Whatever was not invented in-house, or not by the person in charge, has it twice as hard.

Whoever thinks anew therefore almost always stands outside. How free inventors fare there we have described. What becomes of the yield of an invention once it exists is shown in Forty Minutes.

VI. A Place for Open Processes

Laws against laws and forms against forms will not solve this. A society needs places where open processes are permitted and carried. Permitted means: nobody has to know in advance what will come out at the end. Carried means: someone pays for the journey, and the yield flows back to where the next journey begins.

A procedure needs administrators, a process needs a cycle. What such a cycle for inventing can look like we have sketched in the concept of an inventors' cooperative. It is not the only possible model. But as long as there is no such model, all that remains for the new in Germany is the exception, and the exception is a procedure.
