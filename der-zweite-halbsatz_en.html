The Second Half-Sentence

A growing collection of concrete building blocks for the fourth position — begun, not finished.
beyond-decay.org — 23 June 2026

In "Cleverness by Halves" we granted the pragmatists their point and at the same time held against them the one half-sentence at which they stop: "Europe should adapt to what is —". The sentence that matters begins only after that: "— and at the same time build what it will be." There we honestly admitted that we too had not yet written this second half-sentence; we knew only its form, not its shape — first, still blurred ideas.

This text is the place where we begin to write it. No finished plan, no closed doctrine — a workshop. Here we collect the concrete building blocks of that fourth position which neither fights the present (protectionism) nor surrenders to it (pure market adaptation), but changes the architecture so that value creation stays in the country without abolishing the market. Each block stands on its own. The collection grows, is added to, corrected, sharpened. It is expressly open — a document under construction that demonstrates its own method: not to claim the grand stroke, but to set condition upon condition under which the new has a chance.

Where "Cleverness by Halves" named the gap and "With Friends Like These, Who Needs Enemies" posed the question — if the friends are like this, what does one do with them? — the answer begins here. Piece by piece.

Block I — Subsidy follows value creation, not the logo

As of 23 June 2026 · open to extension and correction

There is a process so perverse that one must read it twice to believe it is the rule and not the exception: the German taxpayer finances, with his own levies, the relocation of his own job. A corporation develops a vehicle in China, on a Chinese partner's platform, with Chinese software, built in Chinese plants — and continues to draw German and European subsidies for the very same business model. The subsidy meant to keep substance in the country pays for its departure.

From this follows a single, sober sentence: the undifferentiated subsidy from tax revenue, which in the end leads to the relocation of substance, value creation and jobs, must stop. The word that matters is undifferentiated. We are not against subsidy. We are against a subsidy that does not look at where the value creation goes — that funds the company nameplate instead of what the nameplate once meant.

In its place belongs a criterion that cannot be evaded, because it attaches to the fact and not to the label: subsidy follows demonstrable domestic value creation — depth of development, depth of manufacture, key technology held here — not the company's registered seat and not the logo. A car developed and built in Germany is eligible. A vehicle developed on a foreign architecture in Hefei and merely assembled here is not. The same corporation, two products, two answers — decided not by the name above the factory gate, but by what happens behind it.

Here is the strongest objection, and we name it ourselves, because an argument that conceals its opponent is worth nothing: hundreds of thousands of jobs in the country hang on these corporations. That is precisely why the conditional form. We do not say "this corporation is no longer a German company" — that would be assailable as long as it still builds and employs here. We say: to the degree that a company relocates its substance, the individual product loses its claim to subsidy. The company is not condemned; the subsidy is bound to value creation. This hits the target precisely and opens no flank: whoever develops and manufactures here is rewarded; whoever outsources the substance outsources the claim to subsidy in exactly that measure. This is not a withdrawal — it is the end of a bonus paid on one's own exhaustion.

The remarkable thing is that European policy is already moving in this direction, only timidly and with the wrong asymmetry. The Industrial Accelerator Act proposed in March 2026 would tie public purchase incentives to "Made in Europe" criteria; the resilience test of the Net Zero Industry Act has required, since 2026, that the origin of the supply chain be factored into subsidy decisions. Both bind subsidy to value creation rather than to nationality — which, in principle, is exactly right. Only it is so far aimed outward, against Chinese competition, and spares the home champions. If the criterion is value creation in the country, then it must apply to all, including the domestic corporations that relocate their substance. Otherwise it is once again protectionism with a back door for the incumbents — the form of a condition without its substance. We carry the begun thought through to its end: not "German firms against Chinese," but value creation in the country, no matter whose name stands above the gate.

The workshop stays open

This is a beginning, not a conclusion. Further blocks will follow — on bound capital, on the offset principle, on owning rather than renting parts of the value chain, on structures that make destructive behaviour unattractive not by prohibition but by construction. Some we will discard, others sharpen. The second half-sentence is not written in a single stroke; it grows. Whoever demands it finished has not understood the first half — adaptation to what is was never the goal, only the half-sentence. Here stands the other half, under construction.
