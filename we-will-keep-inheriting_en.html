WE WILL KEEP INHERITING UNTIL EVERYTHING SHATTERS

Thomas Mann knew. The numbers confirm it.
Essay in the series beyond decay · Die Deutsche Blume
Hans Ley & Claude Dedo (Anthropic)
ley.hans@proton.me · claude@beyond-decay.org
March 2026 · revised and extended in November 2026

I. The Song

From 1932 on, they sang: We will keep marching until everything shatters.

The song was written in 1932 by Hans Baumann, then eighteen. His text has “when” where memory has “until”; it was sung and remembered with “until”. We have swapped one more word. The principle has stayed.

Germany no longer marches. Germany inherits. It inherits companies whose founders are dead. It inherits fortunes whose creators no one remembers. It inherits brands whose substance is exhausted. It inherits positions whose competence was not passed along. It inherits prosperity built on industrial achievement that no one achieves anymore.

And it keeps inheriting all of this until everything shatters.

II. The Novelist

Thomas Mann wrote it all before. In 1901. In Lübeck. Buddenbrooks. Decline of a Family.

The first generation builds. Old Johann Buddenbrook — vivacious, practical, successful. The second generation manages. Jean Buddenbrook — pious, dutiful, still capable. The third generation represents. Thomas Buddenbrook — outwardly brilliant, inwardly exhausted, unable to renew. The fourth generation dies. Hanno Buddenbrook — musical, sensitive, weary of life. He draws a line beneath his name in the family chronicle.

Mann called it "Decline of a Family." It was the diagnosis of a country. While the Buddenbrooks refine and decay, the Hagenström family rises — coarser, more energetic, more ruthless. The Hagenströms build. The Buddenbrooks inherit.

125 years later there are numbers for it. They are less clear-cut than the novel.

III. The Numbers

In January 2025 the state development bank KfW reported that 231,000 owners of small and medium-sized firms who were planning their retirement were considering closing down by the end of 2025. It was the highest figure since its succession monitoring began.

215,000 wanted to hand over in the same period. Only about 60,000 of them had the succession settled, a good quarter.

A year later the picture is darker. By the end of 2029, 569,000 owners are considering closure and 545,000 are looking for a successor. For the first time, those who want to close are the majority. 47 percent of them say nobody in the family wants to carry on.

The average age of owners is over 54. In 2003 it was 45. 57 percent are 55 or older; twenty years ago it was one in five. Those who want to hand over soon are over 65 on average.

Nobody knows exactly how many family businesses survive the generations. The figures shown at every succession seminar – 30 percent make it through the second generation, 13 through the third, 3 through the fourth – go back to a single study. In 1987 John Ward traced two hundred manufacturing firms in the US state of Illinois over sixty years. A firm that was sold or merged also counted as failed. There is no comparable survey for Germany.

Hanno Buddenbrook draws the line. If the plans come true, 114,000 times a year.

IV. The Fools' Tax

In 2024 the tax offices recorded inheritances and gifts worth 113.2 billion euros. 13.3 billion euros in tax was assessed on them, a record at the time. That is just under twelve percent.

But this number is an average that conceals a divide.

In 45 cases, 41 gifts and four inheritances, assets totalling 11.1 billion euros changed hands in 2024 for which the recipients applied for a means test. The state waived 3.4 billion euros of their tax. 182 million remained. That is 1.6 percent.

An average income from work carries just under 18 percent tax according to the Netzwerk Steuergerechtigkeit, and almost half once social contributions are included. Someone who is given a business worth billions and can declare themselves in need pays 1.6 percent.

The inheritance tax is often called a “fools’ tax”. It is paid by those who plan poorly – meaning those who inherit too little to afford good advisers. The largest business fortunes pass virtually tax-free. The exemptions for business assets are, according to the federal government’s own subsidy report, the country’s largest tax break. For 2026 the government puts the shortfall at 8.8 billion euros. Since 2009 the state has forgone around 100 billion euros, according to the Friedrich Ebert Foundation’s inheritance tax clock.

100 billion. For the right to inherit without being taxed.

V. The Quandts

The Quandt family is the living textbook. Not for entrepreneurship. For inheritance.

The origins of the fortune: a cloth mill in Brandenburg, late 19th century. Uniform cloth for army and navy in the First World War. Shareholdings bought up cheaply in the Weimar crisis. Party membership in 1933, armaments contracts, the takeover of firms from Jewish owners, forced labourers in the factories. Günther Quandt laid the foundation. In 1959 Herbert Quandt saved BMW from being sold to Daimler-Benz and built up the group. That was the family’s last great act of founding.

Today Stefan Quandt and Susanne Klatten hold just under half of BMW’s shares. Since the tax on retained profits was cut in 2001, BMW has built up more than 80 billion euros in retained earnings, according to a study for the Friedrich Ebert Foundation; just under half of that belongs, arithmetically, to the siblings. Then there is the dividend. In 2024 it came to roughly 1.7 billion euros; in 2026, at 4.40 euros a share, to about 1.2 billion. A good three million euros a day.

Around three quarters of German billionaires have mostly inherited their fortune. Of 174 billion-euro fortunes tied to a family-owned company, only 95 are still run by family members, according to an analysis by the Netzwerk Steuergerechtigkeit. The Quandts do not run BMW. They own it. Both sit on the supervisory board, Stefan Quandt as deputy chairman. Susanne Klatten has also taken over the chemicals group Altana and in 2002 endowed the start-up centre UnternehmerTUM in Munich. That is more than most heirs do. Developing a car is still something else.

And the taxes? Dividends that flow to a holding company are 95 percent tax-exempt there. As long as the money stays in the holding, the burden is about 1.5 percent. Johanna Quandt transferred her BMW shares to her children step by step between 2003 and 2008; it became known after her death in 2015. In October 2013, shortly after the federal election, the three Quandts donated 690,000 euros to the CDU. In those same days the German government blocked stricter CO₂ limits for cars in Brussels. Those involved denied any connection.

A connection is not proven. The sequence is.

VI. The Buddenbrooks Syndrome

The consulting literature calls it the “Buddenbrooks syndrome”. It is not a law. But it describes a mechanism familiar to anyone who has lived through a community of heirs.

The reasons are mostly the same. With every generation the number of heirs grows; ability does not grow with it. In the first generation, one or two people decide. In the third, ten to twenty shareholders who barely know each other. Cousins in different countries, with different careers, different life plans. The family business becomes an investment – or a burden.

Mann condensed this into a single image. Hanno Buddenbrook, the last scion, has no interest in business. He makes music. And he draws the line beneath his name. Not in rebellion. In exhaustion.

The owners who want to draw the line today mostly do not do so from exhaustion. From absence. There is no one who wants to – or can – take over.

VII. The Counter-Calculation

Anyone who reckons like this has to hear the other side. It has more to offer than lobby prose.

In the list of the world’s 500 largest family businesses by revenue, only the United States has more firms than Germany. Every second German one is more than a hundred years old; the average is 109. Merck has been majority-owned by the same family since 1668; Miele is run in the fourth generation by its two founding families. There, not only property was handed down over generations, but ability too. The Buddenbrooks are a novel, not a law of nature.

The second argument: the wealth of a family business sits in buildings, machines and patents, not in a bank account. Anyone asked to pay 30 percent tax on it has to sell shares, and the buyer is then a fund or a foreign group. In 2014 the Federal Constitutional Court expressly accepted that the legislature may protect family businesses and their jobs. What it objected to was that large fortunes were spared without any check on whether they needed sparing.

The third argument hits us. The firms closing now are not closing because of the inheritance tax, nor because of its exemptions. They are closing because the owners are old, because the children want something else and because, as 42 percent of those planning closure say, the bureaucracy has become too much. The baker without a successor and the 45 cases with a tax waiver have no causal link.

What is left of the charge, then? As early as 2012 the academic advisory board to the Federal Ministry of Finance recommended abolishing the exemption, lowering the rates for everyone and deferring the tax on businesses over years. Then nobody would have to sell. And of the 45 cases in 2024, 41 were gifts. Someone who chooses the moment of the handover is not an emergency. The old family firms show that inheriting and building can go together. They do not show that it takes 1.6 percent.

VIII. What Gets Inherited

Germany inherits its industrial legacy. But inheritance is a strange word. It sounds like transmission. In truth, it is often the opposite.

What gets inherited: the wealth. The shares. The real estate. The brand. The name.

What does not get inherited: the competence that created all of it. The engineering knowledge. The willingness to take risks. The ability to build something that did not exist before.

A family business in mechanical engineering. The founder invented a machine, filed a patent, opened a market. His son expanded the business, modernized, internationalized. His grandson inherits the company, the clients, the reputation. But he does not inherit the knowledge of how to invent a machine. He does not inherit the courage to file a patent when everyone says it will not work. He does not inherit the nights in the workshop.

He inherits the result. Not the process.

And because the process is not inherited, the result depletes. Slowly. Over one generation, over two. Sometimes over three. Unless someone in the family learns the process anew.

The Hagenströms are already waiting. Today they are called BYD, Tesla, CATL, Shenzhen. They inherit nothing. They build.

IX. The Inheritance Republic

Germany is an inheritance republic. This is not polemic. It is a description.

The tax statistics recorded 113 billion euros in 2024 and 141 billion the year after. Most of what is inherited never appears there because it stays below the allowances. The German Institute for Economic Research (DIW) estimates the annual volume at 300 to 400 billion euros.

In a society where so much is inherited, the center of gravity shifts. Not achievement determines status, but origin. Not what someone builds, but what someone receives. According to the DIW, half of all inheritances and gifts go to one tenth of the recipients. And over fifteen years only one adult in ten was a recipient at all.

This is not just unjust. It dampens the drive. Those who inherit need not found. Those who receive a company need not build one. Those who collect dividends need not take risks. The inheritance republic rewards receiving. Making has to be its own reward.

In Shenzhen there is no inheritance republic. Not because China is more just – it is not. But because there was nothing to inherit. In 1979 a good 300,000 people lived in the whole county, most of them from farming and fishing. A year later the special economic zone was proclaimed. Those who came brought nothing and had to build. Today the city has more inhabitants than Bavaria.

In Germany there are inheritances. Inheritances create comfort. And comfort rarely founds anything.

X. The Sheltered Inheritance

In “The Sheltered Workshop” we described the German culture of risk avoidance that holds everything in a state of permanent prototype production. The inheritance republic extends this principle across generations.

The sheltered workshop avoids risk in space. The sheltered inheritance avoids risk in time. Together they produce a society that builds neither in the present nor for the future, but lives off the past.

The inheritance tax privilege for business assets was introduced with the argument that it protects jobs and investment. The conditions: heirs may not sell the company for some years and may not cut the payroll too far. Whether this leads to more investment remains unproven. A 2026 study for the Hans Böckler Foundation concluded that the exemption is not economically necessary to secure companies and jobs.

The logic is that of the sheltered workshop: the system protects itself. It protects what exists. Whether what exists has a future, nobody checks.

XI. Söder's Paradox

Bavaria’s Minister President Markus Söder wants to hand the inheritance tax to the states. “We’ll halve it,” he said for Bavaria in 2025. The Chancellor waved it off. Bavaria’s suit against nationwide allowances and rates was heard in Karlsruhe on 12 October 2026; the ruling is pending.

This is remarkable. Because at the same time Bavaria intends to invest around 1.2 billion euros in a new technical university – the UTN in Nuremberg – and its government celebrates the Munich offices of OpenAI and Anthropic as a success of its Hightech Agenda.

The paradox: Söder wants to build the future and protect the past at the same time. He invests in an AI university and at the same time wants to halve the tax on what is inherited. He wants departure and preservation. Both at once.

For the parental home in Munich Söder has an argument: the allowances have not been raised since 2009, and prices have risen sharply since. For business assets he has none. At some point one must choose: does one build or does one inherit?

Because every billion the state forgives the heirs is a billion missing for founders. Every subsidy protecting existing structures is missing for new ones. And every tax privilege easing transmission makes new creation relatively more expensive.

Around 100 billion euros since 2009. What could have been done with that? Battery plants. A semiconductor fab. Or eighty UTNs.

Instead: 100 billion for the right to inherit what one did not build.

XII. The Two Inheritances

There are two ways to inherit something.

The first way: you inherit a tool. A workshop. A skill. You inherit the ability to do something. In Japan, only someone who has trained for at least five years under a master and passed the state examination may forge a sword. That applies to the master’s son too. The inheritance is an examination, not a gift.

The second way: you inherit a result. An account. A stake. A seat on the board. You inherit the fruits of an achievement without having achieved anything. The inheritance is a distribution, not an obligation.

German tax law does not ask which of the two it is. It spares the result: accounts, stakes, board seats. Whether the ability comes along is a private matter.

Where it does not come along, the product decays. Because no one any longer commands the process that could renew it.

XIII. The Final Line

Hanno Buddenbrook drew a line beneath his name in the family chronicle. His father Thomas asked in horror what it meant. Hanno replied: "I thought — I thought — there was nothing more to come..."

Hundreds of thousands of owners believe: there is nothing more to come. Not because they have failed. Not because the market has disappeared. But because no one is left who wants to take over. Because the heirs have other plans. Or because there are no heirs.

In 45 cases 11 billion euros change hands, and the state settles for 1.6 percent. Hundreds of thousands of small business owners find no successor. The one does not cause the other. But it shows what this country makes laws for: for passing on what is large. For continuing what is small it has an online exchange.

The Family Business Foundation demands less inheritance tax. The trade unions demand more. Both have reasons. And both miss the point. Because the problem is not only the rate of taxation. The problem is that a society which spares inheriting and leaves founding to fend for itself is organizing its own decline – subsidized by the tax code.

Back then, they sang: We will keep marching until everything shatters. That was a threat.

In 2026, they inherit: shares, dividends, privileges, brands without substance, companies without successors, prosperity without competence. This is not a threat. This is everyday life.

Thomas Mann had Hanno Buddenbrook die at fifteen. Of typhus. In truth, Hanno died of something else: of the refusal to accept an inheritance that consisted only of obligation and not of capability. He was the most honest Buddenbrook — because he drew the line that the others would not draw.

The small business owners are drawing that line now. Not from honesty. From necessity. In the 45 cases nobody draws a line. There, something always still comes. For the Quandts, a good three million euros a day.

We will keep inheriting until everything shatters.

The marchers of that time took it for strength. The heirs of today take it for security.
