What Comes After the Fat Years?

Hans-Werner Sinn on pensions, electricity, debt, China and AI: where he is right and what he leaves out

Hans-Werner Sinn has an answer for almost everything: pensions, electricity, debt, China, AI. One question he does not ask: who will invent what we are supposed to live on in the future?

I. The fat years

On 26 November 2026 Hans-Werner Sinn gave a long interview to the F.A.Z. The headline is his own sentence: "The fat years are over." It is the third time this year that we have discussed him. We dealt with his speech in Giessen in "Heart Disease. And Nobody Operates.", and with his podcast appearance in "The Second Finding". We will not repeat what is there. New in this interview are pensions, electricity, the debt for rearmament, China and artificial intelligence.

Sinn deserves a close reading. He says clearly what he thinks, and in many places he is hard to refute. That is exactly why what is missing stands out.

II. Where he is right

The growth of just over one percent that the government is celebrating is explained by Sinn without illusions: it comes from debt-financed arms orders and from better use of existing factories. Tanks and ammunition do not create new production capacity. That is correct, and hardly anyone says it so plainly.

On pensions, he points out how the 2007 law raising the retirement age was undermined by the "pension at 63". His proposal for families is well thought out: a state savings plan for young people, from which parents can withdraw part of the money for their children and in return receive a supplementary pension later. Nobody loses anything. This picks up an idea of Oswald von Nell-Breuning, who already under Adenauer wanted children to count in the pension formula. Sinn does, however, repeat the legend that Bismarck was troubled by old people without children. Why it does not hold is explained in "The Second Finding".

On electricity, he describes a real problem: wind and sun need a second fleet of power stations for the windless, dark winter weeks, and two fleets mean double fixed costs. Batteries can smooth out days, not seasons. And on the fuel price cap he admits that it works, against the textbook view, because there is no real competition among a few suppliers. An economist who admits something like that is rare.

III. The example that speaks against him

On artificial intelligence, Sinn offers an example he evidently likes to use: around 1850 more than half of Germans worked in agriculture, today just over one percent. The others did not become unemployed; their children took up new occupations.

But the example says something else as well. According to the German Farmers' Association, one farmer fed about four people around 1900, ten in 1950 and 139 today. This was made possible by machines, mineral fertiliser, crop protection and breeding. One hundredth of the workforce now feeds everyone, because each of them produces many times what his great-grandfather produced.

In his pension argument this insight does not appear. There Sinn only counts heads: too few young people, too many old, so work longer and tighten the belt. One can object that pensions are linked to wages, so that higher productivity also raises pensions and the ratio of contributors to pensioners stays as it is. For the contribution rate, that is true. But whether anyone has to tighten their belt depends on how much those who work produce. If ten workers tomorrow produce as much as twenty today, they can support more old people without becoming poorer. It is the same calculation as with the farmer. Sinn applies it to AI, but not to pensions.

IV. Whose belt?

A third of the new federal budget is financed by borrowing. Sinn considers this unavoidable for rearmament and concludes that the living standard of "the population" will fall because of the debt, since it has to be repaid.

But the population is not a single household. Every euro of public debt is someone's asset, and every euro of interest ends up with someone. According to calculations by DZ Bank, the financial assets of private households in Germany exceeded ten trillion euros for the first time in 2025. More than half of net financial assets belong to the richest ten percent. Part of the public debt is held abroad, a large part at home. For that part, "the belt" is a question of distribution: some pay the interest through taxes, others receive it. Whoever speaks of tightening belts should say whose belt is meant. Sinn does not.

V. Two figures that are wrong

Government spending today is above 50 percent of GDP; that is correct. According to the Federal Ministry of Finance it was 50.5 percent in 2025. Sinn then says that under Gerhard Schröder it had come down to 45 percent, and that this made the economy flourish. Under Schröder, from 1998 to 2005, the ratio according to the same table was between 46.9 and 48.5 percent. It fell below 46 percent only in 2006, and in 2007 it stood at 43.5 percent, that is, under Angela Merkel and in the middle of a worldwide boom. One can argue that Schröder's reforms contributed to this. But then one must speak of the reforms and not of Schröder's term in office.

The second figure concerns nuclear power. The Finnish reactor Olkiluoto 3 "works splendidly, although there were delays in construction", says Sinn, and he would simply build it again several times. The reactor was supposed to deliver electricity from 2009 and was budgeted at three billion euros when construction began in 2005. It delivered more than a decade later, and according to media reports the costs rose to around ten to twelve billion. It was built at a fixed price, so Areva and Siemens bore the extra costs. For almost a decade manufacturer and operator fought each other before an arbitration court. That the electricity is cheap for the operator is therefore also because others paid the bill. Anyone who wants to build this reactor several more times has to say who bears the risk this time. Sinn names no manufacturer that would bid on such terms again.

VI. Competition as needed

On fuel prices, Sinn recognises that markets with few suppliers do not work as in the textbook. With China this thought suddenly no longer applies. Cheap cars from China are for him competition that helps all consumers, and anyone who wants protection against it is a protectionist. That these cars come from state-planned and state-financed overcapacity does not appear in his answer. In the same breath he says that we must not become dependent on China, and that he would handle China with kid gloves. How that fits together he does not explain. We have discussed his China line in more detail in "The Freedom of the Free World".

VII. Who invents the new occupations

The most important thing about this interview is, once again, not in it. Inventions appear in Sinn's answers exactly once: as a possible side effect of rearmament, as the internet once was. "Trade is the source of prosperity," he says elsewhere, because it makes specialisation possible. That is Adam Smith's old insight, and it is true. But one can only specialise in something that already exists, and only what has first been produced can be traded. It is produced with machines, and the machines that build all other machines are machine tools. The industrial revolution only got going when John Wilkinson, from 1775 onwards, could bore the cylinders for James Watt's steam engines accurately enough.

The weavers' children found new occupations, says Sinn. But that did not happen by itself. Someone had to invent the railway, dye chemistry, the electric motor and artificial fertiliser, and someone had to build the factories in which the weavers' children could work. In Sinn's account this process looks like a law of nature that takes care of itself. An economist who thinks that way does not have to ask under what conditions a country invents and under what conditions it does not.

His advice to young people can be endorsed: crafts, care work and the dual vocational training system, which he sees as Germany's great tradition. But the dual system is not only about what is commonly understood as a trade, the plumber, the electrician or the roofer. It is just as much about the manual skills on which industry rests: machinists, toolmakers, electronics technicians, mechatronics technicians. That is where people learn what an invention needs to get from a single piece into series production: building a prototype, setting up a machine, finding a fault nobody foresaw. Robert Bosch started out as a precision mechanic, Gottlieb Daimler as a gunsmith, Artur Fischer, the inventor of the wall plug, as a fitter. A country that trains people only for maintenance and repair can preserve what it has. It cannot invent, nor manufacture inventions in large numbers.

What is also remarkable is who is missing from his advice. Not a word about engineers, inventors and designers, the occupations from which the productivity comes that he ought to be counting on for pensions. Erich Häusser, the former president of the German Patent Office, called the way they are treated a "cartel of ignorance" as early as the 1990s. We have written about this in "The Cartel of Ignorance and the Toxic Silence" and in "The Vanishing Link".

Sinn sees precisely that too little is invested. He does not ask who invents the things that could be invested in. That is less a personal weakness than a habit of his discipline. The classical growth models know capital, labour and a factor called "technical progress" that comes in from outside. Since Paul Romer there have been models in which research produces this progress in the first place, and they earned a Nobel Prize in 2018. Nothing of this appears in Sinn's answers. Whoever takes progress as given is left with only one recipe: the tighter belt.
