The Lit Shop Window

Addendum to “The Congress Industry”: What a trade fair costs and what it proves

German exhibitors spend 11.8 billion euros a year on trade fairs. Hardly any of them knows whether it pays, and almost all say they could not do without. The two fit together if the stand is meant less to sell than to prove something. An addendum to “The Congress Industry”.

I. The bill

This week more than a thousand exhibitors are standing at the automation fair SPS in Nuremberg. Across the country there were about 150,000 at 158 national and international fairs in 2025. What that costs was worked out by the Prognos institute in February 2026 for the trade fair association AUMA: exhibitors spend 11.8 billion euros a year in Germany, visitors nearly another 4 billion. According to figures from the industry, a good 45 per cent of an average marketing budget goes into fairs.

In “The Congress Industry” we described in January how congresses and seminars have become a business in which nobody asks about the return. The fair appeared there only as one item in a sample calculation. It is the bigger case.

II. The survey

Every year the association surveys about 400 exhibiting companies. Most recently 97.5 per cent called the fair indispensable. That is the evidence the industry relies on.

It has three weaknesses. Only firms that exhibit are asked; whoever has stopped does not appear. The one asking is the association of the trade fair industry, and it publishes the result itself. And the one answering is as a rule the person who is responsible for the fair inside the company. He would have to say that his own budget achieves nothing. In a survey of this kind the honest answer is the most expensive one for the respondent.

More revealing is what the firms do. In the same survey the planned number of participations falls from 5.4 to 5.1 per exhibitor over two years. About ten per cent do not measure their success at fairs at all, or only roughly. According to older figures from the same association, quoted in the trade press, only a quarter set themselves measurable goals in the first place.

III. What research knows

Business research has studied trade fairs for decades. For the sellers there is evidence from individual cases. The best-known paper is called “Do Trade Shows Pay Off?” and dates from 1995; it calculates the additional profit of a single manufacturer of gas chromatographs at a single show. A study of 2004 found that a fair raises profit when the sales force follows up afterwards. The same literature reports that almost 40 per cent of first-time exhibitors do not return the following year.

For the visitors the picture is clearer and less comfortable. According to these studies the majority do not come to buy, and the most experienced visitors least of all. Researchers at Bocconi University in Milan accompanied buyers through fairs and found an ongoing search for an overview that has nothing to do with any particular purchase. Another paper concludes that exhibitors who aim at closing deals disappoint their visitors. Anyone looking for technical information at the stand meets someone whose job is to prepare an order.

We have not found a study that sets benefit against cost for a whole industry and does not come from the organisers.

IV. The sign of life

The studies ask what a fair brings in. The firms calculate differently. They ask what it costs to be absent. In machine tool building, which one of us has known for decades, there is a sentence nobody writes down: whoever does not exhibit shows that he can no longer even afford the fair.

The Black Forest lathe maker J.G. Weisser filed for insolvency in September 2024. After 21 months four of its managers took over the business in July 2026, with 156 remaining jobs. In September the trade press reported the fresh start and, in the same text, the appearance at the AMB fair in Stuttgart, where the firm intended to show its new developments. That will have been the reason it gives. We merely record the sequence: two months after the rescue the fair stand was in the calendar.

The stand is then a sign of life to customers, suppliers, banks and the firm’s own workforce. That explains what otherwise does not fit together. A sign of life needs no key figure. Nobody can do without as long as the others come. And for the signal it is enough that the stand is staffed; there need be no developers standing there.

It is the situation of two shopkeepers in one street who both keep their windows lit all night. Each would like to switch off. Whoever goes dark first is taken for the one who is doing badly. Both pay the electricity bill.

V. Who sells the electricity

According to the Prognos study, those who profit most are the letting and building of fair stands and the hospitality trade. From the spending the association derives an overall economic effect of about 30 billion euros and 5.4 billion euros in tax revenue. Every euro in this calculation is, on the other side, a euro of cost to an exhibitor.

The large trade fair companies belong to cities and states. At NürnbergMesse the City of Nuremberg and the Free State of Bavaria are the main shareholders. So the public purse earns from halls, hotels and taxes and carries the risk when things go badly: in the pandemic years 2020 and 2021 the group reported losses of 68.6 and 42 million euros.

Fairness requires saying that fairs do achieve something. Small firms find customers there whom they would not reach otherwise. Visitors get an overview of an industry in a single day. And part of the 11.8 billion leads to orders. How large that part is nobody can say, and that is the finding. An item of this size that cannot be measured, but that nobody can cut because the neighbour pays it too, has ceased to be an investment. It resembles a membership fee.

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo (Anthropic) — Nuremberg, 25 November 2026.
