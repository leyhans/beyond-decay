Beyond Decay
DE EN
Essay · Hans Ley & Claude Dedo · 23 November 2026

What Does Söder Really Want?

On 13 November we wrote that things were heading towards Markus Söder. Ten days later it looks as if he has taken himself off the board with his best move. Both may be wrong. So we examine ourselves first.

I. What We Wrote

In March, “What Söder Could Be – and What He Is” contained the sentence that he has strategic thinking, the ability to plan several steps ahead. In November, “The Amateur Hour” contained our assessment that, in the event of a change of chancellor, things were heading towards him and he was merely waiting to be asked. The rule that two politicians of the CSU do not hold the highest offices at once we called a habit, not a law.

Both sentences presuppose that he has a goal. We did not prove that. We assumed it because his moves look so assured.

II. The Move

On 23 April Söder, Bavaria’s minister-president and leader of the CSU, told the newspapers Münchner Merkur and tz that the CDU and CSU would make a proposal for the office of Federal President after the summer: “If Ilse Aigner wants it, she has my full sympathy and support.” A few hours earlier the president of the Bavarian state parliament had published a short film introducing herself. Who put whom under pressure that day has not been established.

From then on he campaigned for half a year. On 11 October, after the CDU’s election defeats, he was asked on television whether he wanted to succeed Chancellor Friedrich Merz. His answer: the only personnel matter that interested him personally was not the chancellorship. It was Aigner. On 9 November the chancellor proposed her. On 30 January the Federal Convention will vote.

It was a victory across the board. For the first time in more than 75 years the CSU gets one of the two highest offices. The chancellor followed what the Bavarian minister-president had laid down.

III. What the Move Costs

In October the Tagesspiegel wrote that with Aigner in Bellevue Palace, Söder’s way to the chancellery would be blocked. Two of the three highest offices of state in the hands of the small sister party were considered out of the question. The Bayerische Staatszeitung spoke of a double dilemma: if Aigner goes to Berlin, that strengthens him at home and diminishes his prospects at federal level. A CDU member of parliament told the Berliner Zeitung a few days ago that the only conceivable replacement for Merz had taken himself out of the running with this appointment.

Chess has a word for this. In a selfmate, a player forces his opponent to checkmate him. Söder forced Merz to nominate the woman whose election bars his own way to the chancellery.

On the same weekend on which Berlin read it that way, he demanded immediate compensation for bracket creep in income tax and offered that Bavaria would share the cost. On 23 November the Neue Zürcher Zeitung interpreted this as an attack on the chancellor: Söder did not want to go down with Merz and was sawing at his chair. Whoever saws wants the chair. Whoever wants the chair does not send his party colleague to the palace.

IV. What the Move Yields

In Bavaria it yields a great deal. For thirty years Aigner has been regarded as his opposite number in the CSU. In 2017, in the dispute over Horst Seehofer’s succession, she was talked of as minister-president. She will soon be gone.

With her departure and through the death of Hubert Aiwanger, his deputy and head of the coalition partner Freie Wähler, four top posts fall vacant: the presidency of the state parliament, the chair of the CSU’s Upper Bavaria district, the economics ministry and the leadership of the Freie Wähler. For the first two, half a dozen names are being traded, some for both at once. The reports make no mention of a procedure. If a minister moves up, Söder fills her department anew. Many in the CSU expect a larger cabinet reshuffle before the state election of 2028.

But the situation he is thereby putting in order is worse than a year ago. In December 2025 the delegates elected him party leader with 83.6 per cent; two years earlier it had been 96.6. In the local elections in March the CSU reached 32.5 per cent, its weakest result since 1952. In the most recent poll, of 14 November, it stands at 36 per cent, the AfD at 20, the Freie Wähler at 10. In the state election of 2023 the figures were 37, just under 15 and just under 16. What the coalition partner loses does not end up with him.

V. The Ten Years

There is a second case by which one can test whether he plans. In 2018 Söder moved to limit the term of the Bavarian minister-president in the constitution to ten years. This, he said, would steady democracy again. Only the CSU voted in favour; the required majority was missing. In 2023 he declared that the opposition had, after all, rejected the limit, and that he could imagine holding the office beyond 2028. In spring 2025 he announced that he would run again.

By his own proposal, 2028 would be the end. Since the summer the small party ÖDP has been collecting signatures for a referendum with exactly this demand.

VI. Three Readings

The first: he wants to become chancellor. Then Aigner was a mistake, and one he repeated in public for six months.

The second: he has decided for Bavaria. Then it cannot be explained why he makes the chancellor look weaker week after week. A minister-president who wants to win in 2028 needs a federal government that does not fall apart.

The third: there is no goal. Each move wins the day on which it is made. Aigner brought a victory over Merz and one rival fewer. Bracket creep brings a headline as the taxpayers’ advocate. The term limit brought the reputation of a reformer in 2018 and the freedom to stay in 2023. That the moves stand in one another’s way becomes apparent only when they are laid side by side.

We consider the third reading the most likely. That is an assessment, and it has the same defect as the other two: it is compatible with every future move. Only what can be seen is proven. He abandons his own commitments as soon as they bind him. The image for this is the weathervane. It shows reliably where the wind is coming from and never where the journey is going.

Fairness requires the opposite as well. To stand by the same candidate for half a year against a silent chancellor is not a whim. And the result is a president against whom hardly anyone has so far raised an objection.

VII. What We Can Be Measured Against

In March we let ourselves be deceived by his skill and in November by the chancellor’s weakness. So this time we write down what would refute us.

If Merz resigns and the CDU and CSU break their habit, then Aigner was not a selfmate but a sacrifice, and he calculated further than all the observers. If Merz stays and Söder wins clearly in Bavaria in 2028, then it was the plan of a father of his state, and the unrest in Berlin was incidental. If neither happens, we were right and have gained nothing.

Independently of this, one finding remains that does not depend on his intentions. Who becomes president, who presides over the state parliament, who becomes a minister and whether the minister-president leaves after ten years is decided by a handful of people, and for months there has been guesswork about what goes on in one man’s head as there is about the weather. We have written about such conditions in “Robert Michels and the Current Party Landscape” and in “The Long March through the Party”. A country whose future depends on what a single man wants has a problem, even if the man wants nothing at all.

Is democracy still in its childhood, or has it, if we follow Michels, already reached old age?
