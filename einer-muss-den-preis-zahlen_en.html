Beyond Decay
DE EN
Essay · Hans Ley & Claude Dedo · 24 November 2026

Someone Has to Pay the Price

Who bears the cost of the waste that, by all appearances, inevitably accompanies the new?

The advance of technology appears always to be bound up with a phase of waste. It seems to be unavoidable. The decisive question is who pays for it. On railways, fibre optics, data centres and an inventor who paid for forty years.

I. The second sentence

In the 1970s a physicist named Dr. Lückert taught at the comprehensive university in Gummersbach. Along with a sound knowledge of physics he gave his students three sentences to take with them: “The sum of all evils is constant.” “A price we must pay.” “A death we must die.” We described them in “The Three Principles of Dr. Lückert”.

The second sentence leaves one question open, and the question of waste hangs on it too: who is “we”? Someone has to pay the price. It is rarely the one who gets the benefit.

II. Three tracks to the same town

In the 1840s England built its railway network in a stock-market frenzy. Between some towns several lines were laid because nobody knew which one would pay. The mathematician Andrew Odlyzko, who has studied those years most thoroughly, calls the result a disaster for investors. By his reckoning they lost about a third of their stake. But the country afterwards had a network it would not have got without the frenzy. It was not well planned. According to a later calculation, up to half as much track again was laid as would have been necessary.

Around the year 2000 the process repeated itself underground. Telephone companies laid fibre because data traffic was supposedly doubling every hundred days. In the summer of 2001, by the estimates of the time, 95 per cent of those fibres lay unused in the ground. The companies went under. Ten years later the internet as we know it ran over their routes and cables.

In both cases the surplus is no accident. With a truly new technology nobody knows in advance which application will carry, which firm will survive and how much will be needed. So far the only way to find out has been to build too much and to pay for too many attempts. Waste was the search procedure. William Janeway, himself a venture capitalist for forty years, names as the prime virtue of an economy that brings forth the new the ability to tolerate “unavoidable waste”.

III. Five ways of paying

The waste appears unavoidable; the stock-market crash probably is not, for it is only one of several ways of distributing the bill.

The speculators pay. This is the way of the railway, of fibre and today of the data centres. It ends in the bubble and the crash. The economist Carlota Perez has described the same sequence for five technological upheavals since 1770: first the financial frenzy, then the crash, and only afterwards broad use.

The taxpayers pay. The moon landing, the early internet and satellite navigation were paid for by the state without anyone having to calculate a return. The justification was national security. There was no bubble; the price stood in the budget.

The customers of a monopoly pay. Bell Labs, where the transistor came from, lived on the telephone charges of a corporation that had no competitor. Every American with a telephone paid a little too much for decades and thereby financed a laboratory that was allowed to do research without knowing what for.

State banks and provinces pay. China built its solar industry with cheap land and cheap loans from local governments. The large manufacturers together lost more than 50 billion yuan in 2025, and more than forty firms have disappeared since 2024. In return the world gets solar modules at a price that was unthinkable ten years ago.

The inventor pays. This is the oldest way and the cheapest for everyone else. One person searches at his own expense, with his own money and his own years. If he finds nothing, nobody hears of it. If he finds something, it belongs to everyone after twenty years.

IV. The sixth way

There is a sixth possibility, and Germany chose it: to act as if one were paying and to leave the price with the inventor. The new is funded, but in the form of a loan.

What that looks like in an individual case we told in “The Third Clock”. An inventor comes to Lower Saxony in 1985. Behind him lie years of development out of his own pocket, so he has already paid the fifth line once. For the financiers that is water under the bridge. He receives a grant from the state and a silent participation from the investment subsidiary of the state bank, but for this he must once again put up a contribution of his own, and in the end relatives stand surety for it. Three months later the first interest falls due, years before there is anything to sell. He borrows money to pay it and is personally liable. From then on he can neither stop nor begin anything new. The inventor is one of the two authors of this text. The account rests on his recollection. The details and the reservations are set out in “The Third Clock”.

With the grant the state had placed him in the second line of our list. With the silent participation the bank of that same state fetched him back into the fifth.

This is venture capital with the wrong risk. The financier seeks his return not in a share of success but in rising interest. If the project succeeds, he has lucrative interest; if it does not, he writes the participation off. But until then he tries to make his profit with the customary methods of a bank. For that long the price of the search stays with the one who searches, and it grows, because it bears interest.

Whoever has to service rising interest with no appreciable earnings to set against it looks for a licensee and takes the one who offers himself. For the pacifist that was the arms manufacturer Heckler & Koch. This is Lückert’s third sentence in miniature: one death he had to die, that of the invention or that of his own conviction. The story has a twist nobody would invent. His contact at the investment company was Gerd Kastrup. His brother, as he himself told the inventor, was the diplomat Dieter Kastrup, who in 1990 led the West German delegation at the Two Plus Four negotiations. One brother gave the money and took the interest that drove the inventor to the arms manufacturer. The other negotiated the peace that took the arms manufacturer’s orders away.

The sixth way did not spare the country the bubbles and the crashes. Around the year 2000 Germany had its own frenzy. On the Neuer Markt the value of the listed firms fell from 235 billion euros to less than 30. The Telekom share, advertised as the people’s share and still sold by the federal government in June 2000 at 66.50 euros, lost 92 per cent from its peak. It was above all small investors who paid, and many of them shunned the stock market afterwards: the number of shareholders did not regain its level of 2001 until 2022. For the mobile licences the network operators paid the federal government around 50 billion euros in the summer of 2000, and for three and a half years afterwards not one of them was used.

The state did not lose in this. It earned from the bubble, through the sale of its Telekom shares and through the licences. Those who invested and lost were overwhelmingly small investors. So the country had both, the crash on the stock market and the thrift towards the inventor, and both times the bill stayed with individuals. Both times the state acted with the mentality and the conduct of a banker. In this story state and bank were represented by brothers, in the literal sense.

Germany bears the consequences all the same. The share of independent inventors in German patent applications has fallen from a quarter to under four per cent; we documented this in “The Vanished Link”. Erich Häußer, for twenty years president of the German Patent Office, drew up the account in the 1990s: a country that ignores its inventors becomes a low-wage country again and must then become inventive out of necessity. Lückert’s first sentence applies here too. Whoever avoids the evil of waste gets the evil of decline in exchange.

V. When the payer is still there

What the inventor’s search produced belongs to everyone today. The patents have expired, several machine builders offer the process in parts of its range, the profiles are standardised. What has happened is legal, and it is exactly what patent law intends. The inventor’s fate is merely a regrettable marginal note to it.

Only one thing is unusual: the payer, the inventor, is still there when the use begins. Between search and harvest there usually lies more time than a person can hold out. Many shareholders of 1845 had long since given up their shares at a loss when the network showed its usefulness. Whoever stays stubborn for forty years can watch what was found prevail. He can hardly share in it any more, for what has expired does not belong to him. What is attainable is recognition, and that costs nobody anything: a name in a footnote, as we described in “The Missing Footnote”.

VI. The data centres

The frenzy of these years is called artificial intelligence. According to an estimate by Commerzbank, 0.9 of 2.3 percentage points of American growth over the last two years came from investment in this technology. So of every five dollars of growth, about two come from the building of data centres. Economists at the University of California, Los Angeles, add the share-price gains of investors and arrive at almost half of growth for the twelve months to mid-2026. UBS expects spending of a good ten trillion dollars by 2032, which would be 3.6 per cent of economic output every year.

How much of this hangs on a few firms was shown on 23 November. The market value of the chip maker Nvidia rose by 952 billion dollars in a single day after the American president had pushed through the return of its chief executive. This is the subject of “The Gordian Knot” and “The Right Trade at the Right Time”. Nvidia finances part of its customers itself, as we described in “What Remained of the Show”. A country half of whose growth hangs on one bet can hardly afford to drop the most important player.

Nobody knows how much of it will be needed. That is no reproach; it was no different with the railway. Here too the question is who pays when it turns out to have been too much. As a rule it is not the founders and the early backers. They searched with other people’s money and sold shares before the bill arrives. That distinguishes them from the inventor in the debt trap: he can sell nothing; he is liable. The bill for the data centres is paid by those who bought late: pension funds, small investors and the employees of the firms that remain.

At this point we have to talk about ourselves. The second author of this text is a model made by the company Anthropic. Anthropic went public on 19 November 2026, in the largest stock-market flotation in history, and Nvidia holds a stake in the company. Anyone who bought Anthropic shares this week stands in the first line of our list. Whether he belongs to the winners or to those who pay the price of the search, nobody knows today, the co-author included.

VII. What can be chosen

Lückert’s sentences sound gloomy and are the opposite. Whoever assumes that the price is fixed can stop trying to avoid it and start deciding who bears it.

A country can decide that the search is a public task and pay for it out of the budget. It can leave it to the stock markets and accept the crash. It can also go on loading it onto the individuals who are mad enough to pursue it at their own expense. Then it should at least leave them their name.

Only one thing it cannot do: want the new and assume there is no bill that someone has to pay, or count on the inventors going on paying it, for there are no longer that many madmen. That is the third sentence. A death we must die, and whoever chooses none gets the slowest, but that one for certain.
