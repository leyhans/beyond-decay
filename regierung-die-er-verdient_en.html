When Will Spahn Be Chancellor?
— or: Does a State Get the Government It Deserves?

On 18 July 2026 Jens Spahn resigned as chairman of the CDU/CSU parliamentary group. On 22 July, Merz nominated Thorsten Frei as his successor; a cabinet reshuffle is announced. No committee of inquiry, no unblacking of the Sudhof report, no reckoning with the networks. A fall that enacts precisely what it appears to protest — Spahn's own pandemic sentence: We will have much to forgive one another. This essay is the keystone of the personnel series: not another portrait, but the synthetic resolution. Three loops, coupled, produce the personnel they must produce. And Joseph de Maistre's dictum of 1811 — every nation has the government it deserves — is rigorously resolved.

I. The 18th of July — Fulfilment, not Rupture

When Jens Spahn resigned from the office of chairman of the CDU/CSU parliamentary group on Saturday, 18 July 2026, the first public reaction was relief: at last. At last, one of the most heavily encumbered politicians of the recent Federal Republic had been caught up by an error of his own. At last, the system had worked.

We claim the opposite. The fall is not a rupture. It is the most precise fulfilment of what this series has, for half a year, described as personnel, system and media apparatus. It is also the fulfilment of a specific sentence Spahn himself coined. In the spring of 2020, in the middle of the pandemic, he said: We will have much to forgive one another. The sentence was then interpreted as humility. In February 2026 we identified it in The End Product for what it is: a pre-emptive amnesty. A licence the personnel issue to itself before the consequences of its decisions become visible.

On 18 July 2026 that sentence was operationalised. What fell was the person. What did not fall was accountability. The billion-euro loss from the mask affair remains unresolved; the blacked-out Sudhof report remains blacked-out; the Union continues to block the committee of inquiry; the transatlantic network (Bannon, Vance, Thiel, Angermayer, Kurz) exists independently of whatever office Spahn happens to hold. The stated reason for his resignation — the fatherhood via a US surrogate, a practice he himself had previously argued politically against — is the classic case of the double standard, one that hurts no one in particular. It is scandalizable without scandalising the structure. It permits the fall of a person without the reckoning with a matter.

Four days later, on 22 July, Chancellor Merz nominates the former head of the Chancellery, Thorsten Frei, as successor at the head of the parliamentary group. This automatically triggers a cabinet reshuffle, which Merz had already announced in the ZDF summer interview as an occasion to reconsider the composition of the federal government. The decision is expected on 29 July. Whoever follows Spahn will be Linnemann or Dobrindt or Krings; whoever follows Frei will look similar; the cabinet will be rebuilt on the same criteria we listed in February 2026 in Whomever God Gives an Office: state association, gender, party wing, loyalty — professional competence, if there is still room.

This is not a reckoning. It is rotation. The fall of an individual is the executive form of a rotation that reshuffles the personnel without altering the selection mechanics. And that is precisely the point at which Spahn's sentence — we will have much to forgive one another — turns from a rhetorical turn of phrase into an operative principle. One forgives one another the mask affair by no longer mentioning it; one forgives one another the side incomes by carrying them into new offices; one forgives one another the networks by continuing them. The forgiveness is not an act of mildness. It is a lubricant of the system. And whoever refuses this forgiveness — the citizen who wants to clear things up; the journalist who asks; the civil servant who demands the release of the Sudhof report — becomes a nuisance.

This is the situation in which the classical question must be asked anew: Does a state get the government it deserves? Joseph de Maistre put this question in a famous formulation in 1811, and since then it has served mostly the comfort of those who quote it. We want to put it more precisely. Not as a moral verdict on a people, but as a structural question: which mechanisms produce what they produce? And if one knows these mechanisms — are they construction or are they fate?

II. What the Series Already Contains

To put this question with this precision, one needs the groundwork to be in place. We walk briefly through it, not to repeat it — each essay stands on its own —, but to make its convergence visible. Only in the overview does one recognise that these are not six observations, but six locations of the same system.

The foundation lies with Robert Michels. In Political Parties (1911) he formulated the iron law of oligarchy: every organisation large enough to have effect develops a leadership stratum that places its own interests before those of its purpose. Three causes: the technical necessity of the division of labour, the psychological fit between leaders and led, and the autonomisation of the leadership. This is not a German peculiarity, it is not a question of the times, it is a structure. We treated it at length in the Robert Michels essay and drew the consequence in The Founding of a New Party and the Iron Law of Robert Michels: a reform of the parties by the parties is excluded — not out of malice, but because no rational actor reforms a system he lives from.

Onto this foundation The End Product (February 2026) sets the Spahn case — explicitly not as an exception, but as the final derivative of a function that has been running in the wrong direction for decades. There we added the game-theoretic sharpening of the Michelsian finding: Akerlof's Market for Lemons. A market without quality inspection — and the German party system is such a market — drives out good offers and leaves the lemons behind. Not because bad people capture the system, but because the system rewards the properties that define the lemons. Whoever has an alternative in his profession takes it. Whoever has none stays. The longer this process runs, the purer the end product.

The institutional prolongation of that diagnosis is Whomever God Gives an Office (February 2026). There we examined cabinet formation as a whole — not Spahn alone, but the selection procedure that produced Warken's Health Ministry, Schnieder's Transport Ministry, and Hubertz's Building Ministry. Five criteria in descending order of importance: state association, gender, party wing, loyalty, then professional competence. The Merz cabinet finding was precise: Whomever the party gives an office, the party also gives two state secretaries. Professional competence has been relocated a floor lower; the ministers and ministresses are the decorative front. That is the operative explanation of why the forgiveness operates so smoothly: the persons are exchangeable.

The mode of government in which such personnel can operate stands in The People Have Become a Nuisance (July 2026). Three uncoordinated events, one common denominator: the Chancellor tells his critics to fall out; the government guts the Freedom of Information Act; §188 of the Criminal Code — introduced in 2021 for the protection of local politicians — has by now reached federal ministers and a pensioner accused of a Pinocchio jibe. The inversion of the direction of accountability. The citizen becomes not enemy but nuisance. Article 20 paragraph 2 is administratively hollowed without its wording changing. No overthrow, only renaming. That is the climate in which resignations count as the concluding act and in which clarification is treated as impertinence.

The media carrier system that lets such careers rise and, if necessary, rise again, was described by the confetti-cannon trilogy. The Confetti Cannons (January 2026), The Genesis of the Confetti Cannons (February 2026), and The Titanisation of the Confetti Cannon (July 2026) have shown how a certain type of political journalism — the aesthetics of the critical without the practice of examination, celebration instead of research, opinion on an industrial scale, garnished with democratic pathos — prepares the ground on which the personnel grows and, if necessary, grows again. The 40-million-euro catamaran in front of the Frankfurt banking skyline is not the periphery of this diagnosis; it is its cathedral.

Alongside these we examined Weimer — the Minister of State for Culture as a methodological counter-check. Weimer is precisely not the apparatus product: an unaffiliated PhD historian, with a real career biography (dpa, FAZ, Welt, Focus, Cicero, Weimer Media Group). But the Transparency Council view brings up in his case not an empty CV, but the opposite: a documented interweaving of the publisher's interests with the office he now holds. This matters, because it shows that the system tolerates both types — the apparatus politician and the externally co-opted with his own portfolio. There is no personnel problem to be solved by external personnel. The error sits deeper.

Above the six pieces there now stands the typological synthesis: the seven criteria of the apparatus politician. Entry into a party between 14 and 18; unfinished or "slow-track" studies; paid work only within the apparatus; entry into parliament before 30; politics as the only life phase; a consequence-free lobbying affair; ascent despite or because of that affair. Spahn fulfils them all. Amthor fulfils them all. Ziemiak fulfils them with a weaker affair. Kurz fulfils them internationally. The criteria are not polemical, they are operational: they describe what the system selects. And what it selects it will produce.

III. The Three Loops

The six building blocks are not six diagnoses, but three interlocking loops. That is the thesis which appears in none of the earlier essays in synthetic form, because each of them illuminates a portion and touches the coupling only at the edges. Here it should stand at the centre.

The selection loop. It sits with Michels and is sharpened game-theoretically by Akerlof. The party rewards what it produces. Whoever masters intra-party navigation ascends; whoever exercises a profession in which something is produced or built or healed instead does not ascend. The selection procedure is blind to the qualification it cannot measure. It measures the capacity to be measured. At the end of this loop stand people whose only demonstrated capacity is their own career. Those people then decide who is next allowed to rise. Recruitment proceeds in the image of those who recruit. That is the feedback that closes the loop.

The resonance loop. It sits in the confetti-cannon diagnosis. The political personnel needs a carrier medium that accompanies its career without examining it — and without that carrier medium, careers such as Spahn's or Söder's would be unthinkable. The medium supplies the visibility that only makes the state-association / gender / party-wing selection into political capital in the first place. It supplies the stage for the thousand images — bratwurst, crucifix, Shrek, doner — of which Söder consists. It supplies the rehabilitation when a fall becomes necessary. It supplies the personalisation narrative that replaces the reckoning with the matter: a man falls instead of a system fails. And that narrative creates the climate in which the next man rises — as the one who will do it better. The loop closes as the medium accompanies the rise of the very person whose fall it earlier accompanied.

The accountability inversion. It sits in The People Have Become a Nuisance. In a functioning representative constitution, the government is accountable to the people. In the inversion it is the other way around: the citizen becomes an addressee group to whom the government speaks rhetorically, but he is no longer the instance to which the government has to justify itself. It justifies itself before the commentators, before the coalition partners, before its own parliamentary group leadership — before all actors who are part of the selection loop. The citizen is receiver, not oversight. He may vote on what is placed before him; he may live with the result; if he opens his mouth, he may be reminded of his place under §188. This loop closes in that the citizen has to accept the results of selection and the narrative of resonance as his condition.

The three loops are not independent. They are coupled, and their coupling is their actual force. The selection loop brings forth personnel that only become visible in the resonance loop; the resonance loop carries only personnel that do not question the accountability inversion; the accountability inversion is only possible because the personnel produced by the selection loop does not oppose it. Each loop stabilises the two others. And all three stabilise themselves.

The fall of 18 July 2026 makes the coupling visible, because it stays within all three loops. In the selection loop, the successor — Frei — is chosen from the same personnel pool, by the same criteria; the names of the alternatives (Linnemann, Dobrindt, Krings) show the breadth, which is still the breadth of a reservoir. In the resonance loop the resignation is dramatised and personalised (chancellor-aspirant falls), and the succession debate is staged as a political event, not as an occasion for a structural check. In the accountability inversion the citizen is treated as the recipient of the new personnel arrangement — here is the new one — not as the addressee of a reckoning. All three loops are intact after the 18th of July. They did not even have to catch each other. They were never at risk.

That is why the relief narrative — at last the system worked — is wrong. The system did not work. It reproduced itself. The fall was the reproductive movement, not the disturbance. And because the three loops are coupled, no single measure brings them to a halt. Whoever addresses only the selection loop (by founding a new party, as discussed in The Founding of a New Party) fails at Michels as soon as the new party settles in. Whoever addresses only the resonance loop (say, by promoting media-critical associations) fails at the selection loop, which continues to supply the personnel. Whoever addresses only the accountability inversion (say, by demanding referendums) fails at the selection loop, which prepares the proposals to be voted upon.

The coupling itself must be addressed. And that is what the concept papers of this series aim at, as we show in Section V.

IV. The de Maistre Question

Joseph de Maistre in 1811, in a letter to Count Xavier de Maistre — not in a published work — formulated the sentence: Toute nation a le gouvernement qu'elle mérite. Every nation has the government it deserves. Since then the sentence has made a remarkable career, mostly as the comfort of those who cite it: whoever quotes it has removed himself from responsibility in advance. The people has what it deserves. One belongs oneself to those who know better and mourn.

We want to hear the sentence more precisely. In the moral reading — a people deserves what it gets — it is untenable. It tips over into the perpetrator-victim confusion which always sets in when structural results are read as moral verdicts. A people that has to endure a government which cancels its accountability has not deserved that government — it has received it. The distinction is decisive, and precisely in Germany it is historically deeply engraved. The people that in 1933 did not vote the NSDAP into an absolute majority and from 1934 no longer had a vote at all had not deserved the Hitler state. It had endured it. Isaiah Berlin would say: this is the confusion that occurs when one confuses what a person would choose if he were something he is not with what he actually chooses. De Maistre's sentence in the moral reading is an example of that confusion at the level of political theory.

In the structural reading, however, the sentence is precise. A state receives what its mechanisms must produce. That is not morality, it is causality. If the selection loop of the party brings forth the apparatus politician, the apparatus politician will govern. If the resonance loop of the medium carries the career, the career will be carried. If the accountability inversion makes the citizen a nuisance, the citizen will no longer be addressed. In this sense every state deserves its government — not because the citizens were morally inferior, but because they live in a system whose mechanisms they have inherited and not consciously chosen, and because those mechanisms produce with statistical reliability the result they produce.

The question is thus resolved into two answers. No, in the moral sense — Germany does not deserve what it gets, it receives it. Yes, in the structural sense — Germany receives exactly what its three loops must produce. Both answers are simultaneously true, and their simultaneity is not a paradox but the only honest answer to the question de Maistre asked. It is also the only answer that leads neither into cynicism nor into illusion. Cynicism says: they have what they deserve — full stop. Illusion says: they only need better people — full stop. Both lead into the chorus position which we identified in Scobel and the Chorus as the actual intellectual laziness: settling into the spectator's seat while the system runs below.

The third answer is the most difficult and the only one that is any use: The loops are construction. Constructions can be rebuilt. This is not an easy answer, because it actually demands the rebuild and because the rebuild has to be carried out inside a system whose personnel are not interested in the rebuild. But it is the only answer that acknowledges the truth of the first two without getting stuck in either.

For the construction approach we need a foundation that sits deeper than the personnel question. Our series has sketched that foundation in Freedom and Prosperity for All (February 2026). The four-freedoms canon there — Berlin's negative and positive freedom, Kant's maturity, Erhard's regulatory competition, Oppenheimer's freedom from land monopoly — is not a catalogue but an equilibrium. And equilibria demand institutional carriers. A country that accepts freedom as a burden needs institutions that protect freedom from itself: against its own comfort, against silent capture by interests, against the return of paternalism through the back door of care. The three loops are exactly the form of that capture in the German case. And the concept papers are the carrier-proposal without which the four freedoms remain on paper.

V. The Answer That Already Exists

The constructive answer of this series lies in two concept papers published in 2026 on beyond-decay.org: Representatives Without Party (February 2026) and The Transparency Council (March 2026). Both are explicitly formulated as working papers, open to legal review, technical refinement, critical objection. But they are concrete enough to show what is meant when we speak of rebuilding the loops.

The Representatives Without Party addresses the selection loop. The core idea: a voters' association — if electoral law requires it as a party, then in party form, with a charter that knows only a single purpose and tolerates no group discipline — uses the existing rules to bring unaffiliated citizens into parliaments. The candidates are not selected by a board; they are determined by citizens themselves in an open, AI-supported, transparent procedure. The foundation, in our proposal a purpose-bound trust fund under the roof of the GLS-Treuhand, collects funds via crowdfunding and provides the infrastructure. The AI is tool, not judge: it produces transparency profiles, checks consistency, flags conflicts of interest, bundles citizen questions. It evaluates nothing; it provides facts. The citizen decides.

That is the opening of the selection loop. It is not abolished — selection has to occur wherever personnel is needed for offices —, but it is supplemented by a channel that breaks the intra-party recursion. The channel is narrow at the beginning; it will not immediately send parliamentary groups into the Bundestag; it will begin at the municipal level, where voters' associations can stand without party status. But its mere presence changes the statics: the intra-party recursion is no longer the only path. With that, Michels' law is not overcome — it continues to apply to the party that settles in — but it is circumvented. The iron law concerns only those who remain in the structure it describes.

The Transparency Council addresses the other two loops. It is conceived as a fifth constitutional organ, with four core tasks: mandate tracking (every vote is checked against the electoral programme and public statements), lobby transparency (every contact between mandate holders and organised interests is registered and published), legislative impact assessment (every law receives an AI-supported impact report before passage and five years afterwards), financial transparency (all political financial flows in real time). It does not sanction. It does not accuse. It makes visible. And the citizens decide what happens with visibility.

Its effect on the resonance loop is structural: if every deviation from the mandate is immediately visible, if every lobby contact is documented, if every legislative outcome is checked five years later, then the confetti-cannon narrative can no longer fill the public sphere alone. It stands next to facts it can no longer suppress. Its effect on the accountability inversion is even more direct: it reinstitutionalises the accountability relation which the government has cancelled, by providing the facts against which citizens can demand accountability. It does not create an automatism — accountability remains political — but it provides the raw material from which accountability can be extracted.

Together the two concept papers address the coupling of the three loops, not the loops individually. That is the decisive point. A single instrument — the representative alone, the council alone — would be insufficient. Only the combination reaches where the reproduction of the system happens: at the coupling. That is the counter-position to the chorus which in Scobel's video watches wise and sad, and the alignment with what we identified there as constructive action — BSI President Plattner, who takes an incident as an occasion for regulatory work rather than for the Oedipal diagnosis. In our series we do the same, temporally displaced: we have carried the diagnosis long enough. What follows is the work on the exit.

The two concept papers are unfinished, as we expressly write there. They need legal review, technical elaboration, participation of people who know more in their fields than we do. They are offerings to a conversation, not commands to a movement. But they exist. And their existence is the point at which the de Maistre question receives its third answer: no, not fate. Construction. And the construction lies at hand.

VI. What the Fall of 18 July Is Not

On 22 July 2026, four days after Spahn's resignation, Merz nominated Frei. On 29 July the Union is to decide. Shortly afterwards the cabinet reshuffle will follow. By the autumn of 2026 the reshuffle will be complete. By the spring of 2027 the names of the new personnel will have become as familiar as the old. By the election year 2029 — if nothing structural changes — the question of the next chancellor candidate of the Union will be asked. And in that question the name Spahn will be one of the possible answers. Not because Spahn were irreplaceable, but because the selection loop has not replaced him. It has paused him.

The title of this essay — When will Spahn be Chancellor? — is therefore not a prediction. It is a diagnosis in the form of a question. As long as the three loops are intact, the question is not whether but when. Nor is it restricted to Spahn. If Spahn ends up in Brussels or on a supervisory board or in a foundation, the successor will bear the name Amthor or Krings or some other name not yet in the front row. The type will remain, and the type will remain chancellor-aspiring, and the type will reach its goal if nothing stops it.

What can stop the type is not his fall and not his fatigue. Nor is it the end of a legislative period. What can stop him is the opening of the three loops at their coupling. And that opening lies ready — as concept paper, as sketch, as offering to a conversation that has not yet taken place.

The fall of 18 July is therefore not the end of a political career. It is the empirical proof of the functioning of the loops. It is also the moment at which the series can come to a close. Not because observation would be exhausted — it will continue, with every new personnel decision and every new cabinet reshuffle —, but because the point has been reached at which diagnosis must pass over into construction. Whoever has seen the loops and understands them can no longer only describe them. He has to address them.

Kant said it precisely in the essay we read in Freedom and Prosperity for All. It is not the case that most people lack understanding. What they lack is courage. And Kant has an answer to that as well: Dimidium facti, qui coepit, habet. He who has begun has done half. Begin.

This series has begun. What still remains is the other half.
