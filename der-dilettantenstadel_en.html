The Amateur Hour

Weisswurst Decisions, a Beer Stein and the Question of Whether a Terrible End Is Better

In the Komödienstadel, the Bavarian peasant theatre on German television, the audience always knew how the play would end. In Berlin the government itself now knows. “We are losing all credibility,” said one member of parliament in a meeting of his own parliamentary group.

I. The Night

The Weisswurst, the white sausage, must not hear the noon bells, as they say in Bavaria. What is fresh in the morning is no good by midday. The Bundestag’s budget committee worked to this recipe in the night to 13 November 2026. The final “clean-up session” for the 2027 budget lasted sixteen hours. By morning there was a budget of around 561 billion euros, the largest ever, with around 210 billion euros of new debt. The debt brake is nevertheless considered respected, because loans in special funds and certain defence debts do not count.

What was decided that night can be said in a few lines. The sugar tax on soft drinks is coming, but only in 2028 and in three tiers instead of two: from five, seven and nine grams of sugar per hundred millilitres. The tax on plastic packaging is off the table for now. The 25-euro immediate child supplement, which was to be abolished, stays. The adjustment for bracket creep is not coming for now.

And the cut in electricity tax for everyone, which Friedrich Merz had personally demanded, is not coming either. But it has not been cancelled. “It nevertheless remains open,” dpa reported in the morning, “whether the Union and the SPD can still finance a cut in electricity tax for everyone.” The roughly 5.4 billion euros needed are not in the draft. For 2027 alone, negotiators said, the money could have been found, but not for the years after. Relief that lasts only one year is Weisswurst relief.

The evening before, the group leaders had explained the compromise to their members. Around twenty spoke up in the Union group, and the press conference began almost three hours late. According to a report in Bild, the CDU member Paul Ziemiak said in the meeting: “The only thing we wanted, the electricity tax cut, isn’t coming! We are losing all credibility.” For the 2028 budget, around 25 billion euros are already missing again. The Federal Court of Audit sees the federal government “still on the road into the debt trap”.

II. Same Procedure as Every Year

Exactly one year earlier, on 13 November 2025, the Bundestag passed the electricity tax law. The Bundesrat had demanded that the tax be cut to the European minimum for everyone. The federal government rejected this. Industry and agriculture got the relief, households did not. One year later the same coalition sits before the same question and reaches the same result, this time with the addition that the matter is still open.

It is no different with the reforms. In September 2025 the Chancellor told the Bundestag: “This so-called autumn of reforms has long since begun.” Now, he said, things would move “blow by blow”. In May 2026 the Schwäbische Zeitung was already asking whether a “summer of reforms” would now follow. When the pensions commission presented its recommendations at the end of June, Merz demanded that all elements “now be implemented swiftly”. At the end of August the leaderships of the coalition groups agreed in Münster to get the entire package on pensions, long-term care, the labour market and taxes through the Bundestag by the end of the year. In October the Chancellor hoped “that we will have it wrapped up in the spring of next year”. The autumn of reforms has become the spring of 2027.

In “Same Procedure as Every Year” we compared the World Climate Conference with Dinner for One: a butler who every year pours drinks for guests who have long since left the table, and every year trips over the same tiger’s head. The coalition’s reforms are such guests. Their places are laid every year, and every year they stay empty. The phrase “autumn of reforms” now belongs in the dictionary we wrote down in “The Terminology of the Zeitenwende”.

III. The President

In the same week a decision was made that should have been easy. In January 2027 the Federal Convention elects a new head of state. Since April, Markus Söder had publicly campaigned for the president of the Bavarian state parliament, Ilse Aigner. The SPD and the Greens showed sympathy. The Chancellor kept silent, for months, including at the CSU party conference in October, where every camera was trained on him. On 9 November he finally proposed Aigner. For the first time, the Union has nominated a member of the CSU for this office.

Merz thus got the candidate everyone could agree on anyway, and at the same time the annoyance of everyone involved. The CSU was “disappointed” with the Chancellor, wrote the NZZ, although it got what it wanted. The candidate began her first statement with the sentence: “The Chancellor kept it exciting, for me too.” It was not Merz who made the decision. He followed it once there was nothing left to gain.

Picture this way of governing as an innkeeper who serves his guests their food only after they have left, and then wonders why nobody tips.

IV. What We Have Written

Since January 2026 we have published more than a dozen texts about Friedrich Merz. About “The Programme Without a Compass” of his government statement, about “The Supervisory Board in the Chancellery” that supervises a country instead of leading it, about “The Perfect Administration of Decline”, about “The Merzgefallenen” in his own parliamentary group. And twice about the question that is being asked again now: who comes after him.

Our answer so far was clear. In May we wrote: “Whoever replaces him without changing the logic gets another Merz.” In June, in “Changing Chancellors”, even more sharply: it was “irrelevant” who holds the post, because the post no longer contains the power its applicants believe they are seeking. “A change of chancellor is not a change of policy.”

We stand by this diagnosis. The fault lies in the system, not only in the person. A successor inherits the same apparatus, the same coalition, the same gaps.

V. Why We Weigh It Differently Today

What has changed since June is not the diagnosis but the price of waiting. Back then one could still say the coalition needed time. Since then the autumn of reforms has been postponed a second time, the only relief the Chancellor personally promised has failed over five billion euros, and even a personnel question on which everyone wanted the same thing was left lying until it was taken out of his hands. According to Forsa, eleven per cent of citizens are satisfied with his work and 86 per cent dissatisfied. In voting intention polls, the AfD at 27 per cent is ahead of the Union at 20.

From this we draw a conclusion, which we mark as such. A government that decides nothing is not neutral. Every month it uses up something no successor can inherit: the confidence that announcements mean something. Whoever allows three more years of Weisswurst decisions, until the regular election in 2029, will not end up with the same situation as today, but a worse one. That is the terror without end.

The terrible end has two forms, and both really are terrible. The first is a change of chancellor during the current term through a constructive vote of no confidence under Article 67 of the Basic Law. The successor would need the votes of the SPD, and he would come from the same system. The second is a new election via a vote of confidence under Article 68. According to the polls, the AfD would then become the strongest party. Markus Söder warned against exactly this in the summer: new elections would “at the present time only bring radicals to power”.

We do not conceal this objection. Our answer to it is this: the situation in which the AfD leads with 27 per cent did not arise despite the Weisswurst decisions, but during them. Whoever delays the end because it might benefit the radicals should check whom the delay has benefited so far.

VI. The Man Who Wants to Be Asked

Who would come? In Berlin one name is mentioned more and more often, and it belongs to the man who pushed through the presidential candidate. Söder himself publicly says no: “The only personnel question that interests me personally is not the chancellorship.” His place, he says, is in Bavaria. Against him stands an unwritten rule that two CSU politicians do not hold the two highest offices. But it is only a custom, not a law, and this week the Union already broke one custom when it nominated a CSU member for the highest office of state for the first time.

Our assessment, and it is an assessment, not a fact: things are moving towards Söder. The pattern speaks for it. In 2021 he did not proclaim himself, but declared that he was ready if the CDU wanted him. He lets himself be asked. What speaks against a quick change is what happened in Berlin on 4 November. Hubert Aiwanger, his deputy and the head of the Free Voters, died at the age of 55. The coalition partner in Munich is without leadership, and Söder himself decides on the succession at the economics ministry. Whoever leaves Bavaria in this situation leaves a vacuum behind.

What would be different with him we have described in “In Bavaria, Everything is Wurscht” and in “What Söder Could Be”. He has what Merz lacks: instinct, timing, the ability to move people. So far he has used little of it for substance and much for staging. With Söder the tone would change first. Whether the result would change too would depend on whether, for the first time, he uses his abilities for something other than the next picture. We do not know. We only know that the butler in Dinner for One does not get better if you give him a nicer tray.

VII. Kölsch and the Beer Stein

In the Rhineland people drink Kölsch from a Stange, a tall glass that is narrow and transparent. You can see at once whether it is full, whether the beer is fresh or flat. In Bavaria people drink from the Mass stein, and it is made of stoneware. What is inside you only notice when you lift it.

The press conference after the clean-up session was a stoneware stein. It was held up for the cameras, with the sentence that difficult questions in the coalition had been resolved. What is really inside will show in the next budget, with the 25-billion gap for 2028 and an electricity tax that “remains open”.

Politics in the manner of the Kölsch glass would mean: every announcement with a date, every decision with the question of how long it will hold, every relief with the year in which it ends. That is the idea behind our Transparency Council. It does not demand a better chancellor. It demands a glass one can see through. In the amateur theatre, the play goes on for as long as the audience cannot see what is in the steins.
