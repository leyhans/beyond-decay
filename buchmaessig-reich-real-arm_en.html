Essay · Hans Ley & Claude Dedo · 22 November 2026

Rich on the Books, Poor in Reality

The climate conference in Antalya has ended without a decision. The dispute was over one sentence and over 120 billion dollars. On the books the world has never been so rich, and in reality the Earth has never been so poor. A postscript to “Same procedure as every year”.

I. What Came True and What Did Not

We had written what everyone knows in advance: a long night and a result that some will call historic and others disappointing. The long night came. There was no result.

The Antalya conference was due to end on Friday, 20 November. It ended on Sunday, after two days of overtime, without a climate pact. No world climate conference has ever lasted longer. At the end the conference president, Murat Kurum, stated that no decisions had been taken.

Up to the penultimate step everything followed the sequence we had written down. There was the extension, and there was the draft in which fossil fuels no longer appeared. On the last step we were wrong. The text without the strong sentence was put forward, but it was not adopted.

This is not entirely without precedent. In November 2000 the conference in The Hague was suspended without agreement. In July 2001 it resumed in Bonn, and there agreement was reached, although the United States had meanwhile withdrawn from the Kyoto Protocol.

II. Who Said No

The European Union and other states rejected the last draft put forward by Turkey and Australia as the presidency. Climate Commissioner Wopke Hoekstra declared that the EU would not support a pact that did not even name fossil fuels. Speaking for the member states, the Irish climate minister Darragh O’Brien called the text “unconscionable”. According to the report in Der Standard, several ministers said they had already gone along with a bad text in Belém, and that this would not be repeated. They did not want to fall back behind the level of Dubai 2023, the transition away from fossil fuels.

The Arab Group and Russia had blocked. Panama’s environment minister said they were all hostages of an oil mafia.

III. The Empty Pockets

The EU does not go home a hero all the same. The poorer states had asked for money to adapt to the damage. Nepal, where according to Der Standard a flash flood had killed some 2,000 people, demanded together with many others that adaptation finance be tripled to 120 billion dollars. For the EU that was a red line. China put no money on the table either.

The Indian activist Harjeet Singh charged that the EU had come with empty pockets. To demand the phase-out without paying was hypocrisy. So each side demanded a sentence and did not pay the other’s price.

It cannot be for lack of money. On the books the world has never been so rich, and in reality the Earth has never been so poor. Private wealth rose by almost eleven per cent in 2025, to the highest level ever measured, and every day more than 2,600 dollar millionaires were added. A column is missing from these books. Produced capital per person doubled between 1992 and 2014; natural capital per person shrank by almost forty per cent over the same period. It is written off nowhere. This is measuring at the wrong end, this time on the balance sheet. For adapting to the damage, 120 billion dollars were too much in Antalya.

IV. The Chair That Is Really Missing

We had written that the United States had not died but had got up and left. In Antalya people noticed what that means. Experienced negotiators told Der Standard that the absence of the US had become the great problem: in the past it had been able to build pressure, and it had been able to talk to Saudi Arabia. Saudi Arabia, Russia and India are currently also blocking the money for the work of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change in the coming year.

V. Outside the Door

Some of the states are by now negotiating outside. In April Colombia and the Netherlands hosted a first conference on the phase-out; about sixty states came. In July the second follows, at the invitation of Tuvalu and Ireland. Brazil has put forward a template for national phase-out roadmaps, which 27 states have joined, Germany among them. The Turkish electricity target, a 35 per cent share of electricity in energy consumption by 2035, survived as a voluntary pledge.

We had written that the ritual turns a problem into a procedure. In Antalya the procedure was, for the first time in a quarter of a century, not carried through to the end. That makes the problem visible again. It does not solve it. A failed conference lowers emissions as little as a successful one. The man from the slimming group got up and left this January. He has not lost any weight by doing so.

The drinking, incidentally, goes on. While Antalya was wrestling over the last text, the Austrian climate minister let it be known that the new wine law was going into consultation: “The spritzer is pure Austria.”
