A Flash of Genius

A Remake — Or: The Story That Keeps Repeating

The Film

In 2008, a film came to theaters that hardly anyone noticed. "Flash of Genius" with Greg Kinnear in the lead role. No blockbuster, no Oscars, no memory. If you search for it today, you'll find it in the furthest corners of streaming services.

The film tells the true story of Robert Kearns. A professor from Detroit, engineer, tinkerer, family man. In the early 1960s, he had an idea: a windshield wiper that doesn't wipe continuously but in intervals. A small thing. An improvement. But one that fundamentally changed driving in light rain.

Kearns built a prototype. He went to Ford. He showed his invention. Ford was interested. Ford negotiated. Ford broke off negotiations. And then, a few years later, all Ford cars had intermittent windshield wipers — Kearns' invention, without Kearns' name, without Kearns' money.

What followed was a fight that lasted three decades.

The Fight

Robert Kearns could have given up. Most would have given up. Ford was a giant, he was a nobody. Ford had armies of lawyers, he had nothing. Ford offered him a settlement — money, lots of money, but without acknowledgment, without admission of guilt. Kearns declined.

He didn't just want money. He wanted Ford to admit: We stole your invention. He wanted justice, not hush money.

The fight cost him everything. His marriage fell apart. His children helped him with the lawsuits – and grew up with the war against Ford. His health suffered. He had breakdowns, hospital stays, years of depression. His lawyers quit, one after another. In the end, he represented himself — a professor against Ford's legal department.

In 1990, after twelve years of litigation, he won against Ford. $10.2 million. In 1995, he won against Chrysler. Another $18.7 million. He had been right. The courts confirmed: Ford and Chrysler had infringed his patents.

But what had he won?

Robert Kearns died in 2005. A broken man who had sacrificed thirty years of his life for a fight he won — yet lost everything anyway. The money came too late. The recognition came too late. Life was over.

The Pattern

The story of Robert Kearns is not unique. It is a pattern. A pattern that repeats, again and again, in all industries, all countries, all times.

An inventor has an idea. He develops it, builds prototypes, files patents. He goes to industry. Industry is interested — or pretends to be. Industry negotiates — or pretends to. Industry declines. And then, years later, the invention appears on the market — under a different name, in different packaging, without the inventor.

The inventor can sue. If he has money, time, nerves, health. If he's willing to sacrifice everything for a fight he might win — after decades. Or he can stay silent. Give up. Move on. Be forgotten.

Most stay silent. Not out of cowardice, but out of reason. The fight against a corporation is a David versus Goliath fight — except in reality, Goliath almost always wins. Not because he's right, but because he has more resources. More lawyers, more time, more money. He can wait. The inventor cannot wait.

The Methods

The corporations have perfected their methods. They are not crude — they are sophisticated. They don't simply steal. They create gray zones.

Method one: Feigning interest. Invite the inventor, let him present, ask questions, take notes. Then decline — for "strategic reasons," "not our focus," "maybe later." And then develop the idea internally, with small changes, just enough to claim originality.

Method two: Patent circumvention. Study the inventor's patent, find the gaps, engineer around them. Don't copy the invention, but the principle — in different form, with different words, with different claims. Legally barely attackable. Practically identical.

Method three: Waiting it out. If the inventor sues: delay, postpone, defer. Exhaust every deadline, use every objection, apply every procedural trick. The inventor grows older, poorer, more tired. Eventually he gives up. Or dies. Problem solved.

Method four: The settlement. When the court threatens to rule for the inventor: offer money. Lots of money, but with a gag clause. The inventor may tell no one what happened. The truth is buried. The corporation keeps its image. The next inventor knows nothing.

The system is not built for the inventor. It is built against him. Not out of malice — out of structure. The corporation has the power, the inventor has only his idea. And ideas are defenseless against power.

The Remake

The 2008 film tells a story from the 1960s. But the story is not over. It is being remade every day, in new variations, with new actors.

There's the engineer who develops a revolutionary manufacturing process. He shows it to industry. Industry waves it off — "too risky," "no demand," "not market-ready." Thirty years later, the process is still not implemented, even though it would be ten times cheaper. Industry continues manufacturing as in the 19th century.

There's the tinkerer who builds a machine no one thought possible. He presents it at trade shows, writes patents, seeks partners. The partners don't come. The patents expire. The machine gathers dust. Years later, someone else builds the same machine — without mentioning the tinkerer's name.

There's the scientist who makes a discovery. He publishes — but no one cites him. Years later, the same discovery appears under a different name, in a different journal, with a different author. The original discoverer protests. No one listens.

There are a thousand stories, and they are all the same story. The story of the individual against the system. Of the small against the large. Of the inventor against those who take his invention without asking.

The Question

What drives these inventors? Why do they fight? Why don't they give up — as would be reasonable, as everyone advises, as would make their own lives easier?

Robert Kearns was asked why he declined the settlement. Millions of dollars, and he could have had peace. His answer: "I want them to admit what they did."

It's not about money. It's about recognition. About the simple truth: This is my idea. I invented this. Without me, this would not exist.

For a corporation, an invention is an asset. An item on the balance sheet. A tool for profit maximization. For an inventor, it is more. It is a part of him. A child of his mind. Something he brought into the world that would not exist without him.

When someone takes this invention without asking, without acknowledging, without mentioning — they take more than a patent. They take the inventor's identity. They say: You are nothing. Your achievement is nothing. You do not exist.

The inventor's fight is not a fight for money. It is a fight for existence. For the right to say: I was here. I made this. I am not nothing.

The System

The system is not built for justice. It is built for efficiency — and efficiency means: The big one wins.

Patents are supposed to protect the inventor. In theory, they do. In practice, they protect whoever has enough money to enforce them. A patent is only worth as much as the resources to defend it. Whoever has no money for lawyers, for experts, for years of litigation — their patent is a piece of paper.

The patent system is a game that corporations play. They file thousands of patents, not to invent, but to block. They build patent thickets that no competitor can penetrate. They sue each other — and then settle among themselves, over the heads of small inventors.

The individual inventor stands outside this game. He has one patent, maybe two, maybe three. He has no legal department, no portfolio, no bargaining chips. He is an amateur in a game played by professionals.

And politics? Politics talks about innovation, about startups, about the spirit of invention. But it changes nothing. It changes nothing because corporations have lobbyists and inventors don't. Because corporations give campaign donations and inventors don't. Because corporations promise jobs and inventors only promise ideas.

The End

In the film, there's a happy ending. Robert Kearns wins in court. The music swells. The credits roll.

In life, there's no happy ending. Kearns won — and still lost everything. He lost his marriage, his health, thirty years of his life. He got money, but no life back. He got justice, but no peace.

Most inventors don't even get that. They fight and lose. Or they don't fight at all — because they know they would lose. They stay silent, swallow, move on. Their inventions are used without anyone knowing their name. They die unknown.

This is not a scandal. It is normality. Business as usual. Same procedure as every year.

The remake of "Flash of Genius" runs every day. In a thousand variations, in a thousand industries, in a thousand countries. It is not a film — it is reality. And reality has no happy ending.

And Yet

And yet they exist: the inventors who don't give up. Who fight even though they know they might lose. Who keep going even though everyone tells them to stop.

You can call them crazy. Stubborn, unworldly, self-destructive. You can say: They should be reasonable, make compromises, accept the game.

Or you can call them the last upright ones. People who still believe that right is right — even if it cannot be enforced. People who do not sell their dignity — even if the price is right. People who say: No. Not with me. Not like this.

These people don't change the world. The world is stronger than they are. But they preserve something that the world tries to destroy: the idea that inventors deserve more than hush money. That ideas belong to those who had them. That justice is more than a word.

"Flash of Genius" is a 2008 film about a man who fought for thirty years to prove his invention was stolen. He won — and still lost everything.

The story is remade every day. New inventors, new inventions, the same corporations, the same methods, the same ending. The system is not built for justice. It is built for those who control it.

And yet they keep fighting — the inventors who refuse to give up. Not because they will win, but because they cannot do otherwise. Because giving up is not an option when what's at stake is one's own existence. This is not a hero's story. It is a tragedy. But sometimes tragedy is all that remains.
