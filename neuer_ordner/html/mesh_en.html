MESH

The Mayor's Wrong Question — Why Germany's Infrastructure Must Not Be Protected, but Rethought
beyond-decay.org — February 2026

I. The Longest Blackout Since 1945

On 3 January 2026, 45,000 households and 2,200 businesses in southwest Berlin lost their power. It was winter; temperatures were below zero. The cause: an arson attack on high-voltage cables. Nothing sophisticated, no high-tech, no cyberattack — a fire. It took nearly a week until all households were reconnected. It was the longest power outage in the German capital since the Second World War.

With the electricity went the mobile phone networks. The district heating. The suburban rail service. Road tunnels had to be closed because ventilation failed. Hospitals switched to emergency operation. Elderly people sat in dark, cold flats. A single attack on a few cables paralysed an entire district of a four-million city.

Berlin's Governing Mayor Kai Wegner responded by pointing out that there is no such thing as one hundred per cent security — and with a sentence that encapsulates the entire problem:

"What am I supposed to do, put a police officer at every spot?"

Wegner is right that one hundred per cent security does not exist. But his question is the wrong one. It reveals a mindset that knows only two options: total surveillance or resignation. Protect or accept. Police officer or no police officer.

The right question is not: How do we protect every cable? The right question is: Why does a single fire bring an entire city to a standstill?

II. The Architecture of Vulnerability

Germany's infrastructure is a masterpiece of the twentieth century. Large power stations feed large grids through high-voltage lines. Large data centres process large data flows through central hubs. Large waterworks supply large urban areas. Large railway stations distribute large passenger flows through central signal boxes.

This architecture was efficient as long as nobody attacked it. It was cheap as long as security was not an issue. It functioned as long as the environment remained stable.

None of these preconditions still holds.

Germany's Federal Criminal Police Office recorded 321 cases of suspected sabotage against critical infrastructure in 2025 — railway network, energy supply, military installations. In the first weeks of 2026, more followed: broken-into transformer stations in Wuppertal, intrusion attempts at wind farms in Starnberg, stolen Deutsche Bahn radio receivers in Oberhausen, metal parts fixed to the rails in Essen that derailed a freight train with tank wagons — on a route onto which, according to reports, a US military transport had shortly before been diverted.

Security agencies speak of "one-off agents" — people with no intelligence training, recruited for small sums to observe infrastructure or carry out simple acts of sabotage. You need neither specialist training nor explosives. A pair of bolt cutters, a canister of petrol, a mobile phone — that suffices to paralyse a district.

The problem is not the shortage of police officers. The problem is the architecture.

Germany's infrastructure has single points of failure everywhere. A few high-voltage lines whose failure leaves an entire region without power. A few internet exchange points — the DE-CIX in Frankfurt is the world's largest — whose failure could disrupt data traffic across an entire continent. A few central signal boxes whose sabotage shuts down rail traffic in entire federal states. A few pipelines, a few substations, a few data centres.

Each one is a target. And there are not enough police officers in the world to protect them all simultaneously. This is not a complaint — it is physics.

III. The State's Response — More of the Same

The federal government responds as governments respond: with laws, agencies, and money. The KRITIS umbrella law, first announced in 2021, finally reached cabinet in September 2025 and was passed by the Bundestag at the end of January 2026 — accelerated by the Berlin blackout.

The law obliges approximately 1,700 operators of critical infrastructure to conduct risk assessments, implement physical security measures, and report incidents. It covers sectors serving more than 500,000 people — energy, water, food, health, transport, IT, telecommunications, financial services.

A National Security Council has been established, with 13 planners and analysts. Berlin has pledged 200 million euros for burying high-voltage lines underground. Interior Minister Dobrindt has put up a one-million-euro reward for information on the Berlin arsonists. The Bundeswehr is to intercept drones over infrastructure facilities.

None of this is wrong. But it is not enough. And above all, it is not the solution but the continuation of the problem.

For the logic remains the same: protect central infrastructure centrally. More fences around the same substations. More cameras at the same hubs. More laws for the same operators. A Fraunhofer Institute expert puts it plainly: the systems are "so large, so complex, and so interdependent" that complete protection is impossible. Anyone claiming otherwise is pulling the wool over people's eyes.

The state's response treats the symptoms. MESH treats the cause.

IV. The MESH Principle — Decentralised Resilience Instead of Central Defence

MESH stands for Modular European Security through Horizontal resilience. The principle comes not from military strategy but from network theory.

The internet was originally designed to survive a nuclear attack. The principle: no central hub whose failure brings down the network. Every message finds its way via alternative routes. If one node fails, data flows around it. The network has no single point of failure — it is resilient by design.

At some point, this principle was forgotten. The physical infrastructure on which the internet runs is highly centralised. And the energy infrastructure on which everything else depends is even more so. The result: a system that was conceptually indestructible can be brought to a standstill by a fire at a single cable.

MESH applies the original principle to all critical infrastructure: energy, communication, water, transport, food, data. The fundamental rule is: no element whose failure causes more than a local and temporary disruption. No cable, no line, no node, no facility whose loss endangers the system as a whole.

This is not utopian. It is the return to a principle we already knew — and forgot.

V. The Five Pillars of MESH

First pillar: Decentralised energy. Germany already has more than two million rooftop solar installations. It has wind turbines, biogas plants, small-scale hydropower. But all of it feeds into a central grid held together by a few high-voltage transmission lines. MESH inverts the logic: not generation decentralised and distribution centralised, but both decentralised. Every neighbourhood, every municipality, every district becomes an energy cell that synchronises with the wider grid in normal operation — but functions as an island in a crisis. The technology exists: battery storage, microgrids, intelligent inverters that switch from grid to island mode in milliseconds. What is missing is not the technology but the decision to deploy it universally.

Second pillar: Meshed communication. When Berlin lost power, mobile phone masts went down too — because they are connected to the same power grid and most have no or only brief emergency backup. In a MESH architecture, devices communicate directly with each other, without a central hub. Mesh networks — this is not a metaphor but an existing technology — relay messages from device to device, like water through a capillary system rather than a single pipe. Community-owned fibre optic networks, local internet exchange points, autonomously powered communication infrastructure in every district: no single line whose severing interrupts an entire city's communication.

Third pillar: Local supply security. Modern just-in-time logistics is an efficiency miracle — and a resilience disaster. German supermarkets hold stocks for three to five days. When power fails, cold chains break. When trains stop, no resupply comes. MESH means: every municipality maintains decentralised reserves — not as hoarding bunkers but as cooperatively managed infrastructure. Water purification that functions without the external power grid. Food stores that are rotationally managed. Emergency pharmacies with basic supplies. Not a bunker mentality, but organised self-help.

Fourth pillar: Decentralised production. Globalisation has organised production into chains where a single-point interruption blocks the entire system. MESH builds regional production capacities that can cover the most critical needs in a crisis. 3D printing workshops in every district town, capable of manufacturing spare parts for infrastructure components. Cooperative micro-factories not dependent on global supply chains. Open hardware designs enabling local manufacture. The goal is not autarky — that would be absurd — but bridging capability: the ability to survive an interruption of global chains for weeks or months.

Fifth pillar: Citizen-powered resilience. The decisive pillar — and the most underestimated. During Berlin's blackout night, people sat in dark, cold flats waiting for someone to fix the problem. The fire brigade set up warming centres. The district office distributed camp beds. Citizens were spectators of their own crisis. MESH makes citizens into actors. Resilience cooperatives in every neighbourhood, costing nothing in normal operation and immediately capable of action in a crisis. Volunteer emergency teams modelled on the voluntary fire brigade — but for infrastructure rather than fires. Regular exercises, not as a military scenario but as a neighbourhood project: What do we do when the power goes out for 72 hours?

VI. The Geometry of Vulnerability

To understand why MESH works, one must understand the geometry of vulnerability.

A centralised system — a star with a few large hubs — presents a clear cost-benefit calculation for the saboteur: one attack, large effect. The more centralised the system, the more attractive the target. Berlin's saboteurs had to set fire to a few cables to paralyse 45,000 households. The ratio of effort to effect is astronomically in the attacker's favour.

A decentralised system — a mesh with many small nodes — inverts this calculation. To achieve the same effect, the saboteur would have to attack not one cable but hundreds of microgrids simultaneously. Each individual one affects only a few city blocks. And even if one is brought down, the neighbours supply themselves. The effort explodes; the effect implodes.

This is not theory. It is mathematics. In network theory, the principle is: scale-free networks (with a few large hubs) are robust against random failures but extremely vulnerable to targeted attacks on their hubs. Distributed networks (with many equally sized nodes) are virtually immune to targeted attacks — because there are no hubs worth attacking.

Germany's infrastructure is a scale-free network. MESH transforms it into a distributed network. This changes not only the defensive posture — it changes the attacker's calculus. Sabotage is not prevented. It is rendered pointless.

VII. What Switzerland and Finland Know

The idea is not new. Two European countries have been living it for decades.

Switzerland has one of the most decentralised infrastructures in Europe. Over 600 municipal energy providers, many organised as cooperatives. Every valley, every municipality has a degree of energy autonomy. The result: Switzerland has one of the highest supply security ratings in the world — not because it spends more money, but because its architecture is different.

Added to this is the civil defence infrastructure: shelters for the entire population, decentralised reserves, emergency communication systems independent of the central grid. Every canton, every municipality has its own civil defence plan, its own resources, its own capacity for action. It is the subsidiarity principle applied to disaster preparedness.

Finland — the country we already encountered as a model in RIEGEL — has, after decades on the Soviet border, developed a culture of resilience that extends far beyond the military. Finnish households are urged to keep supplies for 72 hours — and many do, as a cultural matter of course. Municipalities maintain independent emergency supply systems. The population regularly trains for crisis scenarios. Not out of fear, but out of reason.

Germany has none of this. Instead, it has a KRITIS law that came years after it was announced and whose protection obligations take effect only after registration, ordinances and further deadlines. Years after Russia began its sabotage campaign.

VIII. Who Builds MESH?

Here lies the decisive difference between MESH and the conventional approach. The KRITIS law asks: Which operators must the state compel to protect their infrastructure? MESH asks: Who can own, operate, and defend decentralised infrastructure?

The answer is not the state. And not the large corporations. The answer is cooperatives.

The cooperative is the natural organisational form of decentralised infrastructure. It belongs to those who use it. It is operated by those with an immediate interest in its functioning. It is local, manageable, democratically controlled. And in Germany it has a tradition older than the federal state itself: the energy cooperatives, the housing cooperatives, the agricultural cooperatives — they are the foundation of an infrastructure that belongs neither to the state nor to the corporations.

MESH means concretely: energy cooperatives operating a microgrid in every neighbourhood — solar panels on rooftops, a shared battery storage in the basement, an intelligent network that functions autonomously in an emergency. Communication cooperatives running their own fibre optic and mesh radio networks — independent of Telekom and Vodafone. Supply cooperatives managing decentralised water purification and food stores. Production cooperatives providing 3D printing workshops and repair infrastructure.

Does this sound like a utopia from the nineteenth century? It is the reality of the twenty-first century in every country that takes its vulnerability seriously.

IX. The Berlin Test

Imagine the Berlin district of Steglitz-Zehlendorf had been organised according to the MESH principle when the cables caught fire in January 2026.

The high-voltage line fails. But in every neighbourhood, microgrids switch to island mode within milliseconds. Rooftop solar panels and battery storage cover the base load — lights, heating pumps, refrigerators, communication. Not full comfort, but the essentials.

The mobile network goes down. But the local mesh network continues to function because it runs on its own battery-backed power supply. Neighbours can coordinate, exchange information, organise help.

The district heating fails. But the resilience cooperative has an independently heated community room in every other street — gas or pellet-powered — serving as a warming centre. Not the fire brigade setting it up, but the neighbourhood, which has trained for precisely this.

The supermarket has no power. But the cooperative store in the neighbourhood has its own generator and supplies for ten days. Distribution is organised by the members themselves.

What happens in this scenario? Not "nothing" — an attack remains an attack, and life is restricted. But instead of a catastrophe in which 45,000 households sit in the dark for a week waiting for the state, there is a disruption that the neighbourhood manages while the state organises the repair of the main line.

The difference is not comfort. The difference is: the attack has missed its target. It intended to create fear — and instead activated community. It intended to paralyse a system — and instead demonstrated that system's resilience.

And that is the real deterrence. Not the million-euro reward for catching the perpetrators. Not the announcement of new laws. But the saboteur's realisation that his attack did not work.

X. MESH and the Principle of Civilisational Deterrence

MESH follows the same logic as NUET and RIEGEL — it is the third concept in the civilisational deterrence series.

NUET neutralises nuclear blackmail — through exclusion of use, not of possession. RIEGEL neutralises the conventional threat to the Suwałki Gap — through automatic reciprocity: closing the gap closes yourself in. MESH neutralises the hybrid threat of infrastructure sabotage — through decentralised architecture: destroying one node destroys only one node.

The connecting principle is always the same: not preventing the attack — that is impossible — but neutralising its effect. Not changing the adversary's intention, but the structure of the situation. Not arming up, but rebuilding.

Berlin's mayor asked: "Should I put a police officer at every spot?" Friedrich Merz spoke in Munich of five per cent of GDP and several hundred billion euros for defence. Both pose the wrong question — the question of the twentieth century. How do we protect what we have?

The question of the twenty-first century is: How do we build it so that it needs no protection?

XI. What Must Be Done

First: A national microgrid programme enabling every municipality in Germany to independently secure its basic power supply for at least 72 hours without the transmission grid. Not as a grant programme with three-year application periods, but as an infrastructure obligation — financed through a levy that costs a fraction of what a single major blackout causes in economic damage.

Second: Legal and fiscal equality for infrastructure cooperatives with municipal utilities. Anyone who wishes to supply their neighbourhood with electricity, communication, and basic provisions must not be prevented from doing so by regulation and bureaucracy. The energy transition has decentralised generation — now supply security must be decentralised.

Third: Municipal mesh networks as mandatory infrastructure — independent of commercial mobile networks, battery-backed, in citizens' hands. The technology exists, the costs are modest, the benefit in a crisis is existential.

Fourth: A mandatory resilience programme for every municipality, analogous to the fire brigade obligation: decentralised reserves, emergency communication, warming centres, trained volunteers. Not as an appendix to disaster management, but as an independent structure with its own budget and its own responsibility.

Fifth: The systematic elimination of single points of failure in all critical infrastructure sectors — not by protecting existing hubs, but by building parallel, decentralised structures that assume their function when a hub fails. Redundancy not as a luxury, but as a security architecture.

321 acts of sabotage in one year. The longest blackout since 1945. And the state's answer is a law whose protection obligations take effect only after registration and further deadlines.

MESH is the citizens' answer. Not because the state fails — although it does — but because the problem cannot in principle be solved by the state. You cannot guard every cable, every line, every transformer. But you can build a society that does not care if a single cable burns — because it has a thousand others.

The internet was once built that way. It is time to apply the same principle to everything else.

Destroying one node destroys only one node.

MESH — Modular European Security through Horizontal resilience — is the third concept in the civilisational deterrence series after NUET (Nuclear Use Exclusion Treaty) and RIEGEL (Reciprocal Immediate Geostrategic Enclosure and Lockdown). All three concepts follow the principle of architecture over threat: destructive action is not retaliated against, but rendered ineffective through the structure of the situation.
