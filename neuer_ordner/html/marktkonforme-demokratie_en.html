Market-Conforming Democracy

On a sentence that reveals everything

I. The Sentence

On September 1, 2011, Angela Merkel said a sentence in an interview that should have gone down in history. Most who heard it forgot it immediately. The few who remembered it didn't understand what they had heard.

The sentence was:

"We live in a democracy and are happy about it. It is a parliamentary democracy. Therefore, the right to pass the budget is a core right of parliament. In this respect, we will find ways to shape parliamentary participation so that it is nevertheless also market-conforming."

— Angela Merkel, September 1, 2011

You have to read the sentence slowly. Very slowly. Because it contains a monstrosity that almost disappears in the bureaucratic language.

Parliamentary participation should be shaped to be market-conforming.

Not the market should conform to democracy. Democracy should conform to the market.

II. The Inversion

To understand what this sentence means, you must understand what it inverts.

Democracy — according to classical theory — is the highest form of political legitimacy. The people are sovereign. What the people want, happens. The market is an instrument, a tool, a method for distributing goods. It serves the people, not the other way around.

This hierarchy was never uncontested, but it was the official position. The economy exists for people, not people for the economy. The state sets the rules, the market plays by them.

Merkel's sentence inverts this hierarchy. Not casually, not by accident, but as a programmatic statement. Democracy must adapt to the market. The market is the standard, democracy is what must adapt.

This is not interpretation. This is the wording.

III. The Context

The sentence was spoken during the Euro crisis. Greece was facing bankruptcy. The "Troika" — EU Commission, European Central Bank, International Monetary Fund — was dictating the terms for bailout loans to Athens: pension cuts, wage reductions, privatizations, layoffs.

The Greek parliament could accept or reject these conditions. Theoretically. Practically, rejection meant: state bankruptcy, chaos, misery. So parliament accepted. Again and again. Against the declared will of the population.

In 2015, Greeks voted in a referendum against the austerity policy. 61 percent said no. The Tsipras government accepted the creditors' conditions anyway. What choice did it have?

This was market-conforming democracy in action. The people were allowed to vote. The vote just wasn't allowed to change anything.

IV. The Logic

How do you justify such an inversion? The logic goes something like this:

First: Markets are global, democracies are national. A country can decide whatever it wants — but if markets lose confidence, interest rates rise, investors flee, the economy collapses. Democracy cannot govern against the markets.

Second: Markets are fast, democracies are slow. By the time a parliament has debated, voted, decided, capital flows have long since moved elsewhere. Democracy cannot keep pace with markets.

Third: Markets are rational, democracies are emotional. Markets assess risks, democracies promise benefits. Markets are the reality principle, democracies are the wish principle. Someone has to represent reason.

This logic is not wrong. It's just incomplete. Because it conceals who decides what is "market-conforming." It conceals whose interests markets represent. It conceals that "the market" is not a neutral mechanism but an aggregate of decisions made by concrete people — fund managers, bankers, investors.

Market-conforming democracy means: Democracy must follow the preferences of these people. Not the preferences of voters.

V. Depoliticization

Market-conforming democracy has a peculiar effect: It depoliticizes politics.

If the important decisions — about budgets, about taxes, about social benefits, about investments — must be "market-conforming," then there isn't much left to decide. The parameters are set. The room for maneuver is narrow. Parties differ only in nuances.

This explains the boredom of German politics during the Merkel years. The big questions were no longer subject to democratic debate. They were "without alternative" — another of Merkel's favorite words. Democracy became administration of constraint.

It also explains the rise of populists. If established parties all have to say the same thing because anything else would not be "market-conforming," then people vote for parties that don't care about market conformity. The AfD, Trump, Le Pen, Orbán — they all profit from frustration with a democracy that is no longer allowed to decide anything.

The irony: Market-conforming democracy was supposed to ensure stability. It created instability.

VI. The Invisible Constitution

Every country has two constitutions. A written one and an unwritten one.

The written constitution stands in the Basic Law. It says: All state authority emanates from the people. The people exercise it through elections and votes. The deputies are representatives of the whole people, not bound by orders and instructions, and responsible only to their conscience.

The unwritten constitution is written nowhere. It says: The economy must not be disturbed. Investors must not be scared. Creditworthiness must not be endangered. Whoever violates these rules will be punished — not by courts, but by markets.

Merkel's sentence made the unwritten constitution visible. It spoke what was otherwise only practiced. That was its scandal — and its honesty.

Most politicians say: We do what the people want. And then they do what markets want and explain it to the people as necessity. Merkel gave up the fiction. She said what is.

VII. The Alternatives

What would be the alternative to market-conforming democracy?

The naive answer is: a democracy-conforming economy. An economy that subordinates itself to the decisions of the people. An economy that serves people, not the other way around.

The problem: This alternative was tried. It was called socialism. It ended in planned economy, scarcity, oppression. Abolishing markets proved to be no solution.

The more realistic answer would be: a democracy that knows its limits but insists on drawing those limits itself. Not "market-conforming," but also not "market-hostile." A democracy that uses the market as a tool without submitting to it.

This is harder than it sounds. It requires institutions strong enough to withstand the pressure of markets. It requires politicians willing to fight conflicts rather than avoid them. It requires citizens who understand what is at stake.

None of this is self-evident. Market-conforming democracy is the path of least resistance. Leaving it requires strength that the political system doesn't have — or doesn't want to have.

VIII. The Question

In the end, one question remains that is rarely asked:

What's the point of democracy if it's not allowed to decide anything?

If all important decisions must be "market-conforming," what's left for democracy? Symbolic politics? Culture wars? Administration of the inevitable?

Democracy loses its legitimacy when it cannot keep its promises. It promises: The people decide. But if the people are only allowed to decide what markets permit, then the promise is hollow.

Perhaps this is the reason for the crisis of democracy that everyone talks about. Not the populists are the problem. Not social media. Not filter bubbles. The problem is that democracy has ceased to be what it claims to be: rule by the people.

It has become rule by markets, mediated through democratic procedures. The people vote, but markets govern.

Merkel spoke it. Most didn't hear it. Perhaps they didn't want to hear it.

Market-conforming democracy is no longer democracy.
It's a simulation.
The people may vote, but not decide.
Decisions are made elsewhere.

Epilogue

Angela Merkel never took back the sentence. Why would she? It was true. It described reality more accurately than all Sunday speeches about democracy and popular sovereignty.

The question isn't whether Merkel's sentence was correct. It was correct. The question is whether we accept it.

If yes: Then we should stop pretending we live in a democracy. Then we should be honest and say: We live in a market economy with a democratic facade. That would at least be consistent.

If no: Then we must ask how we reclaim democracy. How we limit the power of markets without abolishing them. How we restore the primacy of politics without falling into planned economy.

These questions aren't being asked. Instead, we talk about gender language, about climate activists, about bans and regulations. Culture war instead of systemic questions. Perhaps that's intentional. Perhaps it's just inertia.

In any case: The sentence stands. Democracy should be market-conforming. And until someone enforces the opposite, it stays that way.

"Market-conforming" — the gentle word
for democracy's capitulation
to the power of money.
