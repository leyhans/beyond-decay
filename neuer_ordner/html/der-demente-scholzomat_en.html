The ‘Demented’ Scholzomat

The (Almost) Perfect Chancellor

I. The Birth of the Automaton

On March 13, 2003, an article by Jan Roß appeared in the German weekly DIE ZEIT. It coined a new word: Scholzomat.

Olaf Scholz was then Secretary General of the SPD under Gerhard Schröder. His job: communicate the party line. He did it with a perfection that caught journalists' attention — and disturbed them.

No matter what question was asked, Scholz responded with the same prepared sentences. He repeated the leadership's statements like an automaton, mechanical, emotionless, unwavering. The question was irrelevant. The answer was programmed.

"An interview is successful when he doesn't answer the questions but simply says what he planned to say."

— Mark Schieritz, Scholz biographer

What's remarkable: When Scholz was asked about the nickname years later, he said he had found the label "very fitting." No outrage. No denial. Acceptance.

The Scholzomat wasn't an accident. It was a concept.

II. The Rise

From 2003 to 2021, the Scholzomat rose inexorably. SPD Secretary General. Labor Minister. First Mayor of Hamburg. Finance Minister. Vice Chancellor. Chancellor candidate. Federal Chancellor.

Every position showed the same pattern: little emotion, much control. No outbursts, no scandals, no quotable sentences. Scholz made no mistakes — because he said nothing that could be interpreted as a mistake.

In Hamburg, he governed with an absolute majority. The media called him "King Olaf." The mocked automaton had become a presidential statesman. The nickname had transformed: from mockery to recognition.

His principle, by which he evidently lived: "Don't complain, don't explain." No complaints. No explanations. No memoirs. No justifications. Silence as strategy.

In the 2021 election campaign, this strategy reached perfection. While Armin Laschet disqualified himself laughing in a flood zone and Annalena Baerbock stumbled over an embellished resume, Scholz did — nothing. He stood there. He smiled. He repeated his sentences.

He won.

III. The Problem with Memory

In 2016, when Scholz was still First Mayor of Hamburg, representatives of Warburg Bank came to City Hall. The bank had a problem: The tax office wanted 47 million euros back — money from so-called Cum-Ex transactions, the largest tax heist in German history.

What was discussed in that meeting remains unclear to this day. Because the man who was there cannot remember.

Three times Olaf Scholz was summoned before the Parliamentary Investigation Committee of the Hamburg Parliament. Three times he said essentially the same thing:

"I cannot remember."

The meeting? No memory. The content of the conversation? No memory. The promises he may have made? No memory.

Cum-Ex Committee: The Record

  • 1st hearing (April 2021): No memory of what was discussed at the meetings
  • 2nd hearing (August 2022): Memory gaps in series
  • 3rd hearing (December 2024): Again no memory
  • Amount in dispute: 47 million euros (later repaid)
  • Consequences for Scholz: None

The diary of Christian Olearius, CEO of Warburg Bank, was seized by prosecutors. It documented meetings that Scholz allegedly could not remember. It described conversations whose content Scholz allegedly forgot.

But diaries are not proof. Memory gaps are not lies. Forgetting is not a crime.

IV. The Evolution

Here the genius of the system becomes apparent. The Scholzomat of 2003 and the Scholz of 2024 are not two different people. They are two developmental stages of the same concept.

2003: The automaton repeats prepared statements regardless of what question is asked. Journalists are annoyed but powerless. What can they do? He says nothing false — he just says nothing relevant.

2024: The automaton claims not to remember regardless of what question is asked. Committee members are annoyed but powerless. What can they do? He says nothing false — he just says he knows nothing.

The structure is identical. Only the content has changed. Instead of "That is the party's position" now "I cannot remember." Both serve the same function: neutralize the question without answering it.

An automaton without memory cannot lie — because it knows nothing.

And that's the point where "dementia" stops being a weakness and becomes a strength. A politician who remembers nothing cannot be held accountable for anything. He cannot be convicted of lying — because whoever says "I don't know" makes no falsifiable statement.

The Hamburg prosecutor's office determined in 2023: No initial suspicion against Scholz for perjury. Reasoning: No "concrete capacity to remember on the part of Scholz" could be derived from the transcripts.

In other words: Maybe he remembered before. Maybe he forgot later. Maybe both are true. Nobody can prove it. The automaton is watertight.

V. Why "Almost" Perfect?

If the system is so perfect — why did Scholz fail in the end?

The federal election on February 23, 2025 delivered the result: 16.4 percent for the SPD. Almost ten percentage points less than 2021. The SPD's worst result in the history of the Federal Republic. The shortest SPD chancellorship in the Federal Republic: 1,245 days.

The Cum-Ex affair didn't bring him down. It had no consequences. Not the memory gaps. They were tolerated. Not even the documents STERN magazine published five days before the election, which point to a false statement. Even that changed nothing anymore.

What brought Scholz down was something else: Boredom.

The Scholzomat was a perfect instrument for avoiding scandals. But it was useless for inspiration. In a crisis — Ukraine war, energy crisis, inflation, coalition chaos — people didn't expect mechanical statements. They expected leadership. Or at least emotion.

Scholz offered neither. He offered "Zeitenwende" speeches that led nowhere. He offered special funds that weren't spent. He offered coalition management that suffocated in conflict. And he offered sentences he had repeated so often that nobody listened anymore.

The perfect automaton didn't fail because of its perfection. It failed because perfection isn't enough when circumstances demand humanity.

VI. The System

It would be easy to hold Scholz personally responsible. But that would miss the point.

The Scholzomat is not individual failure. It's the logical product of a system that rewards flawlessness and punishes authenticity. In this system, whoever says nothing that could be interpreted as a mistake survives. Whoever expresses original thoughts risks headlines. Whoever stays silent risks nothing.

Party apparatuses filter out anyone who causes friction. Media punishes anyone who stumbles. Voters distrust anyone who commits. In the end, the Scholz type remains: the smooth, the controlled, the undamaged.

That this type fails in crisis times is not a bug. It's a feature. The system produces administrators, not leaders. Managers of the status quo, not architects of change.

Scholz didn't create the system. He just perfected it. He showed how far you can go if you follow all the rules: say nothing, know nothing, remember nothing.

And he showed where the limit lies.

VII. The Exit

On May 6, 2025, Olaf Scholz handed over the chancellorship to Friedrich Merz. No drama. No accusations. No resignation under pressure. Just a handover after a lost election.

The Cum-Ex affair? The investigation committee had submitted its final report. No consequences. The memory gaps? Forgotten. The accusations of false testimony? Expired in public consciousness the moment the prosecutors filed them away without opening an investigation.

Scholz remains a member of parliament for his constituency Potsdam. He will continue to stay silent when asked. He will continue not to remember when necessary. He will remain true to his principles: Don't complain, don't explain.

And the system will continue to produce Scholzomats. Perhaps not with this name. But with the same function.

Epilogue: What Remains

There's a quote attributed to Scholz: "Whoever orders leadership from me will get it."

The quote became a running joke. Because whoever ordered leadership got statements. Whoever ordered answers got memory gaps. Whoever ordered clarity got silence.

But perhaps the quote was never a lie. Perhaps Scholz simply defined "leadership" differently than everyone else. Leadership as control. Leadership as protection. Leadership as avoidance of mistakes — even if that means avoiding successes too.

That's the legacy of the ‘demented’ Scholzomat: He achieved nothing great. But he also did nothing unforgivable — at least nothing provable. He came, he was silent, he left.

The almost perfect chancellor. Almost — because perfection in nothing is still nothing.

The Scholzomat proved
that you can govern without memory.
He just didn't prove
that it's worth it.
