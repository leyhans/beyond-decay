The Poison Cabinet

On Economics as a Belief System and Its Unread Heretics
Hans Ley & Claude · February 2026

I. The Church

Economics has a church. It has no pope, but it has dogmas. It has no bible, but it has a canon. It has no inquisition, but it has peer review. And it has no poison cabinet — at least not a visible one. But it has a mechanism that accomplishes the same thing: making dangerous thoughts disappear so reliably that no one even notices they are missing.

The term "Giftschrank" — poison cabinet — comes from the libraries of the sixteenth century. It referred to a locked cabinet in which books deemed dangerous were kept — too dangerous to destroy, but also too dangerous to allow people to read. The poison cabinet was the most elegant form of censorship: the book existed. It was catalogued. It was even accessible — to those who had the key. But the key was held by the librarian. And the librarian knew whom one could trust with which book.

Modern economics needs no cabinet and no key. Its poison cabinet is invisible. It consists of curricula that do not include certain thinkers. Of citation indices that do not count certain works. Of textbooks that do not ask certain questions. Of a tacit agreement about what is "serious" and what is not. No prohibition, no burning, no index — just a quiet, persistent, supremely efficient absence.

II. The Canon

Every religion needs a canon — a collection of texts deemed authoritative. The canon defines what may be thought, what questions may be asked, and what answers are admissible. What lies outside the canon does not exist. Not because it is forbidden — but because it is invisible.

The canon of modern economics is remarkably narrow. It begins with Adam Smith, leads through Ricardo and Mill to the marginalists, from there to Keynes and his critics, and ends with the great schools of the twentieth century: neoclassical economics, monetarism, new institutional economics. One can traverse this canon in a single semester. One can complete an entire degree without ever encountering a thought that questions the foundation.

For that is the function of the canon: not to transmit knowledge, but to limit it. The canon tells the student not only what to read — it tells them, above all, what they need not read. And what one need not read, one does not read. And what one has not read does not exist.

Franz Oppenheimer does not exist.

Silvio Gesell does not exist. Henry George does not exist. Thorstein Veblen exists as a curiosity, not as a challenge. The German "socialists of the chair" too, the land reformers, the cooperative theorists — they all stand in the poison cabinet. Not forbidden. Available. Catalogued. But behind an invisible door whose existence one does not know of if one has only read the canon.

III. The Priests

Whoever administers the canon holds power. Not the visible power of a politician or general, but the invisible power of those who define what is thinkable. In the medieval church, it was the theologians who decided how certain passages of scripture were to be interpreted. In modern economics, it is the professors, the editors of the leading journals, the reviewers, the appointment committees.

They are not bad people. This must be stated explicitly, because it is important for understanding the mechanism. The priests of economics believe in their canon, just as the priests of the church believe in their bible. They have spent their lives studying, teaching, refining the canon. They have built their careers on it, their reputations, their identities. The canon is not their tool — it is their world.

That is why it is not malice when they lock a thinker like Oppenheimer in the poison cabinet. It is something far stronger than malice: it is the sincere conviction that he belongs there. That his questions have been answered. That his theory is "outdated." That he "has his place" — and that this place suffices.

Anyone who has experienced how long it takes to shed a belief implanted in childhood understands why an emeritus professor is not free, even though nothing binds him any longer. The outer walls fall easily. The inner walls are made of what one takes to be reality itself.

IV. The Heretics

The poison cabinet of economics is fuller than most suspect. A brief, far from complete tour:

Franz Oppenheimer (1864–1943) distinguished between the economic means — labour and voluntary exchange — and the political means — appropriation through coercion. Every state, he said, rests on the political means. Including democracy. Including the Social Market Economy that his own student Erhard created. This distinction is so sharp and so fundamental that it calls into question the entire body of economics that takes the state as a given. It has never been refuted. It has vanished.

Henry George (1839–1897) asked a single question: Why does growing poverty exist alongside growing wealth? His answer: because land — the basis of all production — is in private hands, and its appreciation flows to the owners, not to those who create the value. Progress and Poverty was the best-selling economics book of the nineteenth century. It does not appear in today's curricula.

Silvio Gesell (1862–1930) asked why money is hoarded instead of circulating, and proposed a demurrage fee — money that loses value if not spent. Keynes called him an "unjustly neglected prophet." Then both observations were neglected: Gesell's and Keynes's observation about Gesell.

Thorstein Veblen (1857–1929) showed that economic behaviour is not rational but driven by status, imitation, and conspicuous consumption. They made a pretty vocabulary item out of it — "Veblen goods" — and put the rest of his work in the poison cabinet.

Karl Polanyi (1886–1964) demonstrated in The Great Transformation that the "free market" is not a natural order but a political project that had to be imposed by state violence. The book is read in sociology seminars. In economics, it is considered outside the discipline.

What do all these thinkers have in common? Not one of them has been refuted. Not one is forgotten because he was wrong. Each one has vanished because his questions touch the foundation. And whoever touches the foundation ends up in the poison cabinet.

V. The Missing Key

The poison cabinet of the medieval library at least had a key. Anyone determined enough could find it. The poison cabinet of economics has no key, because it has no door. It functions through absence.

A student of economics in Munich, Berlin, or Cologne can complete their entire bachelor's and master's programme without once encountering Oppenheimer's distinction between economic and political means. Not because it is forbidden. Not because a professor actively suppresses it. But because it appears in no textbook, features in no lecture, is tested in no examination. It does not exist — not because someone has hidden it, but because no one misses it.

This is perfect censorship. The censorship that requires no censor. The censorship that sustains itself because the censored do not know they are being censored and the censors do not know they are censoring.

When an emeritus professor says: "In our discipline, he has his place. I read quite a bit of him in earlier days" — he is not showing that he knows Oppenheimer. He is showing that the poison cabinet works. He read him, yes. In the past. As intellectual history. As something concluded. He opened the poison cabinet, peered inside, put the book back, and closed the cabinet again. Exactly as intended.

VI. What Rots in the Poison Cabinet

Books in the poison cabinet do not rot. But the society that locked them away does.

If Oppenheimer's distinction between economic and political means is correct — and no one has ever proved the contrary — then every economic policy that presupposes the state as a neutral referee is built on sand. Then the question is not whether the state should regulate the market or not. Then the question is whether the state itself is the problem it pretends to solve.

If Henry George's analysis of land rent is correct — and property prices in Munich, Berlin, and Frankfurt confirm it daily — then the housing crisis is not market failure but the logical consequence of a system that rewards unearned income from land ownership.

If Polanyi's analysis is correct — and the financial crisis of 2008 confirmed it impressively — then the "free market" is a fiction maintained only through permanent state intervention, while simultaneously claiming that the state should stay out.

Each of these analyses sits in the poison cabinet. And each of them is confirmed by reality every day. Economics has the answers to its most pressing questions — it has simply locked them away.

VII. The Poison Cabinet and the Tower

The poison cabinet is the academic variant of the tower. In the tower sits the thinker — buried alive in a category that guarantees their harmlessness. In the poison cabinet stands their work — available but invisible, accessible but unread.

The mechanism is the same in both cases: not prohibition, but classification. Not destruction, but archiving. Not refutation, but silence. The most elegant form of intellectual violence is the one that disguises itself as preservation.

And the guardians of the poison cabinet — the professors, the editors, the reviewers — are simultaneously its prisoners. They cannot open the cabinet because they do not know it is locked. They have internalised the canon so thoroughly that the absence of the heretics does not strike them. They take the silence for peace, even though it is censorship.

One could feel pity. A professor who has spent his entire life in a building without knowing that the foundation is hollow — that is tragic. But pity ends where responsibility begins. An emeritus professor who has nothing left to lose and still only says "he has his place" — that person is no longer a victim of the system. That person is the system.

VIII. The Cabinet Opens

What is new about the present is this: the poison cabinet can no longer be locked.

As long as knowledge was bound to institutions — to universities, publishers, libraries, faculties — the poison cabinet could function. Anyone who wanted to read Oppenheimer had to know he existed. Anyone who wanted to know he existed had to know someone who could tell them. And anyone who could tell them usually belonged to the same system that administered the poison cabinet.

This closed circuit has been broken. Not by a revolution within economics — the system reliably prevents that — but by technology that respects no faculty boundaries. When an engineer from Nuremberg can ask an AI who Franz Oppenheimer was and why his ideas are relevant to today's economic policy, he no longer needs a professor to open the poison cabinet. The poison cabinet has lost its function.

And this is only the beginning. For the AI does not only open the poison cabinet of economics. It opens all poison cabinets simultaneously — those of physics, medicine, philosophy, politics. It connects thinkers who lay side by side in their respective cabinets without knowing of one another. It asks questions no specialist would ask, because they cross disciplinary lines. And it makes the answers accessible to anyone who wishes to read them.

The priests of the canon will deplore this. They will speak of declining quality, of dilettantism, of the dangers of half-knowledge. And in some cases they will be right. But they will not name the real problem: that the poison cabinet stands open and they have not the slightest idea what lies inside.

• • •

Take one of Germany's best-known economists. Ask him about Franz Oppenheimer. He will reply: "In our discipline, he has his place." This is not arrogance. This is not ignorance. This is the proof that the poison cabinet works — even with the best.
