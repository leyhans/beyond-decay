No Connection at This Number

On Responsibilities and the Disappearance of Concerns

I. The Message

There was a time when dialing an unassigned number would yield a sentence that said it all: "No connection at this number."

The number didn't exist. Or no longer existed. Or didn't exist yet. There was no one on the other end. All you could do was hang up.

This message no longer exists. But the principle lives on — in German politics, in German administration, in the German way of dealing with citizens who want something.

II. The Pattern

A citizen has a concern. Something concrete, documented, relevant. He turns to a politician — not just anyone, but one with influence, with the right portfolio, with the right connections.

The answer comes quickly. It's friendly. And it says: "Please contact your local representative."

The first referral.

The citizen follows the advice. He writes to the "responsible representative." The answer: The representative is no longer in parliament. Electoral reform. Bad luck. End of the line.

The second referral — into the void.

There would be other representatives who could be responsible. Neighboring districts. Regional structures. But no one refers to them. No one forwards anything. No one takes over.

Months later, the citizen tries again with the first politician. The same answer: "Please contact your local representative." The office didn't know this path had already failed. The system has no memory.

The circle doesn't close. It keeps turning.

III. The Logic

→
Politician A (influence, portfolio, connections): "Cannot and will not take on a mediating role"
→
Representative B (responsible district): No longer in parliament
→
Staff member C (works for Representative D): Doesn't forward, doesn't refer
→
Representative D (neighboring district, could help): "Not responsible"
→
Politician A (months later): Didn't know about the failure, refers again
→
The concern: Falls into nothing

Everyone did their duty. No one did anything wrong. No one lied. No one acted maliciously.

The concern disappeared anyway.

IV. The District Boundary

There's a citizen who lives in the south of his city. Geographically south.

But the electoral district boundary was drawn at some point so that his street belongs to the north. Politically north. Why? No one knows anymore. Maybe someone drew a pen across a map and wasn't paying attention.

The result: The citizen lives in the south but has no representative — because his south belongs to the north, which has no representative. The representative for the south isn't responsible. Even though the citizen geographically lives in his territory.

He could theoretically move. A few streets over, to the right side of the line. Then he'd have a representative. Then someone would be responsible. The concern would remain the same — but suddenly there would be a connection at this number.

As if important questions depend on where someone put their pen.

V. The System

The most absurd part: There are solutions. Some regions have them. There, neighboring representatives take care of orphaned districts. No one falls through the cracks. Other regions don't have this. Why? No one knows. No one asks. No one cares.

That is system. A system constructed so that responsibility matters more than accountability. That forwarding matters more than acting. That rules matter more than results. That no one is at fault when everything fails.

The staff are friendly. The answers come promptly. Everything is correct. And everything leads to nothing.

VI. The Bigger Picture

These cases are small. A citizen, a few emails, a few months of silence. Not worth mentioning, one might say. A hundred concerns per week. You can't take care of everything.

But these cases are fractals. They show in the small what happens in the large.

Why doesn't Germany have leading technology companies in the fields it invented? Why do pioneers of entire industries work abroad today? Why are strategically important companies sold while everyone watches?

Responsibility was passed along until it disappeared. No one was responsible.

The "valley of death" for innovations — the gap between invention and market, where so many German ideas die — is not a law of nature. It's the result of a thousand small decisions that were all correct and together amount to a catastrophe.

VII. The Black Box

Sometimes something happens. You throw something into the system — emails, inquiries, concerns — and receive silence, rejection, referrals. And then, suddenly, something happens. A company gets contracts. A sale is averted. A door opens.

Was it coincidence? Did someone intervene? Did someone make a call?

The system doesn't answer. The system is a black box. You never know why something happens — or why it doesn't.

But at least: For some concerns, there are entrances to the black box. For others, there aren't even that.

VIII. The Way Out

There are people who ignore responsibilities. Who take on accountability even though they don't have to. Who make a call, forward an email, open a door.

They are rare. But they exist.

They don't ask who's responsible. They act.

They work without a connection. They don't wait for someone to become responsible.

Maybe that's the only answer to a system that gives no answers: Stop asking. Start acting.

IX. The Documentation

This essay is not a cry for help. It's a documentation — in real time.

Someday someone will ask: Why did Germany miss these opportunities? Why are the technologies elsewhere, the patents elsewhere, the industries elsewhere?

The answer lies in sentences like "Please contact your local representative." In district boundaries and responsibilities and a hundred concerns per week that were all correctly processed — and all disappeared into nothing.

The system may have no memory. We do.

"No connection at this number."

That's not a technical fault. That's the system.
