The Last Invention

When did Germany last change the world?

The question sounds simple. The answer is alarming.

What was the last great invention from Germany? An invention that changed the world? That affects billions of people? That created or destroyed an entire industry?

Think about it. Take your time.

The answer that occurs to most people is: MP3.

The format that revolutionized the music industry. Developed at the Fraunhofer Institute for Integrated Circuits in Erlangen. Patented in 1989. Released in 1993.

That was over thirty years ago.

• • •

The Chronology of Decline

Let's look back. What came from Germany?

19th Century: The Otto engine (1876). The diesel engine (1893). The automobile (1886). X-rays (1895). Aspirin (1897). These are inventions that fundamentally changed the world. That created entire industries. That are still relevant today.

Early 20th Century: Quantum mechanics (Planck, Heisenberg, Born). The theory of relativity (Einstein, then still in Germany). Television (Nipkow, Braun). Rocket technology (unfortunately mainly for wars). Plastics, synthetic rubber, fertilizers (Haber-Bosch).

Post-war Period: Here it gets thinner. The airbag (1981). The Transrapid (never really caught on). SAP (1972, but that's software, not a product). And then — MP3.

After MP3: What came after that? What has Germany invented in the last thirty years that changed the world?

The silence is deafening.

Germany was once the land of poets and thinkers.
Today it's the land of administrators and naysayers.

The Candidates

One might object: There are candidates! What about...?

BioNTech and the mRNA vaccine? A valid point. But: mRNA technology wasn't invented in Germany. It was developed by Katalin Karikó (Hungarian, worked in the USA) and Drew Weissman (American). BioNTech applied it — brilliantly, quickly, life-savingly. But that's application, not invention. And BioNTech had to partner with Pfizer, an American corporation, to produce and distribute the vaccine. Germany alone couldn't have done it.

Industry 4.0? That's a marketing term, not an invention. Germany coined the word, not the technology behind it. The sensors come from Asia. The software comes from America. The robots come from Japan. Germany put a label on it.

The energy transition? Germany didn't invent solar panels — they come from America and are produced in China. Germany didn't invent wind power — that comes from Denmark. Germany made the energy transition a political decision but contributed little technologically. And then let its own solar industry die.

Electric cars? Tesla is American. The batteries come from Asia. German automakers are laggards, not pioneers. The ID.3 is no Model 3.

• • •

The Irony of MP3

The story of MP3 is instructive — and tragic.

The technology was developed at the Fraunhofer Institute. Karlheinz Brandenburg and his team did pioneering work in audio compression. They created a format that made music portable, that enabled the iPod, that enabled Spotify and Apple Music, that turned the entire music industry upside down.

And what did Germany do with it?

Nothing.

Apple built the iPod. Apple built iTunes. Swedes founded Spotify. The music streaming industry — worth hundreds of billions of dollars — belongs to America and Sweden. Germany got licensing fees. A few hundred million over the years. Peanuts compared to the value created.

This is the German pattern: Invent, then leave the commercialization to others. Do the basic research, then watch others harvest the fruits. Win Nobel Prizes, then lose the economy.

"In Germany, things are invented. In America, money is made."

— Old wisdom, unfortunately true

What Happened?

How could the land of Siemens, Bosch, Benz, and Diesel fall so far? How could the country that once flooded the world with innovations become a museum of its own past?

The reasons are manifold and have been discussed in detail in other essays in this collection. Here just the short version:

Bureaucracy: Anyone trying something new in Germany needs permits, expert opinions, certificates, inspections. That takes years. In China, the factory is standing before the German application is processed.

Risk aversion: German investors invest in real estate, not startups. German banks don't understand software. German corporations prefer buybacks to investments.

Education system: German universities produce civil servants, not entrepreneurs. The focus is on certificates, not skills. Failure is a disgrace, not a learning process.

Mentality: "We've always done it this way." "That can't work." "There's no standard for that." The scissors in the mind cut off every new idea before it can grow.

Demographics: An aging society is not an innovating society. Those who still have to work thirty years at 60 don't take risks anymore.

The Comparison

What have other countries invented in the last thirty years?

USA: The iPhone. The internet (as a commercial platform). Google. Facebook. Amazon. Tesla. SpaceX. CRISPR. GPT. The entire modern digital economy.

China: 5G infrastructure. The world's leading solar production. High-speed trains (better than the Transrapid, which Germany invented and then abandoned). TikTok. WeChat. DJI drones.

South Korea: OLED displays brought to mass maturity (invented at Kodak). The most advanced semiconductors. Samsung and LG dominating consumer electronics.

Taiwan: TSMC — the most important semiconductor manufacturer in the world, without which no iPhone, no PC, no server runs.

Israel: Iron Dome. Countless cybersecurity and medtech startups.

And Germany? Germany builds cars. The same cars it built a hundred years ago. Just with more electronics from others.

The Hidden Strengths

One must be fair: Germany has strengths that aren't immediately visible.

The German Mittelstand — the "hidden champions" — are world market leaders in niches. Machines that build machines. Specialty chemicals. Precision tools. Medical technology. These companies aren't sexy, but they're solid.

The Fraunhofer Institutes still do excellent research. The Max Planck Society wins Nobel Prizes. The universities are better than their reputation.

But: All of this isn't enough. It's not enough to be strong in niches when others control the platforms. It's not enough to research when others commercialize. It's not enough to supply parts when others build the systems.

The hidden champion supplying components to Tesla is still dependent on Tesla. And when Tesla decides to make the components themselves or buy elsewhere — then what?

Whoever only supplies parts is always replaceable.
Whoever defines the whole makes the rules.

The Future

What comes next? Where are the next big inventions?

Artificial Intelligence: Comes from America (OpenAI, Google, Anthropic) and increasingly from China. Germany has SAP, trying to integrate AI. That's not the same thing.

Quantum Computers: IBM, Google, Microsoft lead. China is investing massively. Germany has a few research projects.

Biotechnology: The USA dominates. BioNTech was an exception — and needed Pfizer.

Space Travel: SpaceX, Blue Origin, Rocket Lab. Germany has — ESA, together with others. And to this day no launch vehicle of its own — a start-up's first attempt failed in 2025.

Battery Technology: CATL (China), Panasonic (Japan), LG and Samsung (Korea). Germany is trying to catch up. So far without success.

In all future technologies, Germany is a laggard. Not leading. Not even in the middle. Laggard.

The Honest Answer

So: What was the last great German invention?

The honest answer is: MP3, 1989.

Since then, Germany has invented nothing that changed the world. Thirty years. An entire generation. A third of a century.

That's no reason for panic. Not every country needs to constantly invent world-changing things. You can live well by applying, improving, and marketing others' inventions.

But it's a reason for honesty. Germany is no longer the land of inventors. It's the land of engineers who optimize other people's inventions. That's honorable. But it's not the same thing.

And it's a reason for concern. Because an economy that only optimizes is an economy that gets left behind when others innovate disruptively. German automobile manufacturing is the best example: Perfect internal combustion engines — in a world going electric.

What Would Need to Happen

What would need to happen for Germany to invent again?

The answers are known. They've been discussed for decades. They've been ignored for decades.

Reduce bureaucracy. Reward risk. Destigmatize failure. Support founders, don't administer them. Mobilize capital. Recruit talent instead of driving it away. Decide faster. Act more boldly.

All of this is possible. Other countries demonstrate it. Israel with its startups. Singapore with its efficiency. The USA with its capital. China with its speed.

But to do it, Germany would first have to acknowledge that it has a problem. And that's the hardest step. Because Germany is still rich. Still comfortable. Still proud of its past.

Perhaps too proud to see the present.

"When you discover you're riding a dead horse, dismount."

— Wisdom of the Dakota Indians

Conclusion

The last great German invention was MP3. That was 1989. Since then: silence.

It doesn't have to stay that way. Germany still has talented people, good universities, a solid industrial base. It still has the potential to create great things.

But potential is not reality. Potential is only a possibility. And possibilities pass if you don't seize them.

The question is not whether Germany can invent. The question is whether Germany wants to invent. Whether it's ready to give up comfort, question the rules, accept the risk.

So far, there's little evidence for that.

But "so far" is not "forever." And perhaps — perhaps — recognizing how long the silence has lasted is the first step to ending it.
