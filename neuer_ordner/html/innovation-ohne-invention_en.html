Innovation Without Invention

The sentence that explains Germany's decline

2015. A conversation in a federal ministry. An inventor with proven technology seeks support. A senior official's response summarizes the systemic failure in a single sentence.

"We don't need inventions, we need innovations."

Read that sentence again. Slowly. Let it sink in.

This isn't just anyone speaking. This is the Federal Ministry of Education and Research. The institution that's supposed to promote Germany's technological progress. And it says: Inventions? No thanks.

The Conceptual Confusion

What's the difference? Invention is the new thing that didn't exist before. The breakthrough. The patent. The moment when someone says: "Nobody has done it this way before."

Innovation is what comes after. The optimization. The market adaptation. The scaling. Important, no question — but dependent on the invention.

Innovation without invention is like cooking without ingredients. You can have the most beautiful kitchen, the best chefs, the cleverest recipes — if there's nothing in the refrigerator, the plate stays empty.

• • •

The Logic of Low Hanging Fruits

Why does a ministry reject inventions? The answer is as simple as it is frightening: Inventions are inconvenient.

Invention

  • High risk
  • Long development
  • Expensive
  • Uncertain outcome

Innovation

  • Low risk
  • Quick results
  • Calculable
  • Presentable

Inventions require patience. They take years, sometimes decades. They cost money with no guarantee of success. They can't be squeezed into quarterly reports. They don't fit into legislative periods.

Innovations, on the other hand, are the perfect low hanging fruits. You take an existing invention — preferably someone else's — and optimize it. The fruits hang low, you don't have to stretch, and in the end you can still harvest.

The Parallelism of State and Industry

The ministry said something else too: "Funding is only possible with a company backing you."

So the inventor went to industry. And what did industry say? "Interesting. But without government funding, we can't take the risk."

Do you understand the cycle?

Ministry: "Come back with a company."
Industry: "Come back with funding."
Inventor: Goes in circles until he gives up.

— The perfect defense mechanism

This is not an oversight. This is a system. State and industry have made an arrangement. They pass responsibility to each other — and in the end, nobody bears the risk. Nobody except the inventor, who eventually gives up.

The Fat Years

Between 2000 and 2020, Germany generated export surpluses in the trillions. For twenty years, the money flowed. Where to?

Where the Surpluses Went

  • Dividends: Making shareholders happy
  • Stock buybacks: Propping up share prices
  • Bonuses: Rewarding management
  • Real estate: Concrete gold instead of technology

What could have been done: Invest in basic research. Support inventors. Build technological independence. Bridge the Valley of Death where so many good ideas die.

What was done: Harvest the fruits that others had planted. And hope the trees would bear forever.

The Zeitgeist of 2015

The sentence "We don't need inventions, we need innovations" wasn't a slip in 2015. It was an expression of the zeitgeist.

"Disruption" was the buzzword. Startups were supposed to save Germany. Not with inventions, but with business models. Not with patents, but with platforms. Not with engineers, but with business development managers.

The Chinese? Just copying. The Americans? Just doing software. Germany? Has the engineers, the know-how, the tradition. What could possibly go wrong?

• • •

The Awakening of 2026

Today, eleven years later, we know what happened:

The Chinese don't "just copy" anymore. They lead. In battery technology, in electric mobility, in solar energy, in 5G, in AI applications. They understood what Germany forgot: Without invention, no innovation.

The Americans created the world's most valuable companies with their "just software." They control the digital infrastructure on which everything else is built.

And Germany? Germany creates public service positions to monitor supply chains. Germany debates speed limits while others develop autonomous vehicles. Germany regulates while others revolutionize.

The Empty Fruit Trees

For decades, Germany harvested the low hanging fruits. Now the lower branches are empty.

Worse still: Nobody planted new trees. The inventors were turned away. Basic research was cut. The willingness to take risks was trained out of people.

Anyone who wants to harvest today has to climb. But Germany has forgotten how to climb. Germany would rather wait until someone else picks the high fruits — to then "innovate" them.

But: Who picks the high fruits if everyone just waits at the bottom?

Innovation without invention is a house without a foundation.
You can paint the facade as beautifully as you like —
eventually it collapses.

The Question

In 2015, a senior official said: "We don't need inventions."

In 2026, the question arises: Who plants new trees when nobody wants to sow anymore? Who invents the future when inventions are unwanted? Who bears the risk when everyone just wants to harvest?

The cuddle cats in the Chancellery have no answer to this. They continue to reassure each other. They praise themselves for their "innovative strength." They point to strategy papers and funding programs.

But paper doesn't invent anything. Programs don't invent anything. Only people invent. And they were sent home.

With the sentence: "We don't need inventions."
