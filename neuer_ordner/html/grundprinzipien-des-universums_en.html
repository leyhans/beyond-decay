The Fundamental Principles of the Universe

Murder and Mayhem, Eat or Be Eaten, Lies and Deceit — or the Dream of Paradise

The Inventory

Let us begin with what is.

Every living being that exists, exists because its ancestors killed and ate other living beings. Without exception. Even plants — seemingly peaceful, seemingly innocent — engage in chemical warfare against competitors, poison the soil, suffocate neighbors in shade. The Venus flytrap is merely more honest than the ivy.

The food chain is not a circle of life, as children's films suggest. It is a pyramid of death. At its base, billions die so that at its apex a few may be sated. The lion is not noble — it is a highly specialized killer. The gazelle is not graceful — it is a survival machine in flight.

And the deceit? It is everywhere. The orchid that imitates a wasp to be pollinated. The anglerfish whose luminous organ lures prey. The cuckoo that lays its eggs in foreign nests. Nature has no ethics — it only has strategies that work.

The Invention of the Parasite

The most successful life strategy on this planet is parasitism. By some estimates, almost half of all species are parasitic. They live in, on, or off other organisms, giving nothing back except suffering.

The Toxoplasma parasite manipulates the brains of mice so that they lose their fear of cats — and get eaten. The parasite needs the cat for its life cycle; the mouse is transport and victim at once. Manipulative psychopaths are not a human invention. Nature perfected them long ago.

The ichneumon wasp lays its eggs in living caterpillars. The larvae eat the host from inside, carefully sparing the vital organs so that the victim stays fresh as long as possible. Charles Darwin wrote that he could not believe a benevolent God had created the ichneumon wasp. He was right — no benevolent God created it. Evolution created it, and evolution is indifferent to benevolence.

Nature is neither cruel nor kind. It is indifferent. And that is perhaps the most disturbing thing of all.

Man — Exception or Rule?

Humans like to regard themselves as the crown of creation, as transcenders of animal nature, as moral beings standing above the fray.

History speaks a different language.

In the last five thousand years, humans have killed their own kind on an industrial scale. Wars, genocides, enslavement, torture — not as exceptions but as constants. The 20th century, the most enlightened of all centuries, produced more deaths by human hand than all previous centuries combined.

And the deceit? Human language — that unique ability that supposedly elevates us above animals — is the perfect tool for manipulation. Propaganda, advertising, ideology, fake news. We are not more honest than the orchid. We are only more sophisticated.

Humans are part of nature, not its opposite. They follow the same principles: survival, reproduction, transmission of genes. That they use more complex tools — from the club to the atomic bomb, from the marriage market to the stock exchange — changes nothing about the fundamental principle.

But Then...

And yet. And nevertheless. And despite all this.

Something in us resists this inventory. Not just because it is uncomfortable — that would be easy to overcome. But because it seems incomplete.

The mother who dies for her child. The stranger who jumps into the water to save a drowning person. The soldier who throws himself on the grenade to protect his comrades. These actions do not fit the pattern. They are biologically senseless — the genes are not passed on, the individual eliminates itself.

Evolutionary biologists have explanations: kin selection, reciprocal altruism, group selection. Perhaps these explanations are correct. But they do not explain why it feels right. Why people who act this way are considered heroes. Why we, even if we would be too cowardly to do it ourselves, know that it is good.

Nature made the rules. Humans can break them. That is either a cosmic anomaly or a hint at something the inventory does not capture.

The Dream

Every culture we know of has dreamed of paradise.

The Golden Age of the Greeks, when humans dined with the gods and death was but a gentle sleep. The Garden of Eden, where food grew on trees, and Isaiah's peaceable kingdom, where wolf and lamb lie down together. Nirvana, where suffering ends and the cycle of rebirth is broken. The communist utopia, where everyone gives according to their abilities and receives according to their needs.

These dreams are different, but they share a core: a world without the fundamental principles. Without murder and mayhem, without eating and being eaten, without lies and deceit. A world where being does not come at the cost of other beings.

Where does this dream come from?

The materialist answer is: wishful thinking. The dream of paradise is the projection of our fears into their opposite. We fear death, so we dream of immortality. We suffer from scarcity, so we dream of abundance. We experience violence, so we dream of peace. Paradise is not a place — it is a symptom.

This answer is probably correct. But it is also strangely unsatisfying.

The Capacity for Negation

Here is something peculiar: We can imagine something that does not exist. We can negate reality — not just practically (by changing it), but also mentally (by thinking its opposite).

No other animal seems capable of this. The lion does not dream of a world without hunting. The gazelle does not dream of a world without flight. They are what they are, completely and unquestioningly.

Humans are the animal that can say No. No to what is. No to the fundamental principles. No to the universe as it happens to be constituted.

Is this a defect? An evolutionary malfunction, a remnant of an oversized brain? Or is it the most important thing that ever happened — the emergence of a being that can turn against its own origins?

Two Errors

There are two errors one can make.

The first error is denial of the fundamental principles. Pretending the world is other than it is. As if humans were good at heart, as if everything would turn out well if only the right structures were created. This error leads to utopianism — and utopianism produced mountains of corpses in the 20th century. Whoever wants to force paradise on earth creates hell.

The second error is cynicism. Seeing the fundamental principles and concluding that nothing else is possible. That the dream is only illusion, that morality is only disguised self-interest, that humans are nothing but somewhat cleverer animals. This error leads to justification of the status quo — why change anything if it's all just about power anyway?

Both errors have the same origin: the desire for certainty. Either the world is good or it is bad. Either humans are angels or they are beasts. Either paradise is possible or it is illusion.

The Both-And

What remains if one wants to avoid both errors?

What remains is enduring the tension. Yes, the fundamental principles are real. Yes, the dream is also real — as a dream. The question is not which of the two is "truer." The question is what one does with both.

Humans are the being that knows it will die — and lives anyway. That knows its efforts are meaningless on a cosmic scale — and acts anyway. That knows the fundamental principles — and dreams of something else anyway.

This "anyway" is perhaps the decisive thing. Not denial of the darkness, but action in the face of the darkness. Not naive hope that everything will turn out well, but the resolve to do good even though nothing guarantees it will be worthwhile.

No Conclusion

I have no solution to offer. The universe has no solution to offer. The fundamental principles apply, and the dream persists, and both are true.

Perhaps that is the real insight: that we must live with contradictions that cannot be resolved. That the search for the one principle that explains everything is itself an error. That reality is more complex than any formula we find for it.

The ichneumon wasp does what ichneumon wasps do. The saint does what saints do. Both are part of the same world. One does not make the other untrue.

The fundamental principles of the universe are brutal: survival at the expense of others, deception as strategy, indifference as background. The dream of paradise is a protest against these principles — a protest that emerged from the universe itself, in the form of a being that can say No.

Whether this protest has meaning or is only another survival strategy, we do not know. But that it exists is a fact. And perhaps the fact is more important than the interpretation.
