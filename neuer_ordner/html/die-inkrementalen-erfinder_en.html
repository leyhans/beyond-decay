The Incremental Inventors

Why Germany Perfects the Existing and Misses the New

Two Kinds of Invention

There are two kinds of inventors. Some improve what exists. Others invent what doesn't exist.

The first are called incremental inventors. They take an existing product and make it better. Faster, more precise, more efficient, cheaper. They optimize. They refine. They perfect. Their benchmark is the existing — they want to surpass it, not replace it.

The second are called radical inventors. They invent something that didn't exist before. Something that is not an improvement but a rupture. Something that changes the rules, not just wins the game. Their benchmark is not the existing — it is the possible.

Both kinds are necessary. But they are not equal. And Germany has a problem with the second kind.

The German Strength

Germany is world champion in incremental inventing. Nowhere else is the existing so systematically perfected as here. German mechanical engineering, the German automotive industry, German chemicals — they all live from doing what they do a little better each year.

This is no small matter. The sum of many small improvements is enormous. An engine that becomes two percent more efficient each year is half again as good after twenty years. A machine that works one percent more precisely each year achieves tolerances that were unthinkable a generation ago.

Incremental innovation is the foundation of German prosperity. It made "Made in Germany" a seal of quality. It built the Mittelstand. It employed generations of engineers who were proud to find the best solution to a known problem.

The problem: Incremental innovation is no longer enough.

The Limits of the Incremental

Incremental innovation has a built-in limit: It assumes that the basic principle is right. It improves the combustion engine — but it doesn't invent the electric motor. It perfects mechanics — but it doesn't invent software. It optimizes the telephone — but it doesn't invent the smartphone.

As long as the basic principle holds, the incremental inventor is unbeatable. He knows every screw, every process, every tolerance. He knows where another percent can be gained. He is the master of his craft.

But when the basic principle changes — when someone invents something radically new — the incremental inventor is lost. All his knowledge, all his experience, all his mastery relates to something that is suddenly obsolete. He is the best coachman in the world — and the automobile has been invented.

This is the tragedy of the incremental inventor: The better he is, the more he has to lose. The deeper his knowledge, the harder the leap into the new. He is a prisoner of his own competence.

The German Weakness

Germany is poor at radical inventing. Not because there's a lack of intelligence — German engineers and scientists are as good as ever. But because the system punishes radical inventions.

Anyone who invents something radically new in Germany faces a wall. Industry says: "Interesting, but we have other priorities right now." Banks say: "Too risky, no collateral." Funding programs say: "Doesn't fit our categories." Professors say: "Not state of the art."

The system is optimized for incremental innovation. It rewards those who improve the existing. It punishes those who question the existing. It promotes the engineer who makes the engine two percent more efficient. It ignores the inventor who proposes an entirely different drive.

This is no accident. It is structure. A structure that has grown over decades. A structure that was very successful — as long as the world changed slowly.

The World Is Changing Faster

The problem: The world no longer changes slowly. It changes fast. And it changes radically.

Digitalization is not an incremental improvement. It is a paradigm shift. Software is eating the world — and apart from SAP, Germany has no world-class software industry. Artificial intelligence is not an incremental improvement. It is a paradigm shift. Machines learn — and Germany is still discussing data protection.

Electric mobility is not an incremental improvement. It is a paradigm shift. The engine becomes a commodity — and Germany has built its entire industry on engines. Renewable energies are not an incremental improvement. They are a paradigm shift. The sun shines for free — and Germany subsidized coal for decades.

In a world of paradigm shifts, incremental innovation is not enough. The best coachman doesn't help when everyone drives cars. The best typewriter manufacturer doesn't help when everyone uses computers. The best combustion engine perfectionist doesn't help when everyone drives electric.

The Tragedy of Competence

The tragic part is: The incremental inventors are not stupid. They see what's coming. They know the world is changing. But they can't help themselves.

Their entire identity is tied to the existing. Their education, their experience, their pride — everything relates to what they have perfected. Giving that up feels like self-denial. Like betrayal of their life's work.

So they rationalize. They say: "The new doesn't work." They say: "It's just hype." They say: "Our customers don't want that." They find a thousand reasons why the existing is better — and every single reason is wrong.

That is human. It is understandable. But it is deadly.

The history of business is full of companies that failed because of their own competence. Kodak invented digital photography — and ignored it. Nokia saw the smartphone — and underestimated it. German solar manufacturers mastered the technology — and lost the market. Competence without willingness to renew is a death sentence by installments.

The Radical Inventor in Germany

What happens to the radical inventor in Germany? He is ignored, ridiculed, rejected.

He comes with an idea that questions the existing. The reaction: Skepticism. "We've never done it that way." "That won't work in practice." "There's no market for that."

He seeks partners in industry. The reaction: Rejection. "Interesting, but not our core business." "Too risky for us." "Come back when you have references."

He seeks capital. The reaction: Hesitation. "Where are your revenues?" "Who are your customers?" "Do you have collateral?"

He seeks funding. The reaction: Bureaucracy. "Doesn't fit our program." "Fill out these 47 forms." "The review takes 18 months."

After years of struggle, the radical inventor gives up — or goes abroad. To America, where venture capital flows. To China, where the state invests. To Israel, where failure isn't a stigma. Anywhere that radical ideas have a chance.

Germany loses its radical inventors. Not because they emigrate — many remain physically here. But because they give up inside. Because they learn that radical ideas are not welcome here. Because they adapt — and become incremental inventors. Or nothing at all.

The Costs

What does this cost? Everything.

It costs the future. While Germany perfects its combustion engines, China builds the battery factories. While Germany discusses AI ethics, America trains the models. While Germany cultivates its mechanical engineering tradition, the world programs the software that controls machines.

It costs industry. The German automotive industry is not threatened because it builds bad cars. It builds the best cars in the world. It is threatened because the car of the future is no longer a car — it's a computer on wheels. And Germany doesn't build computers.

It costs prosperity. Incremental innovation maintains the status quo. Radical innovation creates the new. Those who only maintain the status quo while others create the new fall behind. Slowly but surely. A little less relevant each year.

Germany is not poor. It is rich. But it lives off its inheritance. Off what previous generations built. The question is not whether Germany is doing well. The question is whether Germany will still be doing well in twenty years. The answer depends on whether Germany learns to promote rather than drive away radical inventors.

What Would Need to Change

What would need to change? Everything. And none of it will change.

The culture would need to change. Failure would need to be accepted as part of the process, not as personal defeat. Risk would need to be rewarded, not punished. The new would need to be celebrated, not viewed with suspicion.

The financing would need to change. Venture capital would need to flow for ideas that have no revenue yet. Investors would need to understand that radical innovation takes years, not quarters. The state would need to invest in the future, not the past.

Industry would need to change. Corporations would need to be open to ideas from outside, not just optimization from inside. The Mittelstand would need to be willing to cannibalize its core business before others do. Associations would need to stop defending the existing and start enabling the new.

Politics would need to change. Funding would need to be fast, not bureaucratic. Regulation would need to enable innovation, not prevent it. The state would need to understand that its job is not to slow down structural change but to shape it.

None of this will happen. Because all involved profit from incremental innovation. Industry, because it protects its business. Politics, because it doesn't anger voters. Associations, because they serve their members. Banks, because they want security. Everyone has an interest in everything staying as it is — just a little better.

The Irony

The irony is: Germany was once the land of radical inventors. Gutenberg, Diesel, Röntgen, Einstein, Zuse — they all didn't improve, they invented. They broke new ground, against all resistance, against all skepticism.

At some point Germany stopped breaking new ground. It settled into the familiar. It decided that what it has is good enough — and just needs to be perfected.

That was successful for a long time. But times are changing. The world doesn't wait for Germany. It keeps inventing — with or without us.

Germany is the land of incremental inventors. It perfects the existing like no other. But it misses the new. It drives away its radical inventors — not through ill will, but through a system optimized for preservation.

That was no problem for a long time. As long as the world changed slowly, incremental innovation was enough. But the world is changing fast. Paradigm shift follows paradigm shift. And with each paradigm shift, the incremental inventor loses — no matter how good he is.

The question is not whether Germany has good engineers. It does. The question is whether Germany allows them to think radically. Whether it allows them not just to improve the existing but to replace it. The answer so far is: No. We will see the consequences in twenty years.
