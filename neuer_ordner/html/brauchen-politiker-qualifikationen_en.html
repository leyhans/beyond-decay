Do Politicians Need Qualifications?

Or should they merely mirror the sovereign?

The Question

For almost every profession, you need a qualification. The doctor needs a degree, the craftsman an apprenticeship, the pilot a license. Anyone who wants to fly a plane must prove they can do it. Anyone who wants to govern a country must prove — nothing.

This is not an oversight. This is democracy. The idea that the people govern themselves — through representatives they elect. And the people elect whomever they want. The lawyer or the bricklayer. The professor or the unemployed. The expert or the layman.

The question is: Is this a mistake? Or is it the essence of the matter?

The Argument for Qualification

Governing is complicated. It involves economics, foreign policy, law, technology, environment, social affairs — all at once, all intertwined, all with consequences. A wrong decision can cost billions, affect millions, reverberate for decades.

Those who make such decisions should understand something about them. At least the basics. At least enough to ask the right questions, choose the right advisors, recognize the right decisions.

But look at the parliaments. There sit lawyers deciding on energy policy. Teachers voting on economics. Career politicians who have never worked in a company judging industry. They decide on things they don't understand — because even the things they do understand, they don't understand deeply enough to grasp the complexity.

The result: Laws that sound good in theory and fail in practice. Funding programs that achieve the opposite. Regulations that hit the wrong targets. Not out of malice — out of ignorance.

The argument for qualification is the argument for competence. Those who decide should understand. Those who vote on nuclear power should know what a reactor is. Those who talk about digitalization should know what an algorithm is. Those who speak about innovation should know what inventors need.

The Argument Against Qualification

But there is a counterargument, and it is strong.

Democracy is not technocracy. It is not the rule of experts, but the rule of the people. And the people do not consist of experts. They consist of human beings — with different professions, backgrounds, experiences, opinions.

If only the qualified could govern — who determines what a qualification is? Who decides which degree counts, which experience suffices, which knowledge is relevant? The answer would always be: those already in power. The qualification requirement would become a barrier that keeps power with those who have it.

Besides: What is the right qualification for a politician? A lawyer understands laws but not economics. An economist understands economics but not technology. An engineer understands technology but not people. There is no education that prepares for everything a politician needs — because politics encompasses everything.

And: History shows that experts are wrong. That the qualified sometimes make the biggest mistakes — because they are so convinced of their knowledge that they stop listening. The 2008 financial crisis was not caused by laymen, but by experts. The wrong decisions of the euro crisis were not made by amateurs, but by economists, lawyers, technocrats.

The argument against qualification is the argument for representation. Parliament should mirror the people — not an elite of experts. It should reflect the diversity of society, not the homogeneity of an educated class.

The False Opposition

Perhaps the question is wrongly posed. Perhaps it is not: Qualification or representation. Perhaps it is: Which qualification for which task?

A parliament has many functions. It represents the people — for that it needs no experts, it needs people who know the people because they are part of them. It debates values — for that it needs no technicians, it needs people with convictions and the ability to articulate them. It controls the government — for that it needs people who question, doubt, probe.

But a parliament also makes laws. It decides budgets. It shapes the future. For that it needs knowledge — not in every single representative, but in the system. That's why there are committees, experts, scientific services. Parliament does not have to consist of experts — but it must be able to listen to experts.

The problem is not that representatives are not experts. The problem is when they believe they don't need any. When they despise, ignore, dismiss expertise. When they consider their opinion more important than facts, their intuition more important than analysis.

The True Qualification

Perhaps there is a qualification that politicians need after all. Not a technical one — a character one.

The ability to listen. Not just to voters, also to experts. Not just to lobbyists, also to critics. The willingness to change one's mind when the facts speak against it.

The ability to tolerate complexity. Not to simplify everything until it becomes wrong. Not to reduce every problem to a scapegoat. The patience to understand connections before announcing solutions.

The ability to think long-term. Not just until the next election, but across generations. Not just one's own career in view, but the future of those who cannot yet vote.

And: Humility. The knowledge that one does not know everything. The willingness to admit mistakes. The insight that power does not mean wisdom.

These qualifications appear on no certificate. They are tested in no examination. They only show in action — and often only when it's too late. But they are what distinguishes a good politician from a bad one. Not the specialized knowledge, but how one handles it.

The Reality

Reality is sobering. In most democracies, politicians are not selected by qualification, but by loyalty. Loyal to the party, loyal to the networks, loyal to the funders. Those who want to rise must adapt — not qualify.

The parties are advancement machines in which adaptability counts more than competence. Those who are uncomfortable, who contradict, who have their own ideas — they are weeded out. What remains are the smooth ones, the adapted ones, the careerists. Not the best — the most survivable.

The result is parliaments full of people who are good at becoming politicians — but not necessarily at making policy. Who know how to win elections — but not how to solve problems. Who can talk — but not necessarily think.

This is not a German problem. It is a problem of democracy everywhere. The incentives are wrongly set. Who is rewarded is not the competent, but the successful. And success in politics is not measured by good decisions — but by won elections.

The Sovereign

In the end, the responsibility lies with the sovereign. With the people. With the voters.

We elect whom we elect. We could choose competence — or charisma. We could choose honesty — or promises. We could choose long-term thinking — or short-term gifts.

Usually we choose the latter. Not because we are stupid — because we are human. Because charisma is more convincing than competence. Because promises sound nicer than honesty. Because short-term gifts are more concrete than long-term reason.

The politicians we get are the politicians we elect. And we elect what pleases us — not what is good for us. This is not a criticism of the people — it is a description of human nature. Democracy is not perfect because humans are not perfect.

The sovereign mirrors itself — in its strengths and its weaknesses. The qualification of politicians is a mirror of the qualification of voters. Those who want better politicians must become better voters. That is the uncomfortable truth of democracy.

A Way Out?

Is there a way out? Perhaps.

More transparency. Voters should know what their representatives do — not just what they say. How they vote, whom they meet, where their money comes from. Information is the prerequisite for good decisions.

More participation. Not just a vote every four years, but continuous involvement. Citizens' councils, hearings, referendums — not as a replacement for parliaments, but as a supplement. As a correction against the detachment of career politicians.

More education. Not school education — political education. Understanding how democracy works, how laws are made, how decisions are reached. Those who understand the system can choose better representatives.

And perhaps: different incentives. A system that rewards not just election victories, but also good decisions. That sounds utopian — but democracy itself once sounded utopian.

Do politicians need qualifications? Yes and no. They don't need diplomas — but they need abilities. The ability to listen, to tolerate complexity, to think long-term, to admit mistakes.

Should they mirror the sovereign? Yes — but not only in its weaknesses. Also in its potential. In what it could be, if it gives its best.

The quality of democracy depends not only on the politicians. It depends on all of us. On the voters, the citizens, the sovereign. Politicians are our mirror. If we don't like what we see — we must begin to change ourselves.
