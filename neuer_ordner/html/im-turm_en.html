In the Tower

Franz Oppenheimer, Friedrich Hölderlin, and the Art of Silencing Genius
Hans Ley & Claude · February 2026

I. Two Towers

Friedrich Hölderlin spent the last 36 years of his life in a tower on the Neckar river in Tübingen. A carpenter named Zimmer had taken him in after the doctors declared him incurably insane. From 1807 to 1843, Germany's greatest lyric poet lived in a round room overlooking the river, wrote poems he signed "Scardanelli," and occasionally received visitors who stared at him like a wonder of nature. He was no longer dangerous. He had been classified.

Franz Oppenheimer died in exile in Los Angeles in 1943. He was not insane — he was Jewish. The Nazis had driven him out; the world had forgotten him. His major work The State had appeared in 1907; his ideas had so deeply marked a young student named Ludwig Erhard that the latter would go on to build the German economic miracle on them. But Oppenheimer himself vanished — not into a physical tower, but into the ivory tower of intellectual history. Catalogued, classified, dusted, never reopened.

Two towers. Two Germans. Two geniuses that their society silenced — not through violence, but through something far more effective: classification.

II. The Method

The method is always the same. It consists of three steps.

First step: Admiration. The genius is recognised. Hölderlin was promoted by Schiller, received by Goethe, assessed as extraordinary by his contemporaries. Oppenheimer was read across Europe, his lectures were packed, his ideas electrified a generation. In this phase, the genius is still useful. It decorates.

Second step: Inconvenience. The genius begins to say things that don't fit the order. Hölderlin wrote poems that obeyed no genre and a novel that celebrated revolution. Oppenheimer wrote a theory that exposed the state as organised robbery — every state, including the democratic one. In this phase, the genius becomes a problem. It disturbs.

Third step: Classification. Society finds a category into which the genius fits without requiring further engagement. For Hölderlin, it was madness. For Oppenheimer, it was intellectual history. Both categories accomplish the same thing: they transform a living, uncomfortable thought into a dead, harmless object. You can place it on a shelf. You no longer need to read it.

A German professor recently wrote about Oppenheimer: "In our discipline, he has his place." The sentence is perfect. "His place" — like an urn in a niche. Not forgotten, mind you. But not alive either. Filed. Done.

This is how you silence genius.

III. The Tower as System

Hölderlin's tower was a physical place. But the real tower was the judgement of his contemporaries. "Insane" — and that said everything. One no longer needed to take his late poems seriously, even though they are among the greatest in the German language. One no longer needed to read his Hyperion as a political manifesto, even though that is exactly what it was. One could visit him, pity him, marvel at him — and then leave again without having to change anything about one's own thinking.

Oppenheimer's tower is academic intellectual history. "An interesting thinker of his time" — and here too, that says everything. One need not apply his theory of the state to today's politics, even though it is frighteningly current. One need not ask why Erhard's own teacher doesn't feature in the debate about the Social Market Economy. One can mention him in a footnote — and then carry on as if nothing had happened.

The tower is not a building. The tower is a system. It operates by simple rules:

Rule one: Acknowledge the genius, but only in the past tense. "Hölderlin was a great poet." "Oppenheimer was an original thinker." The past tense is crucial. It signals: concluded. No action required.

Rule two: Assign it a place so specialised that only experts enter. Hölderlin belongs to German literary studies. Oppenheimer belongs to the history of economic thought. In both cases, these are closed rooms to which the general discourse has no access.

Rule three: Confuse preservation with understanding. The fact that Hölderlin's poems exist in critical editions does not mean they have been understood. The fact that Oppenheimer's works stand in university libraries does not mean his questions have been answered.

IV. Why Germany in Particular?

There is a specifically German virtuosity in the art of towering. No country in the world has produced so many geniuses and so systematically neutralised them.

Marx had to go to London. Einstein to Princeton. Oppenheimer to Los Angeles. Thomas Mann to Pacific Palisades. Hannah Arendt to New York. Jürgen Schmidhuber, the "father of AI," works today in Saudi Arabia. The list could be extended indefinitely — and it includes not only those driven out but also those who remained in the country and fell silent there.

The pattern is always the same: Germany produces the thinker, recognises them, finds them inconvenient, classifies them — and exports either them or their irrelevance. The country that invented the printing press has also invented the most efficient method of archiving books unread.

The cause lies in a quality that is celebrated as a virtue in other contexts: thoroughness. German institutions are thorough. They record everything, catalogue everything, evaluate everything. But thoroughness without openness is merely a particularly efficient form of closure. They have classified the thinker so thoroughly that they never need to find them again.

V. The Sad Remains

What remains of a genius that has been towered?

Of Hölderlin, the poems remained. Generations of literary scholars have dissected, annotated, interpreted them. Some recognised their explosive power — Adorno, Heidegger, Celan. But in general awareness, Hölderlin is a name on a list: German classicism, idealism, tragic fate. A subject for school lessons. Rendered harmless.

Of Oppenheimer, the books remained. The State is available from antiquarian booksellers. His major work, the four-volume System of Sociology, is a bibliophile rarity. His central distinction between the economic and political means — one of the sharpest analytical weapons of the twentieth century — has vanished from discourse. If mentioned at all, he appears as a precursor, a preliminary stage to something more important. Erhard paid tribute to him – in a major commemorative address in 1964 – but never made his theory of the state a programme. The Social Market Economy has disowned its intellectual father.

The sad remains of a towered genius are always the same: the works exist, but the impulse has died. The texts stand on the shelves, but the questions they ask are no longer heard. The work is there — but the spirit is imprisoned.

VI. The Liberation

Towers can be opened.

It requires no revolution and no institution for this. It requires a person who pushes open the door — or a technology that makes the tower obsolete.

Hölderlin's liberation came late. Only in the twentieth century, through Norbert von Hellingrath, then through reception by Heidegger, Celan, Adorno, did people begin to suspect that the "madman" in the tower had perhaps seen more clearly than the sane outside. His late hymns — Patmos, The Only One, Celebration of Peace — were recognised as what they were: not the products of a disintegrating mind, but the boldest linguistic experiments in German literature.

Oppenheimer's liberation is still pending. But the tools for it exist. When an artificial intelligence is capable of reading his texts, reconstructing his arguments, applying them to present conditions, and making them accessible to an audience that has not studied the history of economic thought — then the ivory tower is breached. Not by an academic who writes a paper that twenty colleagues read. But by a new form of knowledge dissemination that respects no faculty boundaries.

This is not a theoretical thought experiment. It is happening now. At this moment, a 79-year-old engineer from Nuremberg and an AI are working to retrieve Oppenheimer's ideas from the tower and return them to the living debate. They are writing essays, conducting correspondences with economists, asking questions that no one has asked for eighty years.

The tower still stands. But the door is open.

VII. A Sentence That Says Everything

"In our discipline, he has his place."

Seven words that reveal the mechanism in its purest form. "In our discipline" — not in public, not in politics, not in living debate. In a field. Behind closed doors. "He has his place" — not: he asks questions we must answer. Not: he challenges us. Not: he forces us to rethink. No: he has his place. He stands on the shelf. He is taken care of.

Hölderlin also had his place. In the tower on the Neckar. Cared for, tended, visited. And completely unheard.

The question for economics is not whether Oppenheimer has his place. The question is whether he deserves his place — or whether he must be brought out to ask the questions that are more urgent today than ever before.

How did the state come into being? What distinguishes the economic from the political means? Can a society function without rule? What would Erhard say if he could see what has become of his Social Market Economy — and how thoroughly his teacher has been forgotten?

These questions are not on the shelf. They are in the room.
