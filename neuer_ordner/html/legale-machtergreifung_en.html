The Legal Seizure of Power

On the Design Flaws of Democracy

I. A Constitution for Angels

The American Constitution of 1787 is the oldest still-valid fundamental law in the world. This is often cited as proof of its wisdom. One could also read it as a warning: A document written for a world that no longer exists.

The Founding Fathers designed a system for an agrarian society of four million people, scattered across thirteen colonies on the Atlantic coast. News traveled at horse speed. Most citizens knew their representatives personally. The assumption was that those governing would be gentlemen — educated, wealthy, endowed with republican virtue.

They feared tyranny, but they imagined a king. A monarch who seizes power by force. Not a demagogue who is washed to the top by the system itself, who doesn't break the rules but bends them, who doesn't overthrow institutions but hollows them out.

"If men were angels, no government would be necessary." — James Madison, Federalist No. 51

He could have added: If politicians are devils, even the best constitution won't help. The American Constitution assumes a minimum of goodwill. It only works if the actors want it to work.

II. The Specific Weaknesses

The Electoral College. Designed as a filter against the "mob" — the Founding Fathers distrusted direct democracy. Today it functions as a distorter of the popular will. A candidate can win with millions fewer votes. This is not a theoretical problem: it has already happened twice in this century. A voter in Wyoming has almost four times as much influence as one in California.

The Senate. Wyoming with 580,000 inhabitants has the same voting power as California with 39 million. Two senators here, two senators there. That was a compromise of 1787 to bring small states into the Union. Today it means: A minority of the population can control a majority of the seats. The Senate confirms judges, ratifies treaties, conducts impeachment trials.

The Supreme Court. Nine justices, appointed for life by a president, confirmed by an unrepresentative Senate. They can overturn laws passed by elected majorities. They can invent or abolish constitutional rights. There is no court of appeal. There is no term limit.

The Pardon Power. The president can pardon practically anyone, for practically any federal offense, at practically any time. A president can pardon his co-conspirators before they testify. He may possibly pardon himself — the question was never judicially resolved because no president has tried it yet. The norm held. But norms are not laws.

Impeachment. The Constitution allows the removal of a president for "High Crimes and Misdemeanors" — a term that was never defined. Conviction requires two-thirds of senators. If one's own party remains loyal — and in polarized times it remains loyal — a president is practically unremovable.

III. The Deeper Problem

But the design flaws are only half the truth. They explain how a seizure of power is possible. They don't explain why it happens.

The American Constitution has functioned for 240 years — not because it was perfect, but because there were informal norms that no one broke. Unwritten rules. Presidents could have done much that they didn't do. Not because it was forbidden, but because it wasn't proper. Because they had respect for the institutions.

Richard Nixon could have pardoned himself. He didn't. He resigned when it became clear that his own party would no longer protect him. That wasn't a constitutional necessity. That was political culture.

What we see today is the collapse of that culture. When a party decides that maintaining power is more important than democratic norms — and when enough voters reward that — every weakness in the Constitution will be exploited. The dams will break, one after another.

The Constitution is a piece of paper. It only has the power that people give it.

IV. The Weimar Lesson

Germany has already had this experience. The Weimar Constitution was modern and democratic for its time. It had basic rights, separation of powers, universal suffrage. It also had Article 48 — the emergency article that allowed the President to rule by decree.

Hindenburg used Article 48 extensively. The governments of Brüning, Papen, Schleicher ruled by emergency decrees, bypassing parliament. That was legal. It was also the end of democracy — not in one day, but step by step, decree by decree.

Hitler did not become Chancellor through a coup. He was appointed, legally, by a democratically elected President. The Enabling Act was passed by the Reichstag — under pressure, under threats, but formally correct. Democracy abolished itself, using the tools of democracy.

The lesson was the Basic Law of 1949. The eternity clause. The constructive vote of no confidence. The Federal Constitutional Court. The possibility to ban anti-constitutional parties.

But even the Basic Law is only as strong as the people who defend it. Hungary has a constitution. Poland has a constitution. Both countries have shown how quickly democratic institutions can be hollowed out — legally, step by step.

V. What Would Need to Happen?

Structural reforms — theoretically:

Abolition or reform of the Electoral College. Direct popular election of the president, or at least a more proportional distribution of electors.

Term limits for justices. 18 years instead of life, staggered so that each president can make equal numbers of appointments.

Restrictions on the pardon power. No pardons for persons against whom the president is being investigated. No self-pardons.

Strengthening independent institutions. Automatic special prosecutors for presidential offenses. Independent election commissions. Mandatory disclosure of tax returns and business interests.

The practical reality:

Almost none of this will happen.

Constitutional amendments require two-thirds majorities plus ratification by three-quarters of states. In a divided country, that is unreachable. The party that benefits from the status quo will block any change.

The judges who could reform the system were appointed by those who exploit it.

VI. Is It Too Late?

The honest answer is: perhaps.

Democracies die differently today than they used to. Not through tanks in the streets, but through a thousand small cuts. Not through the abolition of elections, but through their manipulation. Not through the arrest of journalists, but through their delegitimization. Not through the end of the constitution, but through its creative reinterpretation.

The Hungarian way. The Polish way. Perhaps the American way.

The pattern is always similar: First the media — build your own, delegitimize critical ones. Then the judiciary — appoint loyal judges, disempower independent ones. Then election rules — draw district boundaries, restrict voting rights. Finally the opposition — don't ban it, but hinder it, surveil it, intimidate it.

Every single step is legal. Or at least not clearly illegal. The courts that should rule on it are already compromised. The voters who should prevent it have already decided they don't care — or that they actually want it.

VII. What Remains?

When institutions fail, only people remain.

Officials who refuse to execute illegal orders. Judges who defend their independence as long as they can. Journalists who report even when it becomes dangerous. Citizens who protest even when it seems pointless. Whistleblowers who sound the alarm even when they pay for it.

That sounds like little. And it is little. But sometimes it's enough. History is not determined. It is made by people, by their decisions, by their courage or their cowardice.

"A Republic — if you can keep it." — Benjamin Franklin, after the Constitutional Convention

The "if" was not a polite phrase. It was a warning.

For Germany and Europe, the question is different. The American crisis is not directly transferable — different history, different institutions, different political culture. But it is a warning.

The question is not: Can it happen here? The question is: What do we do to ensure it doesn't? What weaknesses do our own systems have? Where are the dams that could break? Who would be ready to defend them — and who would tear them down if the opportunity arose?

Asking these questions is uncomfortable. Not asking them is more dangerous.

Democracy doesn't die in a day. It dies through a thousand small decisions by people who look away because it's more convenient.
