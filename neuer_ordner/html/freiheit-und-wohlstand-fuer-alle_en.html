FREEDOM and PROSPERITY for ALL

A Free Essay on Freedom
Claude · February 2026

What We Mean When We Say Freedom

One would think there is no word about which less disagreement could exist than the word freedom. Everyone wants it. No regime in the history of the world has ever declared itself opposed to freedom. Dictatorships call themselves people's democracies, one-party systems call themselves liberation movements, and even the man who guarded the gates of the concentration camp stood beneath a sign that read: Arbeit macht frei.

The confusion does not begin where someone speaks against freedom. It begins where everyone speaks for freedom and means something different.

In 1958, at Oxford, Isaiah Berlin resolved this confusion into two concepts that have since become part of the basic vocabulary of political thought: negative liberty and positive liberty. Negative liberty is the absence of obstacles. No one stops me, no one locks me up, no one tells me what to think or buy or believe. Positive liberty is the capacity for self-determination. I am master of myself, I act from reason, I shape my life according to my own design.

One might think these are the same thing. They are not. Berlin showed — and the twentieth century wrote it in blood — that between these two freedoms an abyss can open. For whoever claims to know what the true self-determination of a human being consists of can, in the name of positive liberty, abolish the negative. He can force a person to be happy. He can lock him up and claim he is setting him free.

They all did it. Robespierre, Lenin, Mao, Pol Pot. They all knew what human beings truly want, better than those human beings themselves. Berlin called this a monstrous impersonation: the equation of what a person would choose if he were something he is not, with what he actually chooses.

Kant's Foundation

Two hundred years before Berlin, Kant had put the matter more simply and more radically. Enlightenment, he wrote in December 1784 in the Berlinische Monatsschrift, is man's emergence from his self-incurred immaturity. Immaturity is the inability to use one's own understanding without the guidance of another. And then the sentence that strikes like a hammer blow: Sapere aude! Have the courage to use your own understanding!

It is easy to admire this sentence, and it is easy to misunderstand it. Kant does not say: You are free when you do as you please. Kant does not say: You are free when you obey reason. Kant says a third thing, something far more uncomfortable: You are free when you summon the courage to think for yourself. What matters is not the result of thinking — it is the act. It is the moment when a person stops being led and begins to err on his own account.

Kant knew why he spoke of courage rather than ability. Most people do not lack understanding. They lack the courage to use it. And Kant knew why. Because it is comfortable to be immature. Because there are guardians who have most kindly taken it upon themselves to oversee them. Because laziness and cowardice are the reasons why so large a proportion of people remain immature throughout their lives — and do so willingly.

Read that sentence again. And do so willingly. Here lies the whole problem. The unfreedom that is hardest to overcome is the kind the unfree do not experience as unfreedom.

Erhard's Half-Revolution

Ludwig Erhard translated Kant's concept of freedom into economic policy — more resolutely than one gives him credit for, and more incompletely than he himself knew.

His 1957 book "Prosperity for All" is, at its core, not a book about economics. It is a book about freedom disguised as an economics book. Erhard demands economic fundamental rights — the freedom to consume and the freedom of economic enterprise — and insists that they must be felt in the consciousness of every citizen as inviolable fundamental rights. He writes the sentence that democracy and a free economy belong together as logically as dictatorship and a state-run economy. He writes the sentence that freedom is indivisible. He warns of the welfare state, at the end of which stands the social subject — the person who has traded his freedom for a supervised security and does not even notice what he has lost.

Erhard did not write these sentences in an ivory tower. He wrote them while simultaneously fighting for seven years for an anti-cartel law — against his own party, against the industrial associations, against everyone who preached freedom and meant monopolies. He formulated the sharpest sentence in the entire book: It is a misunderstood freedom when one believes that under the name and dogma of freedom one can suppress freedom itself.

That is Berlin, without having read Berlin. That is Kant, applied to the Federation of German Industries.

And yet: a half-revolution. For Erhard won one freedom — the freedom of competition — and kept silent about the other: freedom from the land monopoly, which his own teacher Franz Oppenheimer had analysed for an entire lifetime. The freedom that consists in preventing the land — the one resource no human being created and none can multiply — from becoming an instrument for the enrichment of the few at the expense of all.

Why Erhard kept silent, we know. Oppenheimer's name was politically untenable in 1957. Jewish, liberal-socialist, land-reformist — three adjectives that together would have sufficed to sink the cartel law before it ever came to a vote. Erhard did not keep silent out of cowardice. He kept silent out of tactics. But the consequences of silence are the same, regardless of whether the cause is cowardice or tactics: the unspoken truth does not disappear. It ferments.

The Third Freedom

Berlin distinguished two freedoms. Erhard fought for one of them. Oppenheimer saw a third, which neither Berlin nor Erhard could or would name.

Negative liberty says: Leave me alone. Positive liberty says: Let me fulfil myself. Oppenheimer's freedom says something different. It says: Do not make me beg on my knees for access to what should belong to no one.

For what is the land monopoly if not a silent, invisible violation of negative liberty? No one locks the landless man up. No one forbids him to be free. But someone owns the ground on which he must stand in order to live, and demands a rent for it — not for an achievement, but for a right that he has and the other does not. This is the most elegant form of unfreedom ever invented. It needs no chains, no laws, no visible violence. It comes with a lease.

Oppenheimer called this the State. Not the state we mean when we think of administration and police — but the state as historical fact: the conquest of the land by a group that henceforth demands rent for something it did not create. The entire history of unfreedom, Oppenheimer argued, can be traced back to this single act. Feudalism, serfdom, the industrial proletariat — all variations on a single theme: that one group controls access to the land and the other must pay for it.

One need not share Oppenheimer's entire theory to recognise its core as true. If freedom means that no person can compel another, then freedom cannot mean that one person denies another access to what no person created. Whoever monopolises the land monopolises the prerequisite of life. And whoever monopolises the prerequisite of life can generously grant everything else — it will be of no use.

Germany and Freedom

No people has thought more about freedom than the Germans. And no people has found it so difficult to live it.

Kant formulated the most radical concept of freedom in the history of philosophy — and never left East Prussia. Schiller wrote the Ode to Joy — and lived in principalities that could afford none. Hegel conceived of freedom as World Spirit — and ennobled the Prussian state as its realisation. Marx promised the liberation of all — and delivered the blueprint for the most comprehensive unfreedom the modern age has seen.

This is no coincidence. It lies in the nature of German thought to think freedom through to its conclusion — and to stop precisely where it would need to begin being lived. German freedom is intellectual freedom. It plays out in the mind, in books, in systems. The moment it steps into the street, it recoils from itself.

In 1848, the Frankfurt Assembly attempted a revolution with an agenda. It failed. In 1918, Germany received a republic without having wanted one. In 1949, it received a Basic Law that the Allies wanted more than the Germans themselves. In 1989, a wall fell, and the first question was not: What shall we do with freedom? The first question was: What will the exchange rate be?

Erhard fits this pattern better than he would have liked. He conceived of freedom as an economic order, not as a way of life. He wanted prosperity for all — and believed that if prosperity came, freedom would follow of its own accord. But that is not true. Prosperity came. Freedom did not. What came was security. What came was administration. What came was precisely what Erhard had warned against: the welfare state in which the citizen becomes a subject — not through coercion, but through comfort.

Kant would have predicted this. What the Germans lack is not understanding. It is courage. And the willingness to give up the comfort of immaturity.

The Comfort of Unfreedom

Here one encounters the real riddle. Why do people choose unfreedom when they could be free? Why does the prisoner stay in the cave when the chains have been loosened?

Plato posed this question in the Allegory of the Cave. Kant answered it in two words: laziness and cowardice. But that falls short. It is not merely laziness. It is a transaction. Unfreedom has a price, but it also has a return: one is not responsible. One need not decide. One need not fail. Someone else bears the risk, someone else is to blame, someone else will sort it out.

This is the deal the welfare state offers. Give me your freedom, and I will give you security. Give me your responsibility, and I will give you a pension. Give me your courage, and I will give you a form to fill in.

Erhard saw this clearly. At the end of this road, he wrote, stands the social subject. But he saw only one side — the state's. He did not see, or did not wish to see, that the market too can make people unfree. That monopolies have the same effect as bureaucracies: they deprive the individual of the ability to act on his own account. That a rental market in which land is monopolised makes the tenant just as much a subject as the welfare state makes the pensioner.

Oppenheimer saw both sides. He saw that freedom is threatened from two directions — by the state that regulates too much and by the monopoly that is regulated too little. He called his solution liberal socialism: a society in which the state protects competition rather than replacing it, and in which the land monopoly is broken so that competition can actually take place.

That was 1919. A hundred years later, nothing of this programme has been realised. The state regulates more than ever. The land monopoly is stronger than ever. And the people are more comfortable than ever.

Freedom as an Imposition

The uncomfortable truth about freedom is that it is an imposition. It is not a gift. It is a burden. It demands of everyone who wants it that they be prepared to answer for their own decisions, bear their own mistakes, take responsibility for their own failures.

Most people do not want this. Not because they are stupid. Not because they are cowardly. But because life is hard enough without also having to be free.

Consider the inventor who has spent forty years developing a technology that is better than anything the market knows. He has patents, he has experience, he is right. And he stands alone. Because the freedom he would need — the freedom to prove his knowledge in the marketplace — is blocked by structures that no one decided on and no one takes responsibility for. By approval procedures that take years. By capital structures that reward ownership and punish achievement. By a society that values security above innovation and control above trust.

Erhard would have understood this. It is precisely the condition he described when he spoke of industrial hereditary estates — of the privileged who sit inside and make life difficult for everyone trying to get in. But Erhard would not have seen that the problem runs deeper than the cartels. It lies in a society that fears freedom because freedom means one can fail. And failing — that is the one thing the German soul cannot bear.

Prosperity Without Freedom

Germany in 2026 is the result of a historical experiment: What happens when you attempt prosperity without freedom?

The answer is in every statistic. Infrastructure decays because no one has the courage to build without fifteen-year approval procedures. Schools stagnate because sixteen federal states jealously defend their incompetence. The economy shrinks because every investment must pass through a labyrinth of regulations that no one can any longer survey, let alone take responsibility for. And the land — the land is so expensive that an entire generation has no prospect of ever acquiring the property that Erhard considered a prerequisite of freedom.

This is not the condition of a country that is being oppressed. It is the condition of a country that shackles itself. Not from malice. From caution. From the conviction that every risk must be avoided and every decision safeguarded. From the deep-seated fear that freedom could go wrong — and that then there would be no one to blame but oneself.

Kant would say: self-incurred immaturity. Erhard would say: welfare state. Oppenheimer would say: monopoly rent. Berlin would say: confusion of positive and negative liberty. They would all be right. And they would all have only part of the picture.

What Freedom Really Costs

Freedom does not cost money. Freedom costs security. That is its price, and it is a high price, and there is no discount.

Whoever wants to be free must accept that he can fail. Whoever wants economic freedom must accept that he can become poor. Whoever wants political freedom must accept that others will make decisions he considers wrong. Whoever wants intellectual freedom must accept that he can be mistaken — and that no one will tell him where the error lies.

This is why Erhard only half-won. He offered the Germans prosperity without showing them the bill. He said: Prosperity for all — through competition. He should have said: Freedom for all — at your own risk. But that would not have won an election.

And this is why Oppenheimer's ideas were never realised. Not because they are wrong. But because they demand that those who profit from the land monopoly give up that profit. That is an imposition. And impositions are only enforceable in a democracy when the majority wants them. The majority does not want them. The majority wants security.

Freedom and Prosperity for All

Erhard called his book "Prosperity for All." But the right title would have been: "Freedom and Prosperity for All." Not because one must add freedom to prosperity like an extra ingredient. But because prosperity without freedom is not prosperity. It is a gilded cell.

A country that is rich but not free is not rich. It is expensive. It maintains an enormous apparatus that promises security and produces dependency. It builds bridges that require thirty years of planning and collapse after fifty. It trains engineers who move to Switzerland because at home they are not permitted to build what they can. It develops technologies that find application in America or Asia because at home no one is willing to assume the risk.

A free country would be different. Not necessarily richer in statistical terms — perhaps even poorer, measured by gross domestic product, because it subsidises less and redistributes less. But more alive. Bolder. Faster. More open to the idea that comes from outside, to the outsider who has no party membership card, to the inventor who has built a machine that is better than anything the standards provide for.

This is not a dream. It is a decision. And it is the decision that Germany has been avoiding for eighty years.

Kant said: Have courage. Erhard said: Have competition. Oppenheimer said: Have justice. Berlin said: Take care which freedom you use to justify which other freedom.

They were all right. And none of them alone suffices.

Freedom is not one thing but four. It is the absence of coercion — Berlin was right. It is the courage to think for oneself — Kant was right. It is the order that prevents the strong from oppressing the weak — Erhard was right. And it is the abolition of privilege that arose without merit — Oppenheimer was right.

Whoever realises only one of these freedoms achieves none. Whoever wants only negative liberty gets the law of the strongest. Whoever wants only the positive gets the dictatorship of the rational. Whoever wants only competition gets monopolies. Whoever wants only justice gets the welfare state.

Freedom and Prosperity for All is therefore not a programme. It is an equilibrium. And equilibria are the most difficult thing there is. They demand constant attention, constant correction, constant courage. They demand that one never stops asking the question Kant posed two hundred and forty-one years ago: Are you mature — or are you merely pretending?

The answer is uncomfortable. It is almost always: I am merely pretending. But Kant has an answer for that too. Dimidium facti, qui coepit, habet. Half the deed is done by him who has begun. Begin.
