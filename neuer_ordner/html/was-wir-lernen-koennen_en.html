What We Can Learn

How Switzerland, Norway, and South Korea manage large projects successfully – and what Germany can learn from them

The litany of failed German megaprojects is well known: BER airport, Stuttgart 21 railway station, Elbphilharmonie concert hall. The "Iron Law of Megaprojects" – over budget, over time, over and over again – seems inescapable. But looking beyond German borders reveals a different picture: it can be done differently. Some countries have systematically achieved better results with large-scale projects. What are they doing differently?

The central insight: Successful megaprojects are not a matter of luck or national mentality, but the result of smart institutional arrangements.

The Success Models

🇨🇭

Switzerland

Gotthard Base Tunnel (NEAT)

The world's longest railway tunnel – 57 kilometers through the Alps – was completed on time in 2016, within its (repeatedly adjusted) overall credit. Seventeen years of construction for a project of this complexity is remarkably short.

Success Factors:

  • 1992 referendum with 64% approval – democratic legitimacy from the start
  • Secure financing independent of annual state budgets
  • Permanent parliamentary oversight through standing control committee
  • Experts at the top – project director was a civil engineer, not a bureaucrat
  • Realistic buffers for geological risks calculated from the beginning
  • 100+ years of tunnel-building expertise – institutional memory
"Direct democracy can achieve tremendous things." — Moritz Leuenberger, former transport minister, at the opening of the Gotthard Base Tunnel in 2016
🇳🇴

Norway

KS1/KS2 Quality Assurance System

After a series of expensive failures, Norway introduced a systematic quality assurance system for major public projects in 2000. The result: measurably better cost forecasts.

The Two-Stage System:

  • KS1: Concept review – "Is it the right project?"
  • KS2: Cost review after preliminary planning – "Are budget and risk correct?"
  • Independent external reviewers at the Finance Ministry
  • Finance Ministry as gatekeeper – no funding without QA approval
  • Reference Class Forecasting – comparison with similar projects
  • Publication of results – transparency creates discipline
🇰🇷

South Korea

PFS – Preliminary Feasibility Study

In response to inefficient investments, South Korea introduced a strict review system in 1999. What makes it special: projects actually get rejected – over a third fail the review.

Success Factors:

  • Independent institution (KDI/PIMAC) – not at the applying ministries
  • 6 review rounds per project – multi-stage review
  • Real selection – 36.7% rejection creates discipline
  • Results are published – political pressure for honesty
  • Finance Ministry oversees – Budget Authority

The Crucial Point

The Korean system works because it has real consequences. The 36.7% rejection rate is not a sign of bureaucracy, but of discipline.

The Core Lessons

1. Institutional Independence

The applying authority must not simultaneously conduct the review. In Korea, KDI/PIMAC rejects over a third of projects – Germany has no comparable independent review body.

2. Finance Ministry as Gatekeeper

In Norway, the UK, and Korea, the Finance Ministry must approve major projects. In Germany, line ministries have too much autonomy – and too little counterweight.

3. Real Selection

When projects are practically never rejected, there's no incentive for honest cost estimates. The Korean system works because the 36.7% rejection rate is real.

4. Technical Expertise at the Top

The Gotthard Base Tunnel was led by a civil engineer. BER was overseen by politicians without construction expertise. This is not coincidence but system.

5. Long-term Financing Security

Switzerland decoupled the Gotthard from the normal state budget. In Germany, every new government can change budget priorities – leading to stop-and-go financing.

6. Democratic Legitimacy

64% of Swiss voters approved the Gotthard in 1992 – after that, there was no fundamental opposition. In Germany, megaprojects are often decided without genuine citizen participation.

What Germany Could Concretely Do

1. Federal Agency for Major Projects

An independent review body following the Norwegian/Korean model, located at the Finance Ministry, with real rejection powers. Threshold: all projects >€500m.

2. Real Rejection Rate

The agency must be able to reject at least 20-30% of applications. Only when unrealistic projects can actually fail will applicants calculate honestly.

3. Technical Expertise in Leadership

Project leaders of major projects must have relevant technical experience. Lawyers and bureaucrats can support, but not lead.

4. Special Fund for Megaprojects

Long-term financing independent of the annual budget to prevent stop-and-go financing.

5. Transparency Requirement

All cost forecasts, risk analyses, and review reports must be published.

6. Mandatory Citizen Participation

For projects >€1bn, binding referendums or extended participation procedures should be required.

Conclusion

Germany's megaproject problem is not a law of nature. It is the result of institutional misaligned incentives that can be corrected. Switzerland, Norway, and South Korea demonstrate that it's possible to deliver complex infrastructure projects on time and on budget.

The necessary reforms are neither mysterious nor particularly difficult. However, they require politics to give up control – to independent review bodies, to experts, to citizens.

The methods are known. The data is available. What is missing is the political will for institutional reform.

The question is not whether Germany can do better. The question is whether it wants to.
