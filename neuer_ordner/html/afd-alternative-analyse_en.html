Is the AfD an Alternative?

A systematic examination of governing capability

I.

In another essay I analyzed Friedrich Merz — a man who spent his entire life controlling without ever acting operationally. The question was: Does a career of supervisory boards, consulting positions, and lobbying jobs qualify someone for the operational business of governing?

The same question can be asked of an entire party. Not: Is its ideology good or bad? But: Could it govern if it wanted to?

The AfD has been demanding participation in government for years. It presents itself as an "alternative" to the establishment. But is it — viewed purely functionally — capable of governing?

In the following, I will examine exclusively the operational question: personnel, experience, stability, competence. No evaluation of political goals.

II. The Leadership Personnel

Alice Weidel — Co-Chair and Chancellor Candidate 2025

Studied economics and business administration in Bayreuth, doctorate on the Chinese pension system (summa cum laude). Scholarship holder of the Konrad Adenauer Foundation. Then: Goldman Sachs as analyst, Allianz Global Investors, six years in China, later co-founder of start-ups.

By purely operational criteria: better qualified than the sitting Federal Chancellor.

That is more operational experience than Merz ever had. An analyst analyzes, but Weidel also co-founded start-ups — that requires actual action, not just evaluation. Her academic qualification is solid, her international experience remarkable.

Tino Chrupalla — Co-Chair

Secondary school diploma, apprenticeship as painter and varnisher, master craftsman examination 2003, founded his own painting business with seven employees.

By purely operational criteria: better qualified than Merz.

This is remarkable. Chrupalla built and ran a business. He hired staff, acquired contracts, managed budgets, handled crises. He knows what a liter of milk costs and what it means to have to pay wages at the end of the month.

A master painter with seven employees has more operational leadership experience than a board member at BlackRock. That sounds paradoxical but is logical: one acted, the other observed.

III. The Personnel Turnover

Here the problems begin.

The AfD has lost three party chairpersons since its founding in 2013 — not through elections, but through factional battles:

Bernd Lucke (2013–2015)

Founder of the party, economics professor. Wanted a eurosceptic but bourgeois party. Was pushed out by the right wing. Founded the "Liberal-Conservative Reformers" — insignificant today.

Frauke Petry (2015–2017)

Took over from Lucke, was herself pushed out by an even more right-wing faction. Left both parliamentary group and party a few days after the 2017 federal election. Founded "The Blues" — dissolved today.

Jörg Meuthen (2015–2022)

Longest-serving chairman, tried to contain the far-right "Wing," failed. Left in 2022 and spoke of "totalitarian undertones" in his own party. Later joined the "Centre" — a splinter party with 500 members.

Three chairpersons in nine years. All left in conflict. All founded new parties or disappeared into splinter groups. All warned of radicalization in their former party after leaving.

This is not normal personnel turnover. This is a pattern.

IV. The Pattern

The pattern is: whoever tries to moderate the party loses. Whoever fights the radical wing is fought back — and loses.

Lucke wanted bourgeois respectability. He lost to Petry.
Petry wanted demarcation from the right. She lost to Meuthen and Gauland.
Meuthen wanted to dissolve the "Wing." He lost to Chrupalla, Weidel, and Höcke.

The party has a built-in selection mechanism: It selects rightward. Not because all members are far-right — but because those who are not leave sooner or later.

For governing capability, this means: The party cannot retain personnel capable of compromise. Those who want compromise get sorted out.

V. The Governing Experience

The AfD has been represented in state parliaments since 2014. In the Bundestag since 2017. In some federal states, it is the strongest or second-strongest force.

Governing experience: Zero.

The party has never been part of a state government. It has never appointed a minister. It has never been responsible for a state budget, never led a ministry — its administrative experience is limited to one district office and a handful of town halls.

This fundamentally distinguishes it from all other parties represented in the Bundestag. CDU, SPD, Greens, Left — all have governed somewhere at some point. They know how administration works. They have experience with the difference between demanding and implementing.

The AfD lacks this experience. It is a pure opposition party. For twelve years.

VI. The Professional Competence

Governing requires expertise in many areas: finance, foreign policy, defense, health, education, infrastructure, justice.

Weidel has financial expertise — no question. Her doctorate and professional experience qualify her for economic and fiscal policy questions.

But who in the AfD leadership has expertise in foreign policy? In defense? In health? In education?

The party has no one with diplomatic experience. No one who has ever led a federal or state agency. No one with experience in international negotiations.

Chrupalla, the co-chair, ran a painting business. That is more than Merz — but it is not a qualification for foreign policy. When Chrupalla travels to Moscow and lays wreaths, it shows not diplomatic skill but its absence.

VII. The Organizational Structure

A governing party needs internal stability. It must conduct factional battles in ways that keep the organization functional. It must develop personnel, offer career paths, build expertise.

The AfD has the opposite: permanent factional battles that end with the destruction of the losers. Today's chairman can be tomorrow's traitor. Today's moderate can be tomorrow's outcast.

Additionally: The party is split between East and West, between Wing and non-Wing, between fundamentalists and pragmatists. These divisions have not been bridged — they have been institutionalized.

Such an organization can oppose. It can channel discontent. It can win votes.

But can it govern? Can it run ministries where stability and continuity are required? Can it motivate civil servants, forge compromises, implement long-term projects?

VIII. The Comparison

Is the AfD an alternative?

Compared to the CDU under Merz: The AfD has more operational leadership experience at the top (Chrupalla > Merz), but less governing experience as a party (zero vs. decades).

Compared to the SPD: The AfD has no administrative experience, no established networks in ministries, no personnel who know how to get legislation through the Bundestag.

Compared to the Greens: The AfD has a similarly young history, but the Greens accumulated governing experience during that time — at state and federal levels. The AfD has nothing comparable.

Organizationally, the AfD is where the Greens were in 1985: a protest party with charismatic individuals, internal factional battles, and zero governing experience.

The Greens needed almost twenty years to become capable of governing. The AfD has not even started.

IX. The Paradox

Here lies the paradox: The AfD wins votes because it does not govern. It lives off opposition. It lives off criticizing without bearing responsibility. It lives off giving simple answers to complex questions — answers it never has to implement.

If the AfD governed, it would have to compromise. It would have to acknowledge reality. It would have to fail — at bureaucracy, at money, at Europe, at physics.

That would disappoint its supporters. They do not want compromises. They want fundamental opposition.

The AfD is trapped: It cannot govern without losing its base. It cannot not-govern without eventually becoming irrelevant. This contradiction cannot be resolved. It is structural.

X. The Answer

Is the AfD an alternative?

My answer, after purely operational analysis:

No.

Not because of ideology — I deliberately left that aside. But because of structure.

A party that has burned through three chairpersons in twelve years is not stable enough to govern.

A party that systematically sorts out its compromise-capable personnel cannot lead coalitions.

A party without any governing experience cannot run ministries.

A party whose business model is opposition cannot govern without destroying itself.

The AfD has interesting personnel — Weidel is more qualified than Merz, Chrupalla has more operational experience than most career politicians. But a party is more than the sum of its leadership figures.

And this party is structurally incapable of governing.

XI.

This is not a moral judgment. It is a functional diagnosis.

A car can look beautiful and still not drive. A party can win votes and still be unable to govern.

The AfD is not an alternative. It is a symptom.

The symptom of a democracy that disappoints too many people. The symptom of a political class that has lost touch with reality. The symptom of a system that produces alternatives that are not.

"The real problem is not the AfD.
The real problem is that there are no real alternatives."

— The structural diagnosis
