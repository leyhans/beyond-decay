Categories and Horizons

Why we see what we have learned to see — and why expansion is so difficult

The Invisible Walls of Thought

Every person thinks in categories. This is not a weakness but a necessity. Without categories, the world would be unstructured noise. "Chair," "danger," "friend," "useless" — such mental compartments enable quick action. But what helps us also limits us: We can only perceive that for which we have categories. What fits into none is overlooked, distorted, or explained away.

The horizon is the sum of these categories plus the expectations associated with them. A car mechanic sees something different in an engine than an artist does. Both look at the same object, but their horizons determine what becomes visible, what appears relevant, what is even registered as "there."

Expanding Horizons: Possible, but Uncomfortable

Categories and horizons are not set in stone. Education, travel, crises, encounters with the foreign — all of this can expand them. The crucial point: It requires a willingness to be temporarily disoriented. Those who expand their horizon must accept that their previous categories are insufficient. The old worldview must become fragile before a new one can emerge.

This is precisely where resistance sets in.

The Fear of Losing Categories

People cling to their categories because they provide security. Those who "know" how the world works no longer need to ask questions. The refusal to expand one's horizon is rarely conscious. It disguises itself as common sense, as pragmatism, as "that's how we've always done it." Three typical defense mechanisms:

First: Making invisible. What doesn't fit into the categories is simply not perceived. Not actively ignored — it doesn't reach consciousness in the first place. Perception filters it out before it can become a problem.

Second: Forcing to fit. The foreign is bent and twisted until it fits into a familiar compartment after all. A novel process is classified as a "variant of X," an uncomfortable person as a "difficult type." In the process, the new loses precisely what makes it new.

Third: Devaluation. What can neither be made invisible nor forced to fit is declared an enemy. "Unscientific," "impractical," "not relevant." Devaluation protects one's own horizon by denying the foreign its legitimacy.

The Paradox of Experts

Of all things, deep expertise can narrow the horizon. Those who work for decades in a field develop highly specialized categories — and often lose the ability to see cross-connections in the process. The expert knows exactly what is "serious" and what is not, what "works" and what doesn't. Their knowledge becomes a wall.

Thomas Kuhn described this phenomenon for science: Paradigms enable normal science, but they systematically blind us to what doesn't fit the paradigm. Revolutions happen not because the facts change, but because a new generation with different categories grows up.

The expert doesn't recognize the new because they know too much — not too little. Their categories are so finely honed that the unusual falls through the grid.

The Costs of Refusal

At the individual level, the refusal to expand one's horizon leads to an increasingly narrow, increasingly rigid worldview. What doesn't fit must be fought — and less and less fits. Fear grows with narrowness.

At the societal level, collective horizon refusal means stagnation. Institutions built on certain categories defend those categories — even when the world has long since changed. Innovations that cut across established compartments find no home. They are not rejected, they are not even seen.

The Other Possibility

Expanding one's horizon begins with a simple but difficult insight: One's own categories are not the world. They are one view of the world, shaped by origin, education, experience, chance. This insight is uncomfortable because it costs security. But it is also liberating: If the previous categories are not sacrosanct, new ones can emerge.

Encountering the radically other — other cultures, other ways of thinking, other life designs — is more helpful than any theory. Not as tourism that turns the foreign into scenery, but as genuine confrontation that shakes one's own assumptions.

In the end, expanding one's horizon is not a technique but an attitude: the willingness to temporarily relinquish control. Those who cannot or will not do this remain trapped in their categories — comfortable perhaps, but blind to everything that doesn't fit.
