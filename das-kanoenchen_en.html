The Pop-Gun

Julian Reichelt, Nius and the pocket-sized confetti cannon

Rudolf Augstein called his Spiegel the assault gun of democracy. In “The Confetti Cannons” we described how such guns turned into confetti cannons. Gabor Steingart builds ships for them, Mathias Döpfner awards the seals. Julian Reichelt has built one of his own. It is small, it goes off every morning, and a wealthy patron pays for the powder.

I. The gunner

From 2017 to 2021 Julian Reichelt ran Bild. It was the biggest gun in the country, and he liked to fire it. The shot he thought at the time was “the most important story of my life” went off in May 2020. The headline read: “Drosten study on infectious children grossly wrong”.

The story is quickly told. Christian Drosten and his team had published a preliminary version of a study. It found that children carried a viral load similar to adults. Statisticians criticised the method, and Drosten conceded it had been crude. Bild turned this into the claim that the study was wrong and that this had long been admitted internally. Drosten replied that the reporter had misled the team’s English-speaking mathematician on the phone. All the mathematician had said was that a revised version was in the works and would not change the result.

That is what happened. The revised version appeared in June 2020, and the statisticians who had criticised the first one praised it. In 2021 the large analysis followed in the journal Science: at the peak of infection, children up to five reached about 78 percent of the infectiousness of adults.

Reichelt still tells the story as if the study had contained a “simple error” by which “simply the opposite came out”. The shot was loud, and the charge did not hold.

II. The pop-gun

In October 2021 Springer parted ways with Reichelt. The company’s reason: he was “still not clearly separating private and professional matters” and had “told the board untruths about it”. Reichelt denies this. In his own version, his dismissal had to do with his Covid coverage.

In March 2022 his Rome Medien GmbH was entered in the commercial register, with 25,000 euros of share capital. It moved in as a subtenant of CompuGroup Medical, the company founded by Frank Gotthardt. Today it belongs, like the portal Nius, to Vius SE & Co. KGaA in Berlin. Gotthardt keeps the company alive with his money. In 2023 the accounts showed a loss of 13.8 million euros, in 2024 one of just under 16.2 million. By the end of 2024 Gotthardt had put in 37.4 million euros. He told Die Zeit that the loss had not been smaller in 2025.

According to the leaked data described in the next section, Nius had about 12,000 paying subscribers in its fourth year. Bild reports more than 700,000 digital subscribers. Reichelt says his morning show “mostly” reaches 700,000 and “sometimes over a million people”. We cannot check that.

Roughly calculated, using the loss of 2024 and the subscribers of 2026, every subscription comes with more than a thousand euros of loss a year. So far Gotthardt has borne it. That is no assault gun. It is a pop-gun, and the patron pays for the powder.

III. The ammunition

What the pop-gun is loaded with is shown by a data set that the research platform Correctiv analysed in October 2026. It comes from a cyberattack on Nius, comprises just under nine gigabytes and is freely available online. Reichelt has confirmed that there was a leak in May or June and announced a criminal complaint. Correctiv is one of Reichelt’s favourite opponents, and that needs saying. The figures speak for themselves all the same.

The strongest sales month in the data set was October 2025, with 1,939 new subscriptions. That month Nius reported again and again on the business dealings of the Minister of State for Culture, Wolfram Weimer. During the coverage of the constitutional court candidate Frauke Brosius-Gersdorf, about 1,700 new subscriptions came in in July 2025 and around 1,000 in August. In the months before it had been a few hundred. In January 2026 there were 1,064 new subscriptions, more than in any other of the first five months of the year, and the strongest day was 16 January with 115. That day “Nius Live” was about Daniel Günther.

On 7 January, on Markus Lanz’s talk show, Günther had named Nius as an example of media engaging in agitation instead of journalism. Lanz summed up: “We have to regulate it, censor it if necessary, and in the extreme case even ban it.” Günther answered: “Yes.” The Nius videos left out his follow-up sentence about the social media ban for under-sixteens. The editing history in the leaked data shows that Nius first quoted Günther in full. Only later versions shortened the quote so that it sounded like a demand to ban Nius.

Fairness requires this: the “Yes” was said. Günther later said it referred to the social media ban for young people. When Beatrix von Storch claimed on Lanz’s show a week later that Günther had demanded a ban on Nius, Lanz called that “fake news” and played excerpts. Von Storch then conceded that Günther had not directly demanded a ban on Nius. Whoever reads the “Yes” as a call for a ban can point to the wording; whoever relates it to social media can point to the context. Nius went to court. The Schleswig administrative court rejected the urgent application in February 2026, and the higher administrative court dismissed the appeal. Günther, the courts held, had not spoken in an official capacity on the show.

The data show a link in time, not proof of cause and effect. The pattern is striking all the same: subscriptions rise when outrage is directed at a person. The person changes; the powder stays the same.

IV. The charge

What the charge looks like can be heard in a conversation of a good three hours that Reichelt gave the podcaster Ben Berndt (“Ungeskriptet”). First comes the pathos. The new media, he says, are “media love brands”; people love them because “we don’t take them for fools”. Asked what he would write on a slip of paper after losing his memory, he answers: “We shall never surrender”. His most important Bible verse from this time, he says, is “Send me”.

Then come the claims, and they hold up no better than the Drosten story. Saladin, he says, decreed “in the 14th, 15th century” that no research may contradict the Quran. Saladin died in 1193. There is no genocide in Gaza, he says; it would be “the best-fed genocide in history”. In August 2025 the international hunger classification system IPC determined a famine in Gaza Governorate, which includes Gaza City. In September 2025 the commission of inquiry set up by the UN Human Rights Council concluded that Israel is committing genocide in Gaza. Israel rejects both findings. One can dispute them, but not pretend they do not exist.

In “The Confetti Cannons” we described Steingart’s mistake with Icarus: he made King Minos the father, when Minos was the jailer. With Reichelt it is Saladin, shifted by two centuries. These are assertions that sound like facts. The pop-gun fires the same charge as the big confetti cannons, only faster.

V. The recoil

Every gun has a recoil, and in this conversation it hits the gunner.

For three hours Reichelt complains about German “collectivism”. Large parts of the left-wing milieu, he says, would “simply have us taken away at once if they could”. He quotes a supposedly Stalinist formula of power: “Isolate, defame, liquidate.”

In the same conversation he sketches his own programme. He wants to use demonstrations “from caliphate demos to Rafah demos” as a trap: check papers, “round up” the participants and drive them to the airport, at least all those without a German passport, “including their children”. For the others he wants to check when they were naturalised and “think again about the German passport”. And he wishes for “ICE and Fauda”: deportation squads on the American model and, named after an Israeli television series, suppression of extremism at home by intelligence, police and military means. About Berlin taxi drivers he says: “I recognise my Islamist when I see him.” By the beard, by the clothes, by the pendant on the rear-view mirror.

That is the very taking-away he accuses others of. Those to be taken away are people without a German passport who join such a demonstration, children included. And the Islamist is recognised by his appearance.

He should also lay the formula about isolating and defaming next to his own business model. You single out a person, sharpen the point, shorten a quote, and count the subscriptions the next morning. That is our reading of the figures, but it is the obvious one.

VI. What holds

By his own account Reichelt was a war reporter for ten years, in Iraq, Afghanistan and Syria. By his own account he was twice held by men who belonged to IS or worked with it, and narrowly escaped a third arrest. We do not deny him the courage that took.

Some of what he says is documented or at least open to debate. The long school closures during the pandemic are now widely criticised as too long. According to a University of Hamburg study within the research network MOTRA, in which the BKA, Germany’s federal criminal police, takes part, 11.5 percent of Muslims aged 18 to 39 in Germany show a manifest and a further 33.6 percent a latent affinity for Islamism, 45.1 percent in all. What is measured are attitudes, not acts. The measure is disputed, and Reichelt simply turns “affinity” into “Islamists”, but the figure is not invented. And his touchstone for democracy is a good one: what one says in public and what one says behind closed doors must roughly match.

Only he fails his own test. For the evenings with politicians during the pandemic he invokes the protection of sources. In the same conversation he declares that Friedrich Merz, with whom he has had “many personal conversations”, does not believe in the energy transition, and he relates what Markus Lanz told him on the phone. For the phone call he gives a reason: an “intent to destroy” Nius followed it.

VII. Gunner, keeper of the seal, maître de plaisir

In “The Titanisation of the Confetti Cannon” we assigned the roles. Steingart is the maître de plaisir; he stages and celebrates. Döpfner is the keeper of the seal; he grants or withholds approval, as we showed in “Flawless Democrats”. Reichelt is the gunner; he fires. All three circle the same company. Döpfner runs it, Springer took a 36 percent stake in Steingart’s Pioneer in 2019, and Reichelt learned his trade there.

What comes out of all three barrels is confetti, and not because of the noise. Confetti hits people and leaves structures standing. Reichelt rails against “blue-haired beetle conjurers” who supposedly get twelve million euros of taxpayers’ money. Where the money for his own pop-gun comes from does not come up in the conversation. We described it in “Plunder, Lease and Inheritance”: Gotthardt’s fortune comes from software for doctors’ practices, pharmacies and clinics. Their income comes to a large extent from the contributions of the insured.

An assault gun aims at power. The pop-gun aims at whoever brings in the most subscriptions that day.
