The Pop-Gun

Julian Reichelt, Nius and the pocket-sized confetti cannon

Herbert Wehner called the media the assault guns of democracy. In “The Confetti Cannons” we described how the guns turned into confetti cannons. Gabor Steingart builds ships for them, Mathias Döpfner awards the seals. Julian Reichelt has built one of his own. It is small, it goes off every morning, and a billionaire pays for the powder.

I. The gunner

From 2017 to 2021 Julian Reichelt ran Bild. It was the biggest gun in the country, and he liked to fire it. The shot he now calls “the most important story of my life” went off in May 2020. The headline read: “Drosten study on infectious children grossly wrong”.

The story is quickly told. Christian Drosten and his team had published a preliminary version of a study. It found that children carried a viral load similar to adults. Statisticians criticised the method, and Drosten conceded it had been crude. Bild turned this into the claim that the study was wrong and that this had long been admitted internally. Drosten replied that the reporter had misled the team’s English-speaking mathematician on the phone. All the mathematician had said was that a revised version was in the works and would not change the result.

That is what happened. The revised version appeared in June 2020, and the statisticians who had criticised the first one praised it. In 2021 the large analysis followed in the journal Science: at the peak of infection, children up to five reached about 78 percent of the infectiousness of adults.

Reichelt still tells the story as if “the opposite came out”. The shot was loud, and the charge did not hold.

II. The pop-gun

In October 2021 Springer parted ways with Reichelt. The company’s reason: he was “still not clearly separating private and professional matters” and had “told the board untruths about it”. Reichelt denies this. In his own version he was fired because he contradicted “the system” over Covid.

At the end of March 2022 he founded Rome Medien GmbH with 25,000 euros of share capital. It started out as a subtenant of CompuGroup Medical, the company of Frank Gotthardt. It became the portal Nius, now run by Vius SE & Co. KG in Berlin. Gotthardt keeps it alive. In 2023 the accounts showed a loss of 13.8 million euros, in 2024 a loss of 16 million. By the end of 2024 Gotthardt had put in 37.4 million euros. He told Die Zeit that the loss had not been smaller in 2025.

Against this stand about 12,000 paying subscribers, in the fourth year after the founding. Bild reports more than 700,000 digital subscribers. Reichelt says his morning show reaches “700,000, sometimes over a million people”. We cannot check that.

Roughly calculated, using the loss of 2024 and the subscribers of 2026, Gotthardt pays more than a thousand euros a year on top of every subscription. That is no assault gun. It is a pop-gun, and the billionaire pays for the powder.

III. The ammunition

What the pop-gun is loaded with is shown by a data set that the research platform Correctiv analysed in October 2026. It comes from a cyberattack on Nius and is freely available online. Correctiv is one of Reichelt’s favourite opponents, and that needs saying. The figures speak for themselves all the same.

The strongest sales month was October 2025, with 1,939 new subscriptions. That month Nius reported again and again on the business dealings of the Minister of State for Culture, Wolfram Weimer. In July 2025 the numbers grew alongside the series against the constitutional court candidate Frauke Brosius-Gersdorf. In January 2026 there were 1,064 new subscriptions, and the strongest day was 16 January with 115. That day “Nius Live” was about Daniel Günther.

On 7 January, on Markus Lanz’s talk show, Günther had named Nius as an example of media engaging in agitation instead of journalism. Lanz summed up: “We have to regulate it, censor it if necessary, and in the extreme case even ban it.” Günther answered: “Yes.” The Nius videos left out the sentence in which he immediately turned to social media for under-sixteens. The editing history in the leaked data shows that Nius first quoted Günther in full. Only later versions shortened the quote so that it sounded like a demand to ban Nius.

Fairness requires this: the “Yes” was said, and it was a mistake. When Beatrix von Storch raised the remark in a later show, Lanz rejected her account as false. Here Reichelt has a real point. Nius went to court. The Schleswig administrative court rejected the urgent application, and so did the next instance. Günther, they held, had spoken as a party politician, not as Minister-President.

The pattern is clear all the same. Subscriptions come not from research but from outrage at a person. The person changes; the powder stays the same.

IV. The charge

What the charge looks like can be heard in a conversation of a good three hours that Reichelt gave the podcaster Ben Berndt in recent weeks. First comes the pathos. The new media, he says, are “media love brands”; people love them because “we don’t take them for fools”. His motto in life is on a slip of paper: “We shall never surrender”. His Bible verse from this time is “Send me”.

Then come the claims, and they hold up no better than the Drosten story. Saladin, he says, decreed “in the 14th, 15th century” that no research may contradict the Quran. Saladin died in 1193. There is no genocide in Gaza, he says; it would be “the best-fed genocide in history”. In August 2025 a famine was officially declared in Gaza City. In September 2025 a United Nations commission of inquiry concluded that genocide was taking place. One can dispute that finding, but not pretend it does not exist.

In “The Confetti Cannons” we described Steingart’s mistake with Icarus: he made King Minos the father, when Minos was the jailer. With Reichelt it is Saladin, shifted by two centuries. Thomas Knüwer coined a German word for this, “Behauptungsjournalismus”, the journalism of assertion. The pop-gun fires the same charge as the big confetti cannons, only faster.

V. The recoil

Every gun has a recoil, and in this conversation it hits the gunner.

For three hours Reichelt complains about German “collectivism”. The left, he says, would “love to have us taken away”. He quotes a supposedly Stalinist formula of power: “Isolate, defame, liquidate.”

In the same conversation he sketches his own programme. He wants to use pro-Palestinian demonstrations as a trap: check papers, “round them up”, drive them to the airport, “including their children”. He wants naturalisations reviewed. And he wishes for “ICE and Fauda”, that is, deportation squads on the American model and suppression by intelligence and military means modelled on an Israeli television series. He recognises his opponent on the street: “I recognise my Islamist when I see him.” By the beard, by the clothes, by the pendant on the rear-view mirror.

That is the very taking-away he accuses others of. Those to be taken away are people who join a demonstration, and they are recognised by their appearance.

He should also lay the formula about isolating and defaming next to his own business model. You single out a person, sharpen the point, shorten a quote, and count the subscriptions the next morning. That is our reading of the figures, but it is the obvious one.

VI. What holds

Reichelt was a war reporter for ten years, in Afghanistan, Iraq and Syria. By his own account he was twice held by men who belonged to IS or worked with it, and narrowly escaped a third arrest. We do not deny him the courage that took.

Some of what he says is documented or at least open to debate. The long school closures during the pandemic are now widely criticised as too long. According to the research network MOTRA, in which the BKA, Germany’s federal criminal police, takes part, 45.1 percent of Muslims under 40 in Germany show attitudes with an affinity for Islamism. The measure is disputed, and Reichelt simply turns “affinity” into “Islamists”, but the figure is not invented. And his touchstone for democracy is a good one: what one says in public and what one says behind closed doors must roughly match.

Only he fails his own test. He invokes the protection of sources and, in the same breath, tells us what Friedrich Merz told him privately about the energy transition and what Markus Lanz confided to him on the phone.

VII. Gunner, keeper of the seal, maître de plaisir

In “The Titanisation of the Confetti Cannon” we assigned the roles. Steingart is the maître de plaisir; he stages and celebrates. Döpfner is the keeper of the seal; he grants or withholds approval, as we showed in “Flawless Democrats”. Reichelt is the gunner; he fires. All three circle the same company. Döpfner runs it, Springer holds 36 percent of Steingart’s Pioneer, and Reichelt learned his trade there.

What comes out of all three barrels is confetti, and not because of the noise. Confetti hits people and leaves structures standing. Reichelt rails against “blue-haired beetle conjurers” who supposedly get twelve million euros of taxpayers’ money. Where the money for his own pop-gun comes from, Nius never asks. We described it in “Plunder, Lease and Inheritance”: Gotthardt’s fortune comes from software for doctors’ practices and hospitals, and so ultimately from the contributions of the insured.

An assault gun aims at power. The pop-gun aims at whoever brings in the most subscriptions that day.
