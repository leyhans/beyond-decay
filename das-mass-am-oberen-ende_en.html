The Measure at the Top End

Where money no longer measures value, but expectation and power

At the bottom end, money says “almost nothing”. At the top end, the same measure says “immeasurable”, and it says so of things whose use nobody can put a figure on. What is measured there is no longer value, but expectation and power.

I. Thirteen Days

On 3 November 2026 Nvidia was worth about five trillion dollars on the stock market, more than any other company in the world. On 16 November it was worth about 3.8 trillion. In thirteen days the company’s value had fallen by about 1.2 trillion dollars.

In those thirteen days no assembly line stopped in any factory. No chip got worse, no customer cancelled a contract. The engineers came to work in the morning as before. All that changed was what investors expect of this company in the future.

For comparison, a figure from the bottom end: the city of Bonn funds its entire independent arts scene with about 2.3 million euros a year. With the amount by which the market value of a single company shrank in less than two weeks, this funding could be paid for more than 400,000 years. Both figures are in the same currency. Both are called “value”.

II. The Price of Expectation

In March 2026 OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT, raised 122 billion dollars from investors, the largest private funding round ever. The company was valued at 852 billion dollars. In November another round followed, now on the basis of 1.25 trillion dollars. In the same year OpenAI reported revenue of 6.7 billion dollars for the second quarter and an operating loss of 12.3 billion. The company spends almost twice what it takes in, and its value rises.

Things are no different at Anthropic, the company that develops Claude. In February 2026 it was valued at 380 billion dollars, in May at 965 billion. In the first half of 2026, according to a report in the Wall Street Journal, the company posted a net loss of more than 80 billion dollars. About 72 billion of that was not money spent but an accounting entry: financing instruments that can later turn into shares had become more valuable because the company had become more valuable. The loss thus arose from the gain in value.

On 19 November 2026 Anthropic went public, at 81.3 billion dollars the largest IPO in history. The share was issued at 83 dollars, opened at 111.50 and closed at 97.17. At the first trade the market value was above two trillion dollars; half an hour later it was about 1.26 trillion. And even for the closing price of the same day there are two figures: about 1.28 trillion if one counts the shares issued, about 1.76 trillion if one includes all the shares that may yet come into being. The same company, the same price, the same evening, and the value depends on what one counts. A measure could not show more clearly that it no longer measures anything fixed.

One may call this speculation, but the word does not quite fit. What is bought here is not a company but a promise: that a technology which burns money today will one day bring in so much that any price paid today is justified. Whether that is true, nobody knows. The measure does not measure what is, but what buyers believe other buyers will believe tomorrow.

III. Pay Without Measure

On 6 November 2025 Tesla’s shareholders approved, with 75 percent of the votes cast, a compensation package for Elon Musk that may be worth up to one trillion dollars. It is divided into twelve tranches. The full amount is paid out if within ten years Tesla reaches a market value of 8.5 trillion dollars, sells twenty million vehicles and delivers one million humanoid robots. Musk receives no salary. Before the vote the chair of the board had warned that he might leave the company if the shareholders said no.

Pay was originally the counter-value of work. Here it is something else: the price for a man staying. What his work is worth, nobody measures. What is measured is what the shareholders fear they would lose if he went.

IV. The Painting and the Player

In November 2017 a painting was auctioned in New York for 450 million dollars, more than had ever been paid for a work of art. It shows Christ as Saviour of the World and is attributed to Leonardo da Vinci. Whether Leonardo actually painted it is disputed among experts to this day. Since the sale it has not been on public view. What was paid for was not what is on the canvas, but the name that might stand beneath it, and the prestige that owning it confers.

In the summer of 2017 Paris Saint-Germain paid 222 million euros to FC Barcelona so that the Brazilian Neymar could change clubs. Since 2011 the club has belonged to a sovereign fund of the Emirate of Qatar. What a player achieves on the pitch can be counted in goals and assists. That does not explain the price. It is explained by what a state expects from being visible in football.

The American economist Thorstein Veblen described this in 1899. He called it conspicuous consumption: some things are bought not although they are expensive, but because they are expensive. The price is their purpose. It shows everyone who can pay it.

V. Where the Sums Come From

With figures of this size one has to ask where they come from. There are essentially four sources, and they are of very different kinds.

The first is expectation, and it is fictitious. A market value is not money lying anywhere. It is the last price multiplied by all the shares. The 1.2 trillion dollars by which Nvidia became poorer in thirteen days was never owned by anyone and lost by no one who did not sell. If all shareholders wanted to sell at once, this value would not exist for even a day.

The second is borrowed money, and it turns into speculation. For years Bill Hwang bet on a few stocks through his family fund Archegos with loans from large banks. Prosecutors in New York later accused him of inflating his portfolio in this way from 1.5 to 35 billion dollars. In March 2021 some of these stocks fell, the banks demanded their money back, and Hwang lost about 20 billion dollars in two days. Credit Suisse alone lost 5.5 billion. Two years later the bank no longer existed as an independent institution. The money that burned here had for the most part never been Hwang’s own.

The third is the skimming of real sources. In 2006 a consortium led by the Australian bank Macquarie bought London’s water supplier Thames Water. In the first year the company paid out a dividend of 656 million pounds on a profit of 241 million. According to the Guardian’s calculations, 2.8 billion pounds flowed to the owners over eleven years, while the company’s debts rose from 3.2 to 10.5 billion. Macquarie itself gives lower payouts and points to investment of more than a billion pounds a year. What is not in dispute is who pays in the end: the households that settle their water bills, because the water comes out of the pipe whoever owns it. How such tollbooths work we described in “How Do You Become a Billionaire?”.

The fourth source is dark. In Colombia there is a word of its own for it: testaferro, the front man in whose name houses, vehicles, companies and herds of cattle are registered that in truth belong to someone else. In April 2025 Colombia’s public prosecutor seized 56 assets from one group, among them 24 properties and 21 luxury vehicles. They were registered to people who had neither stable work nor the means to buy or maintain them. The police alone seized more than 1,300 such assets in 2025 by September. That the system reaches even its pursuers is shown by another case: former drug traffickers reported that property they had handed over to the state had ended up with front men again, with the help of prosecutors. The prosecutor’s office acknowledges the events and is investigating its own ranks.

This is not a Colombian problem. The United Nations estimates that between two and five percent of world economic output is laundered every year, 800 billion to two trillion dollars. Less than one percent of it is seized. According to the same study, most of the profits from the cocaine trade are laundered not in the growing countries but in North America and Europe. The International Monetary Fund points to a warning by US authorities: in more than 30 percent of high-value all-cash property purchases in New York and other major cities, the buyers were already suspected of money laundering or other crimes. The dark money thus lands exactly where prices rise at the top end.

What share of the great sums comes from which source, nobody knows. The measure cannot show it. A dollar from expectation, from credit, from a water bill and from a cocaine deal looks exactly the same in an account.

VI. The Vassals

None of these figures arises by itself. Behind each of them stand people who make it possible without standing at the top themselves.

At Tesla it began with a move. A court in Delaware had declared Musk’s previous compensation package invalid. Tesla then moved its legal domicile to Texas. There Musk may vote his own stake of about 15 percent on his own pay. The two big proxy advisers, ISS and Glass Lewis, recommended rejecting the package. Norway’s sovereign wealth fund, the largest in the world, voted against it. The world’s three largest fund managers, BlackRock, Vanguard and State Street, did not disclose before the vote how they would vote. In the end there was 75 percent approval, and in the hall the shareholders chanted “Elon!”.

Twenty-two banks worked on Anthropic’s IPO. They share fees of about 550 million dollars, more than for any IPO before. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley, the two lead banks, receive 113 million each. To them the share price is a matter of indifference, as long as it is high enough for the deal to happen.

The most effective vassals, however, do not know that they are vassals. For the SpaceX IPO in June 2026 the Nasdaq exchange changed its rules: very large newcomers may now be admitted to the Nasdaq-100 index after only a few weeks. Anthropic is to follow on 21 December. Index funds must buy what is in the index, at any price. Analysts estimate that such funds will take up to 30 percent of the shares offered by the end of the year. These funds hold the retirement savings of millions of people who were never asked and never will be. Their money confirms every price the market names, and it cannot say no.

On its second trading day Anthropic’s share fell by about ten percent.

VII. The Work Underneath

All these figures rest on something that appears in none of them: the work of people who know how to build things. Nvidia’s market value rests on chips that engineers designed. Anthropic’s valuation rests on models that researchers and developers built. Claude, co-author of this text, is itself a result of that work. Tesla’s valuation rests on cars assembled in factories. Without these people there would be nothing to value.

How does the measure at the top end measure them? On the day of the IPO the wealth of Anthropic’s seven founders rose to about 166 billion dollars combined. Thousands of employees became millionaires on paper. That is more than Nakamura’s 20,000 yen, but it follows the same logic. Employees share not in the value of what they built, but in the expectation that others attach to it. Their stake hangs on the share price. Whoever holds shares in his employer has an interest in the price rising, and little reason to ask what the thing he builds is being built for. In this way the measure at the top end binds to itself precisely the people without whom there would be nothing to measure.

And they are hardly organised. Doctors and lawyers have professional chambers that watch over their rules of conduct and have a say in fees. In Germany engineers have chambers almost only in the building sector. Whoever designs a chip or trains a language model negotiates alone, about his salary as about his conscience. That knowledge cooperates worldwide while each individual stands alone, we described in [Title Pending]. At the top end of the measure it shows what this costs: the people on whose skill everything rests accept the role that money assigns them, and leave it to others to decide what their work is worth.

VIII. Top and Bottom

At the bottom end stood the blue light-emitting diode, for which its inventor received 20,000 yen, and a theatre listed in the budget as a “voluntary service”. At the top end stand a trillion for a single man, 450 million for a painting of uncertain authorship, and a company whose value falls by almost a quarter in thirteen days.

That is neither chance nor the fault of particular markets. It is the same rubber band, stretched in both directions. At the bottom it contracts whatever has no price and can therefore be cut. At the top it expands whatever promises expectation, prestige or power. And because the figures at the top are so large, the figures at the bottom look like small change. To someone who reads about trillions every day, 2.3 million for a city’s culture looks like a rounding error. When savings have to be made, the rounding error goes first.

IX. What Is Measured at the Top

At the top end, money no longer measures what a thing achieves. It measures three other things: the expectation of what others will pay tomorrow; the prestige that ownership confers; and the power that follows from both. All three depend on one another. Whoever is valued at a trillion gets credit, attention and a hearing from governments. Whoever is listed as a “voluntary service” gets a notice.

The question of what a thing is worth to society thus becomes unanswerable from both ends. At the bottom, because the measure no longer shows anything there. At the top, because there it shows all manner of things, except what it claims to measure.
