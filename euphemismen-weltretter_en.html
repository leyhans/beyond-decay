The Euphemisms of the Ideological World-Savers

How to tell that a higher purpose is costing human lives

Whoever accepts the loss of human lives as the price of saving the world needs a word for those who die in the process. It is never the word “die”. That is how one recognises him, whether he speaks in the name of God, of history, of class, of race or of the planet.

I. A word

The political consultant Martin Besecke publishes on his website messages from the “subtle planes”. He receives them, as he writes, from a “Solar Hierarchy” of ascended masters. One of these messages concerns climate change and the greed that drives it. Because of them, it says, “not a few people will be taken from this planet by environmental and natural disasters, epidemics and plagues, and by wars, or, to put it correctly, sent into their astral phases. Not because 7 billion people are fundamentally too many for this planet, but because this planet first has to regenerate!”

The masters, it goes on, could end climate change “with a snap of the fingers”. They do not, because people must come to recognise things for themselves.

One can laugh at this. But the sentence has a construction, and the construction is no laughing matter. Nobody dies in it. People are “taken” and “sent”. A child that drowns in a flood, chokes in an epidemic or lies under rubble in a war is not taken away. It perishes, wretchedly.

Whoever calls that an “astral phase” has done two things. He has justified the dying, for it serves a purpose, the regeneration of the planet. And he has made sure he does not have to look at it.

II. The one pot

We are used to treating religions, sects and political ideologies separately: the first is faith, the second a worldview, the third a programme. On one point they belong in the same pot. Any of them can reach the place where it sacrifices people for a higher purpose or approves of such a sacrifice. And at that place they all speak the same language.

The test question is this: may a person who has not chosen it die for a higher purpose? Whoever answers yes has opened the door to the totalitarian, however gently he speaks otherwise.

What the purpose is called makes no difference. God, history, nation, class, planet: what matters are two properties. The purpose is so high that no single life weighs against it. And it cannot be checked. Nobody can look to see whether eternity, the end of history or the purified karma were worth the sacrifice. The higher and the less checkable the purpose, the cheaper the human being.

The cover word is the visible sign of this calculation. It appears before the first deed is done.

III. The dictionary

One can leaf through it across the centuries.

Of the conquest of Jericho the Luther Bible says: “And they carried out the ban on everything in the city with the edge of the sword, on men and women, young and old, cattle, sheep and donkeys.” Carrying out the ban: it sounds like the administration of justice. It means that the infants too were struck down. The Zurich Bible says they “consecrated to destruction” everything in the city. That too is still a word from the temple.

In July 1209 a crusader army stood before Béziers, to root out the Cathars. Asked how heretics were to be told from Catholics, the papal legate Arnaud Amalric is said to have answered: “Kill them. The Lord knows his own.” The monk Caesarius of Heisterbach recorded this a good decade later, expressly as hearsay. Whether the sentence was spoken is uncertain. What is certain is that the town was put to the sword. And the sentence has survived eight centuries because it contains the whole logic. Eternity takes over the sorting of the dead. Down here nobody needs to look any more.

On 27 December 1929 Stalin announced the “liquidation of the kulaks as a class”. To liquidate is a word from bookkeeping: it is how one winds up a business. A class has no children. The peasant families carried off in freight wagons to Siberia and Kazakhstan had them.

In October 1939 Hitler signed a letter, backdated to 1 September. It empowered doctors to grant “incurably sick persons, after the most critical assessment of their condition”, a “mercy death”. Mercy was the word for the murder of hundreds of thousands of the sick, the disabled and children. A little later, in the files of the SS, killing was called “special treatment”.

The Khmer Rouge, who ruled Cambodia from 1975 to 1979, gave their cadres a sentence about the “new people” from the cities: “To keep you is no gain, to lose you is no loss.” Not a word about killing. Only a balance sheet.

In Germany a jury of linguists chose “ethnic cleansing” as the un-word of the year in 1992, and “collateral damage” in 1999. One cleans floors. Collateral damage is damage at the edge of a thing that was actually meant. What is meant are people killed because they stood next to the target. The second word did not come from a dictatorship. It came from NATO usage in the Kosovo war. We too belong in the pot.

And finally: “taken from this planet”, “sent into their astral phases”.

Ban, eternity, liquidation, mercy death, special treatment, no loss, cleansing, collateral damage, astral phase. The purposes change: God, class, race, revolution, nation, strategy, planet. The dictionary stays.

IV. What the cover word is good for

Hardly anyone kills another person of his own accord. He has to be brought to it, in stages that are known. We described them in “The Manufacture of Killing”. One of these stages is the word. It does four things.

It protects the perpetrator from himself. Whoever “carries out the ban” strikes no one down. He executes what a higher power has decided. The guilt lies with the one who gave the order, and he is out of reach.

It protects the onlooker. The follower in the second row does not have to decide whether he agrees. He only has to accept a word. Reading about the liquidation of a class costs nothing. Reading about the child of a peasant family starving in a freight wagon costs something.

It erases the victim as a person. Whoever dies no longer has a name. He belongs to a category: heretics, class, the hereditarily ill, new people, collateral, souls with negative karma. Categories have no faces.

And it moves responsibility upwards until it disappears. In Besecke’s account it is not people who kill. The disasters do the work, and the masters who could prevent them look on, for educational reasons. Whoever agrees with this has never killed anyone. He has only agreed to a plan in which dying occurs. It is the most comfortable form of participation.

V. The counter-check

Not every weighing of lives is the logic of sacrifice. An emergency doctor who must decide after a bus crash whom to treat first is selecting, and whoever comes last may die. A country defending itself against an attack sends people to their deaths. These are tragic decisions, and whoever makes them has to bear them.

Three things distinguish them from the sacrifice for a higher purpose. The purpose is close at hand and can be checked: to save this life, to protect this city, not eternity and not the end of history. Nobody is chosen so that he dies. Death is the consequence one tries to avoid, not the means. And it can be said out loud. The emergency doctor says: “I could not save him.” He does not say he sent him into his astral phase.

Immanuel Kant put the boundary into one sentence in 1785: “Act so that you use humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end, never merely as a means.” The sacrifice for a higher purpose uses the human being merely as a means. That is its definition.

The boundary is not always sharp. That is exactly why the word is such a reliable indicator. Where an army begins to call its civilian dead “collateral damage”, it is on its way from one side to the other. And where it is fixed before an attack how many bystanders it may cost, death is no longer only a consequence. It is planned in. We described this in “The Number before the Strike”.

VI. The man before the carriage

On 15 February 1905 the poet and Socialist Revolutionary Ivan Kalyayev waited in Moscow with a bomb for the carriage of Grand Duke Sergei Alexandrovich. When it came, he saw that the Grand Duchess and the two children of his brother were sitting in it. He did not throw.

Two days later he killed the Grand Duke in the Kremlin. The coachman died too. Kalyayev was hanged. He was no saint; he was an assassin. But he had a limit, and the limit was the children.

Albert Camus made a play of it in 1949, “The Just Assassins”. In it the conspirators argue over the aborted attack. The hardest among them considers it betrayal: if the revolution costs children, then children it is. Kalyayev and his companion hold against him that a revolution which kills children is no longer the one they are fighting for.

This voice exists in every camp, and it is never the loudest. In Dostoevsky, Ivan Karamazov asks his devout brother Alyosha: would you build the edifice of human happiness if it required one single small child to be tortured to death? Alyosha says no. Ivan then returns to God his ticket to a harmony paid for with a child’s tears. And in the Gospel of Luke two disciples want to call down fire from heaven on a village that would not receive Jesus. He rebukes them.

The terrorist who does not throw, the doubter who returns the ticket, the prophet who refuses the fire: all three do the same thing. They refuse to let the child disappear behind a word. They look at it.

VII. The test

To test a doctrine one needs neither theology nor the critique of ideology. One request to whoever holds it is enough: say it with the plain word.

Not “taken”, but “dead”. Not “carry out the ban”, but “strike children down”. Not “collateral damage”, but “killed because they stood next to the target”. Not “sent into the astral phase”, but “perished wretchedly”.

Our conclusion: whoever can say the plain word and still holds his purpose worth it has at least calculated honestly, and one can argue with him. Whoever cannot say it knows that his sum does not add up. His euphemism is the admission.

The totalitarian world-savers of all camps differ in their aims. In their dictionary they do not differ. A world that is to be saved by the dying of others has stopped counting its dead. It only books them.
