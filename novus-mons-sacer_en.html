Novus Mons Sacer

The new Sacred Mount, or why the people’s walkout needs a ballot paper with the box “None of them”

The Roman plebs walked out of the city and got tribunes for it. The German plebs stays at home on election day and gets nothing. On three peoples, one fable, and the question of why those who made a law are outraged by it.

I. The Outraged

Since 17 November 2026 it has been known how the chief executive of a publishing house can be given shares worth a billion euros and have the tax on them almost entirely waived. We described the case in “Poor for Six Days”. Something has been added since: the outrage.

The general secretary of the SPD, Tim Klüssendorf, calls the case a “scandal foretold”. The chairman of the CDU’s employee wing, Dennis Radtke, says that cut-off dates and bookkeeping tricks must not be the basis for a so-called neediness. Both are right. Both belong to parties that govern together.

The rule they are outraged by is called the Verschonungsbedarfsprüfung, a means test for heirs to large business fortunes. It has been in the statute since 2016. On 20 June 2016 Wolfgang Schäuble for the CDU, Sigmar Gabriel for the SPD and Horst Seehofer for the CSU announced their agreement. Four days later the Bundestag passed the law with the votes of the CDU/CSU and the SPD, against the Greens and the Left. It was the answer to a ruling of the Federal Constitutional Court, which had found the sparing of large fortunes excessive. Last year, 3.7 of 3.9 billion euros in tax were waived under this rule in 34 cases.

So the scandal was foretold from within their own house. A Green motion to abolish the rule is before the Bundestag. The SPD has a reform concept of its own. The Chancellor rejects it, and the Bavarian premier wants to halve the tax. The coalition has the majority to change the rule. It has opted for outrage.

One can call that hypocrisy and leave it there. The more fruitful question is whom these people are actually speaking for when they say “the people of this country”.

II. Three Peoples

In German politics the word Volk, the people, denotes three different things, and whoever uses it switches between them as it suits.

The first is the people as sovereign. According to Article 20 of the Basic Law, all state authority emanates from it. It is the people of the ceremonial speech and a legal figure: it acts on one day in four years and otherwise through representatives.

The second is the voting people, those who actually turn up on that day. In the 2025 federal election that was 49.9 of 60.5 million entitled to vote. In the constituency of München-Land 88.1 percent voted, in Duisburg II 73.5 percent.

The third is the little people: the majority without wealth, without advisers and without access. In Rome it was called the plebs.

Officially, government is conducted in the name of the first. The representatives of the people are elected by the second. The third is put off. The billion of 2020 shows what that looks like in a single case: four tax offices confirmed in advance, in writing, that a man counts as needy, by way of a construction, on the day he is given a billion euros. A student who transfers 7,000 euros to her mother before applying for a state grant gets no binding ruling. The money is counted as her own.

III. The Sacred Mount

According to tradition, the Roman plebs left the city in 494 BC. It was in debt, it supplied the soldiers, and it had no say in any office. It moved to a hill outside the gates, the Mons Sacer, and stayed there. It did not plunder and it did not fight. It was absent.

The Senate sent a negotiator, Menenius Agrippa, and he told a fable. Once the limbs of the body rebelled against the belly because it did nothing and received everything. The hands no longer carried food to the mouth, the teeth no longer chewed. Then the whole body grew weak, and the limbs understood that the belly nourishes them all.

It is the oldest version of an argument still in circulation: what arrives at the top benefits everyone. It is not entirely wrong, and in “We Will Keep Inheriting” we took the family firms’ counter-calculation seriously. What is remarkable is what Livy reports about the outcome. The plebs returned, but the fable alone was not enough for that. It was given magistrates of its own, the tribunes of the plebs, who were inviolable and could intervene against any official act that wronged a plebeian. A good forty years later the law was written down, so that it was no longer known only to those who interpreted it. In 287 BC the resolutions of the plebs became binding on all citizens.

It achieved this with the only means it had. It showed that no army could be raised without it.

IV. The Walkout Nobody Misses

The German plebs walks out too. On 23 February 2025, 10.6 million eligible voters stayed at home. That is more people than voted for the second-largest party. And it was the highest turnout since reunification.

Only this walkout works the other way round. Whoever left Rome was missing from the army. Whoever does not vote in Germany is missing nowhere. The Bundestag has 630 seats, and it has them at any turnout. The salaries are the same, the majorities are the same, and by the Monday after the election the number is forgotten.

What becomes of those who are missing was studied by three researchers for the Federal Ministry of Labour. Lea Elsässer, Svenja Hense and Armin Schäfer examined around 250 policy questions from 1998 to 2013: what did respondents want, and what did the Bundestag decide in the two years that followed? For the well-off a clear relationship appeared. The more of them wanted a change, the more likely it came. For the poor none appeared. And where the two groups were clearly apart, the prospect of a change even fell when more of the poor supported it.

The researchers expressly do not say what causes this. In America it is explained by campaign donations; for Germany the mechanism has not been studied. We therefore assert none. What is established is the result: the plebs has chosen the one form of walkout that benefits the other side.

V. What Became of the Tribunes

The Roman story has a second chapter, and it is the more uncomfortable one. The plebeians won. Their wealthy families rose into the offices, married into the old houses and formed with them a new ruling class, the nobility. The tribunate, once the tool against the Senate, became a step on the way into the Senate. When the Gracchi brothers used it a century and a half later as it had been intended, they were killed.

The SPD was founded as the party of the plebs. In the Bundestag the share of workers today is three percent. 123 of the 630 members are lawyers, 76 political scientists, six master craftsmen. The agreement of 2016 bears the name of a Social Democratic vice-chancellor. Tim Klüssendorf was not in parliament then, and his anger may be real. But he is the general secretary of a governing party. A tribune outraged by a decree of the Senate casts his veto. He does not give an interview.

The same goes for Dennis Radtke. The CDU’s employee wing is the tribunate of the Union, and it has gone the same way.

VI. The Walkout That Works

There are people in Germany who have an effective walkout at their disposal. On 6 May 2025 Friedrich Merz stood for election as Chancellor. His coalition had 328 seats, 316 votes were needed, he received 310. Eighteen members of his own side had withheld their vote in a secret ballot. The election had to be repeated.

That is exactly the form the voter lacks: a vote that says no, that is counted and that has a consequence. Colombia knows it as the voto en blanco. If the blank vote wins a majority there, the election is repeated, and for single offices the same candidates may not stand again. In “The Missing Form” we described how this could be carried over to Germany: as an empty block in the chamber, marked in white, as large as the share of blank votes. The objections are set out there too, and they deserve to be taken seriously.

The proposal has been turned down twice in Berlin, both times without reasons. The Petitions Committee replied, in substance, that one could always cast an invalid ballot. That is the Senate telling the plebs it may grumble in the marketplace.

The second refusal has a date. On Sunday, 4 May 2025, the proposal went to Carsten Linnemann, general secretary of the CDU. On the Tuesday his party’s candidate for Chancellor failed because of the eighteen votes withheld in his own ranks. On the Wednesday evening the reply came from Linnemann’s office: he did not see the blank vote as “a suitable instrument for strengthening our democracy”. No reason was given. The proposal was part of a longer paper, and nobody has to discuss every paper. But the sentence was written one day after eighteen members of parliament had used exactly this instrument. For members of parliament the withheld vote is a right with consequences. For voters it is unsuitable. And the pronoun deserves a question in return: whose democracy?

The new Sacred Mount would be a box on the ballot paper. The plebs would not have to leave the city. It would have to turn up and be visibly absent.
