Two Trillion Without a Course

What the EU wants to spend its money on from 2028, and where it cuts

Brussels is fighting over the size of the next EU budget. Hardly anyone asks what the money will be spent on. We looked.

I. The fight over the sum

On 26 November 2026 the EU heads of state and government met for a special summit on the budget for 2028 to 2034. After almost fourteen hours they parted shortly after midnight without a result. Denmark, Sweden, Belgium and Austria had walked out of the rounds of talks held by Council President António Costa. The continuation planned for the next day was cancelled. The next attempt is the summit on 17 December.

The quarrel was almost entirely about one number. The Commission had proposed just under two trillion euros, Costa turned that into 1.78 trillion. Chancellor Merz called even that too much and demanded further cuts. The so-called Friends of Cohesion, above all the recipient countries in the south and east, want no cuts at all. As described in “The Perpetual Diet”, the decision must be unanimous.

The sum has been negotiated for months. What it is spent on is the question that gets lost in this fight.

II. Where the money goes

Under Costa's proposal, 42.4 percent of the budget goes to agriculture, fisheries and cohesion, that is, support for the poorer regions. 27.7 percent goes to competitiveness, security and defence, 9.5 percent to external action, 6.1 percent to administration. 8.9 percent, around 158 billion euros, flows into repaying the coronavirus debt the EU took on jointly in 2020.

In the current budget for 2021 to 2027, the share of agriculture and cohesion was still 59.1 percent. So the shift is real. Compared with the current budget, the funds for competitiveness rise by 120 percent according to Costa, those for defence by 232 percent. Anyone defending the proposal starts with these figures, and they are not wrong.

III. Where the cuts fall

More revealing is where Costa saves compared with the Commission. From competitiveness, prosperity and security he takes around 95.5 billion euros, about 16 percent. From the defence industry, security and space it is almost 30 percent. Agriculture, cohesion and related tasks, by contrast, lose around 54 billion, about 5 percent. External action loses 21 percent.

This contains a contradiction no one in Berlin says out loud. The six net payers who call themselves “modernisers”, Germany, Austria, the Netherlands, Denmark, Sweden and Finland, demand two things: a budget several hundred billion smaller, and a focus on competitiveness, security and defence. The two do not go together. For agricultural policy, 300 billion euros are protected by law. Cohesion is defended by its friends with their veto. Whoever pushes the total down under unanimity inevitably pushes it down where no blocking minority stands. That is the future.

So the net payers get what they demand and thereby lose what they claim to want. The cuts fall on competitiveness not despite their demand but because of it.

IV. What is in the Competitiveness Fund

The centrepiece of the Commission is a new European Competitiveness Fund with 409 billion euros for seven years. The number is bigger than it sounds. It includes the research programme Horizon Europe with 175 billion. That leaves around 234 billion for the fund itself, spread over four so-called windows: 131 billion for defence, security and space, i.e. more than half, 51.5 billion for digital technology, 26.2 billion for clean technology and the decarbonisation of industry, 20.4 billion for health, biotech and agriculture.

One has to picture these magnitudes. The window for clean industry has 26.2 billion for seven years, less than four billion a year for the whole Union. The subsidy that Germany pledged in 2023 for a single Intel chip factory in Magdeburg alone came to 9.9 billion euros. The factory was never built. And for repaying the coronavirus debt the budget plans six times as much as for the entire conversion of European industry. These are the Commission's figures, before Costa's cuts.

V. What Draghi demanded

In September 2024 Mario Draghi called for additional investment of 750 to 800 billion euros a year to close the gap with the US and China. A year later he raised the stakes: the European Central Bank now estimates the annual investment need for 2025 to 2031 at almost 1.2 trillion euros. The public share has nearly doubled, from 24 to 43 percent, because defence is paid mainly from public funds.

Against this, Costa's proposal sets around 70 billion euros a year for competitiveness, security and defence combined. The Brussels think tank Bruegel has calculated that even the larger Commission draft could at best close 12 to 22 percent of the investment gap in European public goods, and 4 to 8 percent of total needs. Draghi himself warned in 2025 that even doubling the research budget is of no use unless the money is concentrated on a few large projects.

In fairness one must say: no EU budget of just over one percent of economic output can cover the need Draghi describes. Most of it would have to come from the member states and from private investors. But precisely for that reason, the small common part ought to go where only Europe together can achieve something. That is exactly where the savings fall.

VI. Who gets the money

That leaves the question we asked in “The Patient Who Does Not Know His Disease”: who decides where the money goes?

In January 2026 the economist Daniel Gros examined for the European Parliament's Committee on Budgets how the previous competitiveness programmes had worked. His finding: the research programmes are shaped by industry associations and national champions. These favour incremental improvements and hardly support anything that challenges their own business model. Europe is strong in medium technologies such as mechanical engineering and cars, but barely present in software and artificial intelligence, and only a small part of the programmes targets them. Gros recommends shielding the work programmes from the influence of incumbents and creating more instruments that also fund single applicants with disruptive ideas.

That is exactly the point at issue here. If the incumbents help decide the funding for the new, the new is selected by the standards of the old. As far as is known so far, the Competitiveness Fund changes little about this: it gathers fourteen existing programmes under one roof, but the roof does not protect against who sits beneath it.

With the largest item it is even clearer. Agricultural and cohesion funds are to flow in future through national and regional partnership plans, one plan per member state. The plans are agreed with the Commission but implemented by the governments. Whoever distributes the funding pots in the capitals today will distribute them then as well.

VII. What must be said in fairness

The farmers have their own arithmetic. Their association Copa-Cogeca points out that the protected agricultural budget is to fall from 387 to 300 billion euros, by its own calculation by almost a quarter, and that food security is also security. That is not merely defending vested interests. Anyone who has lived through the war in Ukraine and the dependence on wheat, fertiliser and energy cannot simply dismiss the argument. Cohesion has brought countries such as Poland closer to the West within two decades.

And the shift in favour of the future is real, only too small. The problem is not that nothing moves. The problem is that the weights shift as slowly as unanimity allows, while the world shifts as fast as competition demands.

VIII. The till and the helm

In “More Speed, Same Course” we wrote about the German budget that the finance minister sits at the till and not at the helm. In Brussels it is the same, only with twenty-seven cashiers. Each defends his share, and each can block everyone else. The course, that is, what Europe should be able to do in seven years that it cannot do today, is not negotiated at all. It results from whatever is left over after the vested interests.

What the money is spent on is not a technical question. It is the only political question this budget raises. On 17 December it will again not be asked.
