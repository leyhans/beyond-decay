Wokeness – Aberration or the Attempt at an Objective Historiography

For some decades now, history has no longer been told only from the point of view of the victors. The excesses of wokeness have given those who accept only their own narrative the material to ridicule every such attempt. Whoever wants to keep the two apart needs a standard that applies to all sides.

I. The Cave

In November 2026 the German public broadcaster ZDF began the series “Terra X: Die Geschichte der Deutschen” (The History of the Germans). In the first shot the narrator, the actor Matthias Brandt, stands in an Ice Age cave among hunters and gatherers, and all of them except him are dark-skinned. That is the state of research. The first modern humans who reached the territory of present-day Germany some 45,000 years ago came from Africa. The lighter skin of Europeans is considerably younger and goes back in good part to immigrants from Anatolia who brought agriculture with them. The series sets out to tell history not as a sequence of kings and battles, but from the lives of ordinary people.

The mockery came promptly. A right-wing blog headlined its review “The past as Moorish migration”. It is worth noting where it took aim. It offered nothing against the findings of genetic research. It attacked the accompanying texts. A news agency report on the series had said that the “so-called bio-German” was anything but blond and fair-skinned. That is a present-day fighting word, and it had no business in a text about the Ice Age. A single superfluous word was enough to dismiss a carefully made series as ideology.

This small case shows the whole problem. The research is sound. A provocation supplies the ammunition. The mockery then hits not the provocation but the cause.

II. An Attempt That Is Older

History from below is not an invention of wokeness. In 1978 the Swedish writer Sven Lindqvist published a handbook entitled “Gräv där du står”, “Dig Where You Stand”. He urged workers to research the history of their own workplace instead of letting company chronicles tell it to them. In West Germany, in the late seventies and above all in the eighties, numerous history workshops arose in which laypeople and historians together investigated the history of their neighbourhood, their factory, their neighbours under National Socialism. Everyday history, oral history and regional history became recognised branches of scholarship.

The political recognition of crimes long passed over in silence likewise follows not the spirit of the age but the files. Between 1904 and 1908 the German Empire had tens of thousands of Herero and Nama killed in what is today Namibia. As late as 2012 the Bundestag rejected a motion to call this genocide. In May 2021 the federal government recognised the genocide. The facts had not changed in those nine years. What had changed was the willingness to state them. And even this recognition was cautiously worded: the events had been genocide “from today’s perspective”, said the foreign minister. Critics saw this as an attempt to avoid legal claims to compensation.

The attempt to write history from the point of view of the victims as well is therefore decades older than the word “woke”. It has methods, archives and results. Wokeness attached itself to it, and burdened it with excesses.

III. The Prosecutor

No German author has represented the victims’ point of view as consistently as Karlheinz Deschner. Between 1986 and 2013 the ten volumes of his “Kriminalgeschichte des Christentums” (Criminal History of Christianity) appeared, a collection of the crimes committed in the name of Christianity, supported by thousands of source references. Deschner never claimed to be neutral. In the introduction to the first volume, referring to Cicero’s demand that the historian write neither out of favour nor out of enmity, he writes: “I write ‘out of enmity’! For the history of those I describe has made me their enemy.” And he adds: “I am refuted only if what I wrote is false.”

That is more honest than a church history that passes off its partisanship as neutral presentation. In the Catholic “Handbuch der Kirchengeschichte” (Handbook of Church History), Charlemagne’s Saxon wars appear under the heading “The Rounding-Off of the Frankish Empire”. Deschner takes the same wars apart and heads a chapter “The Butcher of the Saxons”.

Deschner also named the criterion by which he should be measured: “Our one-sidedness is not what matters. What matters is that we admit it; not that we feign a mendacious ‘objectivity’ […] What is decisive is how many and how good the reasons are that underpin our ‘one-sidedness’, what relevance the source base has […]”. And he takes apart historiography’s favourite excuse, that everything must be understood “from the spirit of the time”. He calls it a “gesture of appeasement, trivialisation and belittlement” and quotes Goethe: “What you call the spirit of the times / Is at bottom the gentlemen’s own spirit.”

Yet precisely by his own standard one can show where the prosecutor reaches his limit. In his chapter on the Saxon wars Deschner quotes Alcuin, Charlemagne’s most important adviser, as a cheerleader for the war and as a critic of the ruthless collection of the tithe. The sentence with which Alcuin rejected forced baptism in 796, that a man can be forced to baptism but not to faith, he mentions only in a footnote. Yet this very sentence is the strongest evidence against the excuse of the spirit of the time. It shows that at the perpetrator’s own court people knew better. The prosecutor collects what incriminates. The voice that would have made the verdict even heavier, because it came from within the accused’s own camp, did not fit his collection.

A prosecutor is necessary. Without him the defence is left alone in the courtroom. But a prosecutor must not pronounce the verdict.

IV. The Aberration

On 23 June 2020 demonstrators in Madison, Wisconsin, pulled two statues from their pedestals in front of the state capitol. One of them depicted Hans Christian Heg, a Norwegian immigrant who had been active in the movement against slavery and had fallen in 1863 as a colonel of the Union army fighting the Confederacy. The demonstrators beheaded the statue and threw it into a lake. They later said the monuments stood for the false narrative that Wisconsin was on the side of Black people. A descendant of Heg called the act thoughtless: his ancestor had been a committed opponent of slavery and would have supported the movement in whose name he was toppled. Whoever brought this man down from his pedestal had not looked at the sources. He did not need to.

More subtle is the case of the “1619 Project”, with which the New York Times set out in 2019 to retell the history of the United States from the standpoint of slavery. The concern was legitimate. But the lead essay stated that the colonists had fought the War of Independence in part to protect slavery. Five of the country’s most respected historians, among them Gordon Wood and James McPherson, called this claim “not true” in a letter to the paper. Wood wrote a sentence that serves the whole subject: “We all want justice, but not at the expense of truth.” The paper at first refused any correction. Only in March 2020, after one of the historians who had checked the text in advance had stated publicly that she had warned against precisely this passage, did the paper change the sentence. Since then it says that protecting slavery was a primary motive of “some of” the colonists.

In both cases conviction replaced the source. In the first case crudely, in the second with footnotes. The result was the same: those who wanted to make the victims of slavery visible supplied those who did not want to talk about slavery with proof for their claim that it was all just ideology.

V. The Counter-Aberration

The aberration has an opposite side, and it is no less harmful. In July 2023 Florida’s Board of Education adopted new standards for African American history. They state that students should learn how slaves developed skills which, “in some instances, could be applied for their personal benefit”. This was adopted in the course of the governor’s “anti-woke” policies.

In March 2025 the American president signed an executive order entitled “Restoring Truth and Sanity to American History”. It states that over the past decade Americans had witnessed an effort to rewrite their history, “replacing objective facts with a distorted narrative driven by ideology rather than truth”. There followed a review of the exhibitions of the Smithsonian, the country’s largest group of museums, to “assess tone, historical framing, and alignment with American ideals”. In July 2026 a second order directed that signs be put up in the National Museum of American History informing visitors that the exhibits should be renovated and directing them to “accurate information”.

Here the word “objective” is used by a government to lay down what museums may say about history. That is exactly what it accuses the other side of, only with more power.

This too is not new. When in the 1930s Alfred Rosenberg condemned Charlemagne as a “butcher of the Saxons” for having killed “Germanic” people, church voices denied the massacre of Verden. In 1935 the church newsletter of the diocese of Osnabrück spoke of the “fairy tale of the ‘Verden blood court’”, and in 1936 a Protestant church historian declared the word for “behead” in the sources to be a copying error. Two camps bent the same event in opposite directions. The sources remained as they were.

VI. A Street Sign

How difficult it is to distinguish correction from aberration is shown by a Berlin street sign. On 23 August 2025, after years of litigation, the Mohrenstraße (“Moors’ Street”) in Berlin-Mitte was renamed Anton-Wilhelm-Amo-Straße. Amo, born around 1703 in present-day Ghana, was the first known philosopher of African origin at a German university. In the reporting, the uncertain tradition that he had been abducted to Germany as a child appeared as fact.

A month later the historian of slavery Michael Zeuske published two documents from the Dutch National Archives. He read them as showing that Amo came to Europe not as a slave but as a member of the elite of an African community that itself held slaves. Other historians disputed that the most important of these documents, one from 1706, refers to Amo at all. Specialists agree that Amo was not a slave. How he came to Europe is disputed.

Within days right-wing media wrote that Amo may have been a “slave owner”, and elsewhere there was talk of a “historical flop”. The documents do not support that. The supporters of the renaming, for their part, had spread a life story that was not secure even before. Both sides knew more than the sources said. The only ones who kept to the sources were the historians who argued with each other. This is exactly what the attempt at an objective historiography looks like: not as a result, but as an open dispute over evidence.

VII. How the Aberration Becomes Ammunition

The statue in Madison, the untenable sentence in the New York Times, the “bio-German” in the accompanying text of a television series: each of these cases is small in itself. Together they make an arsenal. Whoever wants to accept history only from the victors’ point of view needs no arguments against the history workshops, against the recognition of the genocide of the Herero or against genetic research. He needs only the excesses. He points to the beheaded abolitionist, and everyone laughs at the woke. That in the same breath a curriculum attests personal benefit to slaves is lost in the laughter.

That is the real damage wokeness has done. Not its concerns, which are often legitimate, but its carelessness with sources has supplied the opponents of every reassessment with what they could never have produced themselves: the credibility of mockery. In the end the mockery hits not the aberration but the attempt.

VIII. The Court

If neither the conviction of the one side nor the conviction of the other decides, what does? Deschner gave the answer himself, even if he did not always keep to it: the quality of the reasons and the relevance of the source base. From this a standard can be made that applies to all sides.

A reassessment is a correction if it rests on sources that were previously passed over, and if it also admits the sources that speak against it. It becomes an aberration if it puts conviction in the place of the source. That applies to whoever topples a statue and to whoever writes a curriculum.

What this looks like in practice we attempted in our essay on Charlemagne. We took the Royal Frankish Annals seriously, the court historiography of the victor, and read them from the point of view of the defeated. We did not adopt the number of Verden without naming the dispute over it. We recorded that according to the Annals the men of Verden were executed as rebels, not because they refused baptism, and based the charge of forced baptism on the law that documents it. And we called the witness whom the prosecutor had banished to a footnote: Alcuin, who wrote to his king that people can be drawn to faith but not forced.

A court is not neutral in the sense of indifferent. Deschner was right when he wrote that whoever views history without any compassion is like someone who registers the victims of a great fire without feeling. But the court hears both sides, and it measures them by the same evidence. The prosecutor may be angry. The court may not.

IX. An Attempt That Is Never Finished

The historian Johann Gustav Droysen mocked the belief in a perfectly objective historiography with the sentence: “Only the thoughtless are objective.” Deschner liked to quote him. He is right if one understands objectivity as a state that one reaches and then possesses. No one writes history from nowhere.

But objectivity is not a state, it is a direction. It consists in letting oneself be contradicted by the sources again and again, including those that harm one’s own cause. The history workshops, archaeogenetics, the historians arguing over a document from 1706 move in this direction. The statue-topplers and the authors of decrees about “objective facts” move in the opposite one, under whatever flag.

Wokeness is therefore neither the objective historiography its adherents take it for, nor the mere aberration its opponents make it out to be. It is part of an older and legitimate attempt that its carelessness has made vulnerable. The attempt itself remains right. It will only be taken seriously if it keeps to the rules it demands of others.
