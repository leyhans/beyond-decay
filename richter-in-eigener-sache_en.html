Judges in Their Own Cause

How the Bundestag Decides on Its Own Composition

Karlsruhe has rejected the BSW’s challenge to the 2025 Bundestag election, and rightly so. The problem lies not in this ruling but with those who decided before it.

I. The ruling

On 18 November 2026 the Federal Constitutional Court dismissed the BSW’s electoral scrutiny complaint as inadmissible. The party had won 4.981 per cent in the election of February 2025 and missed the Bundestag by 9,529 votes. It considered it possible that its votes had been confused with those of the small party “Bündnis Deutschland” and that votes had wrongly been declared invalid, and it demanded a nationwide recount.

The court ruled unanimously that suspicions are not enough, not even many suspicions. Anyone who alleges an electoral error must set out concrete facts; otherwise every election could be called into question “by means of speculative considerations”. That the result was close changes nothing. Different results in different polling districts, the court said, are “a characteristic and consequence of free elections”.

There is nothing to object to in this. We follow the same rule: where there is no evidence, we do not speculate. Whether the BSW is missing votes, we do not know. Nor is that the question here.

II. Who decides first

Before an election reaches the Federal Constitutional Court, the Bundestag examines it itself. That is what Article 41 of the Basic Law provides. Objections go to the Committee for the Scrutiny of Elections, whose members are appointed according to the strength of the parliamentary groups, and the plenary decides. Only against that decision can one go to Karlsruhe.

In the BSW case this meant that the CDU/CSU and SPD had the majority in the committee. Had the BSW entered parliament after the fact, the coalition would have lost its majority of twelve votes. Around 30 members of parliament owe their seats to the fact that the BSW stayed out, among them, according to a CSU politician, the President of the Bundestag, Julia Klöckner. On 18 December 2025 the Bundestag rejected the recount. Only the AfD had spoken in favour of it.

What a recount could have set off is easy to calculate. Had the BSW cleared the threshold, the 630 seats would have been redistributed. Two independent calculations arrive at 34 seats for the BSW. All the other parties would have had to give up seats, the AfD, the Greens and the Left included. The CDU/CSU and SPD would then have had 310 seats, while a majority requires 316. Merz would have remained Chancellor. But for every law the coalition would have had to look for votes outside its own ranks. Otherwise it would have had to take in the Greens as a third partner, carry on as a minority government or call new elections. Whether the laws passed up to that point would have stood would have been a legal question of its own, one that now nobody has to answer. And on 30 January 2027 the Federal Convention elects the successor to Federal President Steinmeier. It consists of all 630 members of the Bundestag and an equal number of delegates from the Länder. A different Bundestag would also have meant a different Federal Convention.

Imagine a football match after whose final whistle the winning team votes on whether the opponents’ disputed goal should have counted. Or a board that audits its own annual accounts before an auditor may see them. Perhaps both decide correctly. But nobody can believe them, and in an election that is what matters. The legal officer of the SPD parliamentary group, Johannes Fechner, declared: “There were no electoral errors, there were no counting errors that could justify a recount.” Counting errors there had certainly been. Recounts in some districts found votes that had not been credited to the BSW, and between the provisional and the official final result its shortfall shrank from just over 13,000 to 9,529 votes. Whether these errors justify a recount is a matter on which one can differ. But Fechner is one of those who voted on the recount, and part of the majority that would have lost it.

III. A legacy of the Empire

The rule is older than the Federal Republic. The Imperial Constitution of 1871 laid down that the Reichstag itself decided on the validity of the election of its members. As early as 1888 the constitutional lawyers Georg Jellinek and Max von Seydel demanded that judges should examine elections.

The Weimar Republic took a step in that direction. Under Article 31 of its constitution, elections were reviewed by an electoral court attached to the Reichstag, composed of members of parliament and judges of the Reich Administrative Court. In 1949 the Basic Law took that step back. Since then parliament has once again examined itself first, and only then does a court come in.

IV. Time

There is no deadline for the Bundestag’s decision. The scrutiny committee of the 21st Bundestag was only elected in June 2025, three months after parliament first met. It decided on the first objections to the federal election in September 2025. In August 2025 and in July 2026 the Federal Constitutional Court noted that it was not readily apparent why the Bundestag had not taken the necessary steps immediately after it first convened. It saw no compelling reason for this delay. Fifteen months after the election, only 450 of around 1,000 objections had been dealt with. The previous Bundestag had decided on about 2,000 after 14 months.

For the BSW the arithmetic is this: the election was in February 2025, the Bundestag decided after ten months, Karlsruhe after a further eleven. Of the four years of the legislative term, almost 21 months had passed before it was settled whether parliament was correctly composed.

This is not an isolated case. In the federal election of September 2021 so much went wrong in Berlin that the vote had to be partly repeated. In November 2022 the Bundestag, with the votes of the traffic-light coalition, ordered a repeat in 431 polling districts. In December 2023 the Federal Constitutional Court extended it to 455. The repeat vote was held on 11 February 2024, two years and four and a half months after the election it was meant to repair.

Austria shows that it can be done differently. There the Constitutional Court reviews elections directly. The run-off for the office of Federal President took place on 22 May 2016. On 1 July 2016, after several days of public hearings, the court annulled it and ordered it to be repeated. It needed less than six weeks.

V. What the observers say

The OSCE’s election observers have criticised the procedure more than once. After the 2017 election they wrote that the legal framework did not provide a time frame for deciding post-election complaints, and added that a system in which the newly elected Bundestag reviews the lawfulness of the election of its own members may raise questions of conflict of interest.

In its report on the 2025 election the OSCE is more explicit. A few interlocutors, it says, “pointed at the potential conflict of interests in parliamentarians deciding upon the validity of their own mandate.” The committee deliberates in closed meetings, its minutes are not publicly available, and oral hearings rarely take place. Over the whole previous legislative term it met in public only twice. There are no deadlines either at the Bundestag or at the Federal Constitutional Court. The mission was told that an investigation in the Bundestag could take between six months and a year, and that Karlsruhe would decide by the second year after the complaint was submitted. The OSCE’s verdict: “The lack of clear deadlines does not ensure effective remedy, contrary to international standards.” It recommends judicial review with public hearings at all stages of the electoral process and timely deadlines for filing and adjudicating appeals.

The report appeared in June 2025. Its forecast has come true: the BSW lodged its objection within the two-month period after the election, and it was decided in the second year afterwards.

German scholars see it the same way. The Leipzig constitutional lawyer Christoph Degenhart calls the procedure “dysfunctional in several respects”; even in the case of serious electoral errors, timely legal protection is not guaranteed. The Chemnitz political scientist Eckhard Jesse speaks of an “untenable state of affairs” and says the procedure must be changed, regardless of the BSW’s objection.

VI. What follows

Our conclusion: the procedure cannot create trust, even when it reaches the right result. If the Bundestag rejects a recount, it looks like self-protection. If it orders one, it may look like tactics. Today’s ruling is soundly reasoned, but it came almost 21 months after the election, and before it the decision lay with those whose seats were at stake.

The obvious reform would be an independent electoral court with fixed deadlines, such as existed in Weimar and is customary in Austria. So far no majority has wanted to change this. The reason is simple: the majority that would have to change it is the same one that today decides on its own composition. In “The New Nomenklatura and the Party Cartel” we described how the established parties administer access to power. In the scrutiny of elections they also administer the review of their own election.
