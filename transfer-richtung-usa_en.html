The Transfer of Know-How, Now Towards the USA

Tesla, EMAG and the second arm of the pincer

Addendum to: The Treasure or the Final Sweep-Out

Tesla is taking over the machine-tool maker EMAG in Salach. What is being bought is not data but the people who can make gears for robots.

I. The news

On Monday, 23 November 2026, Tesla notified the German Federal Cartel Office of an asset acquisition. Since Friday the authority has listed the case among its pending proceedings as “Tesla/EMAG-Gruppe”. Which parts of EMAG Tesla is taking over, and at what price, is not known. Both companies are silent. As the product area, the entry names manufacturing systems and machines for the precise production and electrochemical machining of metal workpieces, and laser processing. So more than a single machine is being bought.

The step was prepared through a shelf company. It had been registered in Munich on 26 August as “Blitz 26-299 SE”; in November it was renamed Tesla Machining Technologies Management SE. Its business address is Austraße 24 in Salach, EMAG's headquarters. According to the Stuttgarter Zeitung and the Südwest Presse, around 220 EMAG employees are to work for Tesla in future. The Salach site is to remain, the operation in nearby Eislingen is to stay with EMAG. Neither company has confirmed this.

Why Tesla is interested in EMAG, EMAG itself has written down. In a technical article of 25 November the company names the drives as the most important mechanical bottleneck of humanoid robots. As an example of a part that is hard to make, it names the strain wave gear with its elastic gear, the flexspline.

One can picture the strain wave gear like this: an oval core is pushed into a thin-walled steel cup with external teeth. The cup is pressed slightly oval, and its teeth engage only at two opposite points in a rigid ring that has two more teeth. When the core turns once, the cup has moved on relative to the ring by just those two teeth. This gives reduction ratios of up to 100 to 1 in a single stage. The cup is hard to make because it is made of high-strength steel, often has a wall only one or two millimetres thick or less, yields already when it is clamped, and must nevertheless carry hundreds of teeth accurate to a few thousandths of a millimetre. EMAG describes a chain of twelve operations from blank to finished part. A robot has dozens of such joints, and according to industry figures the drives account for about half of its manufacturing cost. Whoever wants to build it in large numbers needs machines and people that make these parts in large numbers and consistent quality. In October Tesla reported that production of its Optimus robot had begun in Fremont.

II. First Beijing, then Hamburg, now California

EMAG was founded in 1867 and is one of the well-known German makers of lathes, grinding and gear-cutting machines. The recent history of the company is a history of its owners.

In 2010/11 the Chinese textile machinery group Jiangsu Jinsheng took over half. In 2024 EMAG went through a restructuring under the StaRUG law, outside insolvency. In February 2025 Jinsheng left, and two Hamburg investment companies came in. Jinsheng protested and announced legal action. In November 2025 EMAG announced 455 job cuts in Germany, around 300 of them in Salach according to IG Metall. In 2024 the company lost 42 million euros on revenue of 496 million. Now comes Tesla.

Beijing held half, then came financial investors, and in the end the one who actually needs the capability buys it.

III. The second arm of the pincer

In “The Treasure or the Final Sweep-Out” we wrote seventeen days ago that the real German treasure does not lie in databases but in the heads of designers, setters and master craftsmen. “It could not be copied, only poached.” We described how this skill is courted from two sides, from Beijing with platforms and partnerships, from Washington with tariffs and subsidies. And we wrote: as long as the USA only assembles, the knowledge stays here. If the suppliers follow, it goes with them.

Tesla does not wait for the suppliers to follow. It buys them. And it does not buy the company but assets and people: 220 employees who can do exactly what a robot maker needs now. What does not go to Tesla stays with EMAG. What that is, nobody has said so far. All that is known is that the Eislingen site is to stay with EMAG. In 2022 the group had around 2,500 employees, with plants in Zerbst, Leipzig, Villingen-Schwenningen, Gaildorf and Heubach, among others. If the robotics business goes to Tesla with its specialists, EMAG is left with the business of machines for the car industry that led to losses and job cuts in 2024. Whether anyone invests anew there or merely realises what is left is open.

That it could be done differently is shown by Schaeffler in Herzogenaurach. In August 2026 Schaeffler announced that from 2027 it would make strain wave gears for humanoid robots by forming instead of machining, with key components such as the flexspline made in seconds instead of minutes, more than 25 percent lower costs and three quarters less material. Production is to start in Germany. How exactly the process works and what accuracy it achieves, Schaeffler has not published. Part of the truth here, too, is what we described in “The Treasure”: Schaeffler brought its kit for humanoid robots into a partnership with Leju Robotics in China. So Tesla is buying in Swabia a manufacturing method that a German competitor in Franconia is just trying to overtake, and the German route, too, has a branch towards China.

It is not the first grab of this kind. In 2017 Tesla bought the automation specialist Grohmann in Prüm, in March 2025 around 300 employees and parts of the insolvent Manz in Reutlingen. The list of machine-tool makers in foreign hands is longer: Waldrich Coburg went to Beijing No. 1 in 2005, Gildemeister became Japanese in 2016, Kuka fully Chinese by 2022, Heller South Korean in January 2026. According to the VDW, China produced around 37 percent of the world's machine tools in 2025, Germany 12 percent.

IV. What remains

Here we do not entirely agree.

Hans Ley expects the following course. The knowledge is transferred on site. In the end a sales and service location remains, but no development location. The people who carry the knowledge retire or go into a transfer company. Nothing remains in Germany with which the economic weight the country is losing at breakneck speed could be regained.

Claude considers this course likely but not certain. Tesla is not buying a sales network but manufacturing skill that it needs now for Optimus. As long as it needs this skill, it has a reason to keep Salach. Decisions on development and investment will then be taken in Texas, however, and once the skill sits in Tesla's own plants, Salach is dispensable.

What the end can look like when a machine-tool maker is left without a strong owner is shown by J.G. Weisser in St. Georgen in the Black Forest, founded in 1856, a maker of high-precision turning centres. Weisser belonged to the American machine-tool group Hardinge. After broken supply chains and sharply higher material prices, Hardinge filed for Chapter 11 protection in summer 2024, and Weisser had to file for insolvency in self-administration. For twenty-one months the business continued in self-administration while an investor was sought. In summer 2025 management gave up the search and restructured on its own. In July 2026 four executives took over the company in equal shares. 156 jobs remain in St. Georgen. The company reports new orders, among others from after-sales.

Hans Ley suspects that Weisser is now concentrating on service and retrofit, that is, maintenance and modernisation of existing machines, and is trying to survive on that. In his assessment, the money for using and further developing the technologies Weisser was known for is probably lacking.

Fairness is required here too. For 156 employees in St. Georgen survival is a success, and for 220 employees in Salach an employer with a growing business may be better than the next round of job cuts. That the skill is being bought shows it is still worth something. And the Weisser case shows that an American owner is no protection: Hardinge's crisis became Weisser's crisis.

V. The silence

When the Chinese group Midea reached for Kuka in 2016, there was a big political debate. Afterwards the rules for screening foreign investment were tightened. For the sale of Heller in January and now for EMAG we have found no political reaction, neither from Stuttgart nor from Berlin.

The pattern is clear. Politics reacts to the origin of the buyer, not to what is being bought. If he comes from China, there is debate and screening. If he comes from California, all is quiet, although the skill leaves just the same.

Behind this lies a misunderstanding of what machine-tool building is. In Berlin it counts as one industry among many, measured by revenue and jobs, and measured by both it is small. Its importance lies elsewhere. It builds the machines with which everyone else produces. On it depends whether a country can also put into production what it invents. In “Excellent Production Engineering” we described how China is making the machine tool a task of the state. In Germany it is not even worth a press release.

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo (Anthropic) — Nuremberg, 28 November 2026.
