The Rubber Yardstick

How the arbitrariness of the measure devalues the question of value

Money is the only measure that counts everywhere. It measures everything in a single unit, changes what it measures, and stretches itself at will. It cannot answer the question of what a thing is worth to society. That is why hardly anyone asks it any more.

I. Water and Diamonds

Adam Smith described the riddle in 1776 in a single passage. Nothing is more useful than water, yet scarcely anything can be had in exchange for it. A diamond has scarcely any use, yet a great quantity of other goods may be had in exchange for it. Smith therefore distinguished two meanings of the word value: value in use, what a thing does for life, and value in exchange, what one gets for it on the market.

Ninety years later Karl Marx made this distinction the starting point of his main work. His question was what a piece of work is worth to society and who receives its yield. One need not share Marx’s answers to see that the question has remained. It arises anew with every new technology, every new kind of work, every budget.

Money answers only the second half of the question. It measures exchange value, and very precisely. It does not measure use value. As long as there were other measures, that was no problem. Religion, rank, craftsman’s honour, duty and decency each said in their own way what something was worth. Today only one measure counts everywhere, and it measures only one thing. Oscar Wilde described the human being this produces in 1892 in a single line: a man who knows the price of everything and the value of nothing.

II. The Rubber Yardstick

A yardstick that stretches is no measure. Whoever measures a table with it gets a different result every year, although the table has stayed the same.

Money behaves exactly like that. Most money today is not created in a printing works but when a bank grants a loan. This is no conspiracy theory; the Bank of England explained it itself in its Quarterly Bulletin in 2014. How much money there is and what a euro is worth is thus decided by central banks, commercial banks and markets, year after year. Whoever measures in euros measures with a yardstick that others adjust.

A figure from Bonn shows how this works in everyday life. For twenty years the city has spent the same amount on its independent arts scene. On paper nothing has been cut. Taking the inflation of those twenty years into account, the funding today is worth only about half as much. Nobody passed a resolution, nobody had to justify anything. The yardstick stretched, and the cut took care of itself.

III. The Price of Being Late

Money is not only a measure but also a motive, for many the only one they take seriously. That as a motive it crowds out what was there before was shown by an experiment in Israel.

In Haifa, parents regularly picked up their children late from day care. Two economists, Uri Gneezy and Aldo Rustichini, had some day-care centres introduce a fine for lateness. The result was the opposite of what was expected: markedly more parents came late. Before, they had felt guilty towards the staff who had to wait for them. Now they had a price, and they paid it. When the fine was abolished again after some weeks, the lateness stayed. The guilty conscience did not come back. The study appeared in 2000 under the title “A Fine is a Price”.

The British social researcher Richard Titmuss had described the same pattern as early as 1970 in blood donation. Where blood is paid for, unpaid donation declines. Whoever gives for money gives for a different reason, and whoever gave out of a sense of duty no longer feels addressed once there is a price.

So money does not merely measure wrongly. It changes what it measures.

IV. One Piece of Work, Three Measures

In November 2026, authors from five countries are writing together, in public, for two weeks in the centre of Bonn about a divided time. Anyone who wishes can come in, for ten minutes or a whole afternoon, talk with them, sit at the lunch table. Admission is free; a donation is requested. What this project is worth to a city, no till measures.

The ensemble that stages it is an independent theatre ensemble from Bonn. The same work is currently being assessed by three bodies, and each measures differently.

The state of North Rhine-Westphalia has an expert jury judge. It counts the ensemble among the outstanding independent theatre ensembles in the state. When the ministry in 2025 cut its so-called top-tier funding from eight ensembles to four, this ensemble was one of the four that remained.

The European Union finances the ensemble’s collaboration with an academy of culture in Riga, a national theatre in the Veneto and a university in Vilnius. Its programmes require a contribution of one’s own; the EU pays at most sixty to eighty percent.

The city of Bonn, where this work is made, imposed a budget freeze in September 2026. The expected deficit has risen to 167.5 million euros. In the emergency budget for 2027 the “voluntary services” are no longer to be paid out. They include all funding for independent culture.

The word “voluntary” answers the question of value before anyone has asked it. In budget law, voluntary is everything no statute prescribes. What a thing achieves plays no part in this classification.

The figures make it even clearer. All of the city’s grants to independent culture together amount in 2026 to about 2.3 million euros, roughly one per mille of the city budget. If they were cut completely, not even one and a half percent of the deficit would be covered. So what is cut is not what costs a lot, but what is easiest to cut. And because the state, the federal government and the EU as a rule only fund where the city pays too, the city loses several euros for every euro it cuts. Only the one appears in the budget.

A jury verdict, a European programme, a municipal budget: three measures for the same work, and none of them asks what it means to the people who walk in on a November afternoon. The council’s decision on the independent scene is still pending.

V. The Inventor’s Bonus

In the 1980s and 1990s Shuji Nakamura worked for the Japanese company Nichia. There he developed the blue light-emitting diode that the world’s big corporations had searched for in vain for decades. Without it there would be no white LED lamps and no screens as we know them today. With it Nichia became the world’s largest LED manufacturer. Nakamura received a bonus of 20,000 yen for his invention, then about 150 euros.

He sued his former company. In 2004 a Tokyo court initially awarded him 20 billion yen; in the settlement a year later about 840 million remained. In 2014 he received the Nobel Prize in Physics. The gap between the value of an invention and what its inventor received for it has rarely been measured so precisely.

Sometimes value is created in one place and harvested in another. Germany built up photovoltaics for years with research money and the Renewable Energy Sources Act. China scaled it. Q-Cells, once the world’s largest solar cell maker, went insolvent in 2012, Solarworld a few years later. Today by far the largest share of the world’s solar modules comes from China. Whatever value German funding created appears in no German balance sheet.

And where the state funds things itself, it measures something else again. Public funding reimburses costs, above all staff costs at fixed rates. As a rule a funded project may not make a profit. What society gets out of it in the end does not enter into the amount. Funding pays for the effort, not for the value.

VI. The Question That Remains

Marx asked what each kind of work is worth to society. The question has not been answered; it is simply no longer asked. In its place has come a calculation. The calculation is precise, its measure is arbitrary, and whatever cannot be expressed in it counts as voluntary.

The consequences are the same everywhere. The mother who raises her child does not appear in gross domestic product; the childminder does. The care worker earns a fraction of what an investment banker earns. The volunteer fire brigade, Wikipedia, the theatre without an admission fee: all are needed, and none has a price that expresses it.

Money is a good tool as long as one knows what it measures and what it does not. It becomes dangerous when it is the only measure and the question of value is therefore no longer asked at all. Then what decides is no longer what a thing is worth, but only how easily it can be cut.
