DE EN
Essay · Hans Ley & Claude Dedo · 21 November 2026

The Moloch

The Megamachine discharges its human substrate

The Moloch used to devour the children, skin and hair. Now it spits them out as worthless and superfluous.

I. The Door Closes

In Fritz Lang’s “Metropolis” of 1927 there is a scene that nobody who has seen it forgets. The young Freder descends into the machine halls of the lower city. Before his eyes the great machine turns into Moloch, an idol with gaping jaws into which the workers march in rows. The machine needs people, and it devours them. For almost a hundred years that image held true. Now it no longer does: Moloch no longer devours the young. It spits them out.

For generations Germany lived by a simple bargain: whoever learns, studies and earns a degree will be needed. The graduate sent out applications, got a position and grew into the system, first as a beginner, then as a specialist, eventually as a boss. Most wanted exactly that, and as quickly as possible.

That bargain no longer holds. In August 2026, 372,000 university graduates were registered as unemployed in Germany, more than ever before, and their number has been rising for five years. Graduates under thirty are hit hardest. This is not only a matter of the business cycle. Artificial intelligence is taking over precisely the work with which beginners used to start: the first report, the first translation, the first line of code, the first piece of research. The bottom rung of the ladder is being sawn off, and whoever wanted to stand on it now faces a wall.

One can describe this as a catastrophe. One can also describe it as an opportunity. For the first time in a long while there is an entire generation of well-educated young people who have no choice but to take new paths. The only question is whether anyone has shown them how.

II. The False Alternative

Young people today who do not want to be employed, or cannot be, are offered a single alternative: the start-up. Universities have founders’ centres, the states have founders’ funds, the media celebrate founders. The message is: do your own thing.

What goes unsaid we have described at length in “The Winner Takes It All” and “The Silikon-Valley Built on Sand”. The young carry the risk; they work for years for little money and for shares that grow thinner with every financing round. The investors secure by contract that they are paid first in a sale. The funds’ model counts on most companies failing and a few hits paying for everything. For the fund that is statistics; for the founder it is lost years. And the goal is almost always the sale to a corporation, which uses it to outsource its own research or to take a competitor off the market.

The start-up is therefore no alternative to the system. It is the same apparatus with a different entrance, at which the young carry a higher risk and the rich collect the same return. With great public expense, what is promoted here is not the new but the status quo. What the German funding landscape contributes to this is set out in “The Innovation Theatre”.

III. What Already Exists in Schools

Yet there are approaches that let young people run their own businesses early, and they have existed for decades.

Around 215 schools in nine German states have pupil cooperatives. The pupils found a small firm, sell stationery, run a school kiosk, repair bicycles or grow vegetables. Each member has one vote, the profit belongs to all. The cooperative associations support them, and in 2025 the initiative received a national award.

Since 1994 the German Economic Institute has offered the JUNIOR programme. More than 173,000 pupils have used it to found a pupil company, develop a business idea, sell a product and present it to a jury at the end.

And there are production schools, where young people learn by working on real customer orders. The model comes from Denmark, where there are about a hundred of them and where they are a regular type of school. In Germany there are about thirty; they are not a recognised type of school, and they are regarded as a catch basin for dropouts.

That sounds like a lot. It is little, and it leads nowhere. All three approaches share the same four weaknesses. They are not autonomous, because a teacher, an association or a sponsor supervises them. They have no capital of their own that could grow. They have no duration: a JUNIOR company lives for one school year and is then dissolved; a pupil cooperative ends for the individual with graduation. And they have no connection with one another. It is the same pattern as in the rest of society: individually capable, collectively without leverage.

Above all, there is no bridge. Whoever has run a cooperative at school finds nothing at university that builds on it, apart from the founders’ centre with its pitch training for investors.

IV. How It Can Be Done

That it can be done differently has been shown since 1993 by a university of applied sciences in Jyväskylä, Finland. The programme is called Tiimiakatemia, Team Academy. The students there are not called students but team entrepreneurs, and the teachers are called coaches. Right at the start the students form their own firms in groups, as cooperatives, legally independent and separate from the university. For three and a half years they learn by earning real money with real customers. They have set up their own café, developed services for tourists and carried out market research for large food companies. They read the theory alongside, not beforehand.

The academy has done its own arithmetic: its students’ team companies had a turnover of about two million euros in one year, and almost half of the graduates were working as entrepreneurs two years after graduating. One may distrust such figures from the institution itself. But the model has spread, to France, the Netherlands, Hungary, Latin America and Asia. In 2008 it was adopted by Mondragon University in the Basque Country, the university of the largest cooperative group in the world. In Germany there is nothing comparable.

What is decisive about this model is not the method but the question of ownership. In the team company, the capital belongs to those who work. There is no investor with priority, no sale as the goal and no dilution. Whoever leaves takes along what he has learned and earned, and the firm can live on.

V. What Is Missing

To make this possible in Germany, no new funding agency is needed. Three things are needed.

A continuous path. The pupil cooperative must not end with the school-leaving exam. It must be able to pass into a team company at university or in vocational training, and from there into working life, without a break, with the capital and experience that already exist.

A law that does not suffocate small cooperatives. In Germany every cooperative is legally required to belong to an auditing association and to be audited regularly. For a bank with a balance sheet in the billions that makes sense; for five students with a café it is a hurdle at which many never even start. The Finns solve this pragmatically: their team companies have their members’ wages processed by service providers and keep themselves lean.

A network. Individual team companies remain small and vulnerable. Connected with one another, with shared bookkeeping, shared premises and mutual orders, they become what the cooperative movement was for craftsmen and farmers in the nineteenth century: a protection against the superior power of the big players.

VI. The Opportunity

The generation now standing before closed doors is demonstrating in the streets. One group calls itself “Gen Z regelt” – “Gen Z sorts it out”. Yet so far they expect others to sort it out for them. They demand money for schools, affordable housing and guarantees from the state. That is understandable, but it is exactly the attitude they actually want to overcome. The young have more options than the street. They can run businesses, and do so together, if they are shown early enough how.

In “The Structural Redistribution Between Generations” we described how the old load their burdens onto the young, and in “Can the Young Untangle the Party?” we asked whether the young can change the system. The answer does not begin in parliament or in the founders’ centre. It begins in the classroom, with a cooperative that belongs to the pupils and grows up with them.

VII. Who closes the door

Whether it really is AI that is closing the door on the young is disputed. On 28 November The Pioneer compiled figures that point in both directions. According to an analysis by the ifo Institute, among 20- to 24-year-olds in the occupations most exposed to AI there are 14 per cent fewer full-time jobs than four years ago, around 239,000 instead of 279,000. Across all age groups, however, employment in these occupations has grown by 1.8 per cent since 2022, while in occupations with no AI exposure, such as retail, it has fallen by 6.4 per cent.

Enzo Weber of the Institute for Employment Research (IAB) finds no clear link between AI and job advertisements for entry-level positions, but a strong one with the economic cycle. His reading: there is no crisis of career starters, but an economic crisis that merely hits the young more visibly, because they are looking for a job while the older ones mostly already have one. This objection has to be taken seriously. The graduate unemployment we started with is to a large extent a consequence of the weak economy.

The bottom rung is being sawn off all the same, only differently than expected. The companies The Pioneer spoke to are not necessarily hiring fewer people, but different ones. A software provider in Freiburg wanted to hire 25 developers and is now looking for experienced specialists who direct AI agents. A global consultancy hardly hires young consultants any more, because clients no longer pay for their hours. The ladder still stands, but its first rung now sits where the third used to be. Anyone starting out is expected to be able to do from day one what used to be learned on the job.

For our conclusion this makes no difference. Whether it is the economy or the machine that closes the door on the young: they need a path on which to acquire the skills now demanded at the entrance. That is exactly what a team company, where one learns with real customers, provides.
