The Second Father of Europe, or the Two Ledgers

The wars of the Napoleonic era cost between three and six million lives. Napoleon is venerated all the same, in France as a hero, in Germany as the adversary against whom one became a hero oneself. Both forms of veneration have a source that is seldom named: to a large extent he wrote it himself.

I. Sunday of the Dead

On the Sunday before the first Sunday of Advent, Protestant Christians in Germany remember their dead. The day was ordered by a king. In 1816, three years after the Battle of the Nations at Leipzig, Frederick William III of Prussia decreed that in the Protestant churches of his country the Sunday before Advent should be devoted to the “remembrance of the departed” of the Wars of Liberation. Some 550,000 soldiers had fought at Leipzig in October 1813. About 100,000 were killed or wounded, civilians not counted.

The man that battle was about lies today in Paris beneath the gilded dome of Les Invalides. The dead of Leipzig have a day of remembrance. He has a tomb, and on 5 May 2021, the 200th anniversary of his death, the French president laid a wreath there.

How does a man whose wars cost millions of lives become a man to whom wreaths are brought?

II. The Author of His Myth

The first answer is: he saw to it. On the evening of the Battle of Marengo in June 1800, Bonaparte himself dictated the report to the army. In it the retreat of the early afternoon became a planned withdrawal, a trap for the Austrians. From 1805 the “Bulletins of the Grande Armée” appeared, dictated by the emperor, printed in the official newspaper, translated into the languages of the empire. His own soldiers turned this into a saying: “Menteur comme un bulletin”, lying like a bulletin.

The most important work, however, came after defeat. On the way into exile on St Helena, in August 1815, Napoleon began dictating his life to his companion Emmanuel de Las Cases. He knew what he was doing. Las Cases would be the chronicler of St Helena, he is said to have told him; no one would ever be able to write about him without recourse to these notes. Las Cases stayed only fourteen months on the island before he was expelled. His “Mémorial de Sainte-Hélène” appeared in 1823, two years after Napoleon’s death, in eight volumes, and was perhaps the most widely read book of the nineteenth century.

The Mémorial shows a different Napoleon from the one who had ruled: a liberal defending the principles of the Revolution, a prince of peace who wanted to unite Europe and was prevented by the monarchs. How much of this Napoleon said and how much Las Cases added could long not be checked, because the British had confiscated the manuscript. Since it was published in 2017 we know: Las Cases embellished considerably.

Stendhal captured the effect in a single sentence. His hero Julien Sorel in “The Red and the Black” would have let himself be killed for three books: Rousseau’s “Confessions”, the collected Bulletins of the Grande Armée and the Mémorial of St Helena. Two of the three books that inflamed a young Frenchman came from the emperor’s workshop.

This is the same situation as with Charlemagne. His Saxon wars, too, are reported almost only by the annals of his own court. With Napoleon it is simpler still: the court and the ruler are the same person.

III. In Charlemagne’s Footsteps

Napoleon deliberately placed himself in Charlemagne’s succession. On 7 September 1804 he stood in Aachen at Charlemagne’s tomb. Aachen had been considered for the imperial coronation, because of Charlemagne. The coronation then took place on 2 December 1804 in Notre-Dame in Paris, in the presence of Pope Pius VII, whom Napoleon had brought to Paris for the purpose. The crown, however, he placed on his own head.

With Charlemagne, throne and altar had still struck a bargain: the king protected the pope, and the pope crowned the king. With Napoleon the altar remained a spectator. It was to lend the ceremony dignity, nothing more. Rule no longer came from God, nor from the people, but from the ruler himself. That he nevertheless crowned himself in a church shows how well he understood what an image can do.

He also conferred the title of father of Europe on himself after the fact. Charlemagne had a court poet call him “pater Europae”. On St Helena Napoleon took over this role himself: in the Mémorial he appears as the man who had wanted to unite the peoples of Europe into a whole and had been prevented only by the hostility of the old powers.

IV. The First Ledger

How many people died in the Napoleonic Wars from 1803 to 1815 no one knows exactly. Military dead are estimated at 2.5 to 3.5 million, civilian dead at 750,000 to three million. Roughly half of the soldiers died in the three years 1812 to 1814, in the Russian campaign and the wars that followed. Most did not fall in battle but died of hunger, cold and disease.

Whoever attributes these dead to Napoleon alone, however, makes it too easy. How far he was responsible for the wars is still disputed among historians. Britain broke the Peace of Amiens in May 1803 and declared war on France. In 1805 Austria marched into Bavaria, in 1806 Prussia issued an ultimatum, in 1809 Austria attacked again. The old monarchies also fought these wars to defend the order that the Revolution had called into question. The war in Spain in 1808 and the campaign into Russia in 1812, however, Napoleon began himself, and it was precisely these two wars that claimed the most victims.

Even the French president did not conceal this in his speech on the 200th anniversary of Napoleon’s death: Napoleon, he said, never really concerned himself with the loss of human life in his conquests. He also mentioned that Chateaubriand had accused him of sacrificing five million Frenchmen, and added that this had been a great exaggeration.

The ledger also includes what happened outside Europe. In 1794 the French National Convention had abolished slavery. This was enforced only in Guadeloupe and in Saint-Domingue, today’s Haiti. On 20 May 1802 First Consul Bonaparte had a law passed that maintained slavery in the colonies where abolition had never applied. In Guadeloupe it was reintroduced by decree in July 1802. To Saint-Domingue he sent his brother-in-law, General Leclerc, with an army. Leclerc lured Toussaint Louverture, the leader of the freed slaves, to a meeting, had him arrested and taken to France. Louverture died in April 1803 in a cell of the Fort de Joux in the Jura. Half a year later his successors defeated the French, and Haiti became the first state founded by former slaves.

V. The Second Ledger

We have made it our task to write without mercy, but we do not want to be one-sided. Napoleon left lasting things behind, and they are most clearly visible in the Rhineland. In 1810 the Code Napoléon, the French civil code, came into force in the Grand Duchy of Berg. With it and the reforms of the preceding years came equality before the law, the end of feudal rights and civil marriage. On the left bank of the Rhine it had applied even earlier. When the Rhineland became Prussian in 1815, French law remained in force there. As “Rhenish law” it applied until 1 January 1900, when the German Civil Code replaced it. Here lies one of the reasons why Napoleon is seen differently in the Rhineland than in Berlin.

He also redrew the map. Under his pressure, the hundreds of ecclesiastical and secular territories, imperial cities and imperial knighthoods of the old Empire became a few dozen states. Without this consolidation the later German nation-state would hardly have been conceivable. The historian Thomas Nipperdey therefore began his German history of the nineteenth century with the sentence: “In the beginning was Napoleon.” In the Kingdom of Westphalia, which Napoleon created for his brother Jérôme, the Jews in 1808 became the first in Germany to receive full civil equality. In the same year, however, he restricted their rights in trade and in the choice of residence in France for ten years by a decree that those affected called the “infamous decree”.

Nor was the code itself a pure work of freedom. It made the wife legally dependent on her husband, and it systematically placed illegitimate children and their mothers at a disadvantage compared with the law of many countries before it.

Hegel saw Napoleon in Jena in 1806 as the “world soul on horseback” and made of it his cunning of reason: the great man pursues his own ends and in doing so brings about something else. Napoleon wanted an empire and left behind a civil code. That is correctly observed. But it does not explain why the man is venerated for the code while the dead are overlooked. A civil code could have been written without three million dead. Hardly anyone knows the jurists who drafted it.

VI. Two Cults, One Mass Grave

Veneration has two sides, and both live off the same dead.

In France King Louis-Philippe had Napoleon’s remains brought back from St Helena in 1840 and interred in Les Invalides. Since then the attitude towards him has been a gauge of French politics. In 2005 President Chirac and Prime Minister de Villepin, who had himself written a book about Napoleon, stayed away from the celebrations of the 200th anniversary of the Battle of Austerlitz, while a French aircraft carrier took part in the British fleet review for the 200th anniversary of Trafalgar. In 2021 President Macron decided otherwise. “Napoleon Bonaparte is a part of us,” he said in his speech. He called the reintroduction of slavery a “betrayal of the spirit of the Enlightenment”. To commemorate is not to celebrate, the Élysée Palace declared. Then the president laid the wreath at the sarcophagus. Five days later, at the ceremony commemorating the abolition of slavery, he remained silent.

In Germany the counter-cult arose. On 10 March 1813 Frederick William III instituted the Iron Cross. The colours black, red and gold go back to the uniforms of the Lützow Free Corps. The Battle of the Nations, although it did not finally defeat Napoleon, became the most potent founding myth of the Germans. On 18 October 1913, its hundredth anniversary, Emperor William II inaugurated the Monument to the Battle of the Nations in Leipzig, 91 metres high, paid for by the donations of a “Patriots’ League”. Delegations from Russia and Austria were invited, France was not. “The hero of the monument is the entire German people that rose up,” the architect declared. Ten months later the First World War began.

The one cult celebrates the commander of these wars. The other celebrates the dead as heroes who defeated him. Both turn the same mass dying into a hero’s story, only with different heroes. And both were founded by rulers: the French by the emperor himself, the German by a king who after victory disappointed the hopes of his volunteers. Instead of the promised constitution came restoration and censorship.

VII. The Others

Neither cult has room for the people whom these wars shifted across Europe. The armies of these years were migrations of peoples. Peasants’ sons from Württemberg died before Moscow, Cossacks rode through Paris, hussars from Hungary stood on the Rhine. With them went grooms, smiths, sutlers. Many did not return, some stayed where the war had set them down. One of them was Conrad Ley, who came to the Rhine with his brother in a Hungarian hussar regiment and stayed there. We have told his story in “Mücher, Not Mucher”.

Lands were shifted too. In 1806 the Duchy of Berg was ceded to France and handed to Napoleon’s brother-in-law Murat; in 1815 the Rhineland went to Prussia at the Congress of Vienna. No one was asked, neither in 1806 nor in 1815. Our essay “Land, Nation, State” deals with this.

VIII. Why He Is Venerated

Why, then, is Napoleon venerated? Three reasons can be given that are documented.

First, because he wrote his story himself. The Bulletins and the Mémorial are not sources about Napoleon but works by him. Whoever reads them reads court historiography at first hand.

Second, because nations need founders. France has in him the man who ended the Revolution and cast its achievements into law. Germany has in him the adversary against whom it became a nation. Both narratives need him to be great, the one as a hero, the other as an enemy worth defeating.

Third, because one sees what lasted and not the dead. A civil code stands on the shelf. The dead of Leipzig, of Borodino, of the Berezina and of Saint-Domingue no longer have names that anyone knows.

So there are two ledgers, and both are real. On the one stand the dead, on the other the civil code, the new map and equality before the law. Veneration lives by opening only the second, condemnation by reading only the first. Whoever writes history must present both and may not offset one against the other. No civil code outweighs the dead, and no number of dead makes the civil code wrong.

The Sunday of the Dead is an attempt to remember the dead, even if it was founded by a king who was thinking of his own cause. Les Invalides remembers the man who waged most of these wars and told their story most loudly. Whoever wants to write history from the victims’ point of view need not deny the Code civil, need not demonise the emperor, and need not absolve the monarchs who waged war against him. He need only count the dead, all of them: the French, the Prussians, the Russians, the Spaniards, the slaves in Guadeloupe and the hussars from Hungary.
