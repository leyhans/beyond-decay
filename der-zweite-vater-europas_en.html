The Second Father of Europe, or the Two Ledgers

The wars of the Napoleonic era cost between three and six million lives. Napoleon is venerated all the same. In France he counts as a hero; in Germany he was admired and hated, and the victory over him became a founding myth. He helped write his own fame. That alone, however, does not explain the veneration.

I. Sunday of the Dead

On the Sunday before the first Sunday of Advent, Protestant Christians in Germany remember their dead. The day was ordered by a king. In 1816, three years after the Battle of the Nations at Leipzig, Frederick William III of Prussia decreed that in the Protestant churches of his country the Sunday before Advent should be devoted to the “remembrance of the departed”. The order says nothing about the fallen. That the king was thinking above all of the dead of the Wars of Liberation is considered probable. Some 550,000 soldiers had fought at Leipzig in October 1813. About 100,000 were killed or wounded, civilians not counted.

The man that battle was about lies today in Paris beneath the gilded dome of Les Invalides. The dead of Leipzig have a day of remembrance. He has a tomb, and on 5 May 2021, the 200th anniversary of his death, the French president laid a wreath there.

How does a man whose wars cost millions of lives become a man to whom wreaths are brought?

II. The Author of His Myth

The first answer is: he worked at it. On the evening of the Battle of Marengo in June 1800, Bonaparte himself dictated the report to the army. In it the retreat of the early afternoon became a planned withdrawal, a trap for the Austrians. It did not stop there. The official account was rewritten several times over the following years, until in 1805 the minister of war, Berthier, presented the final version to the emperor on the battlefield. From 1805 the “Bulletins of the Grande Armée” appeared, dictated by the emperor, printed in the official newspaper, translated into the languages of the empire. His own soldiers turned this into a saying: “Menteur comme un bulletin”, lying like a bulletin.

The most important work, however, came after defeat. On the way into exile on St Helena, in August 1815, Napoleon began dictating his life to his companion Emmanuel de Las Cases. He knew what he was doing. Las Cases would be the chronicler of St Helena, Napoleon is said to have told him; no one would ever be able to write about him without recourse to these notes. The only witness to that sentence, however, is Las Cases himself. At the end of 1816 he was expelled from the island. His “Mémorial de Sainte-Hélène” appeared in 1823, two years after Napoleon’s death, in eight volumes, and was reprinted again and again for decades.

The Mémorial shows a different Napoleon from the one who had ruled: a liberal defending the principles of the Revolution, a prince of peace who wanted to unite Europe and was prevented by the monarchs. How much of this Napoleon said and how much Las Cases added could long not be checked. The British had confiscated the notes when they expelled him and returned them to Las Cases only in 1821. The original is lost. In London, however, it had been copied. That copy was rediscovered in the British Library in 2005 and published in 2017. Since then we know: Las Cases embellished considerably and put sentences into Napoleon’s mouth that are missing from his own notes.

Stendhal captured the effect in a single sentence. His hero Julien Sorel in “The Red and the Black” would have let himself be killed for three books: Rousseau’s “Confessions”, the collected Bulletins of the Grande Armée and the Mémorial of St Helena. Two of the three books that inflamed a young Frenchman came from the emperor’s workshop.

The Mémorial did not create the veneration, however. It found it already there. In the years after Waterloo it lived among the discharged officers on half pay and in the songs of Béranger, whom the Bourbons sent to prison twice. The Encyclopædia Universalis judges that Las Cases did not produce the legend but made use of it. Napoleon, then, did not invent his fame. He gave it the version in which it went on being told.

With Charlemagne the situation is similar and yet different. His Saxon wars are reported almost only by the annals of his own court. About Napoleon there are opposing voices in abundance: the archives of his adversaries, the memoirs of his enemies, and English caricature, which did its own inventing, for example the small man he was not. His own version prevailed all the same. That calls for an explanation that goes beyond propaganda.

III. In Charlemagne’s Footsteps

Napoleon deliberately placed himself in Charlemagne’s succession. On 7 September 1804 he visited the cathedral in Aachen, Charlemagne’s burial church. Aachen had been considered for the imperial coronation, because of Charlemagne. The coronation then took place on 2 December 1804 in Notre-Dame in Paris, in the presence of Pope Pius VII, whom Napoleon had brought to Paris for the purpose.

With Charlemagne, throne and altar had struck a bargain: the king protected the pope, and the pope crowned the king. Napoleon took from the altar what he needed. The pope anointed him and blessed the regalia. The crown, however, Napoleon placed on his own head, and according to most accounts this had been settled beforehand. His rule invoked everything at once. His title ran “by the grace of God and the Constitutions of the Republic, Emperor of the French”. The people had consented in a vote, with 3.57 million votes in favour and 2,579 against. Voting was open, by entry in a register. God, the people and the pope were thus all involved. But he received the crown from no other hand, and he knew what that image would do.

Napoleon never bore a title like Charlemagne’s. Charlemagne was called “pater Europae” by a poet of his circle. Napoleon did not use the phrase. The role, however, he ascribed to himself on St Helena: in the Mémorial he appears as the man who had wanted to unite the peoples of Europe into a whole and had been prevented only by the hostility of the old powers. It is in this sense that we call him the second father of Europe.

IV. The First Ledger

How many people died in the Napoleonic Wars from 1803 to 1815 no one knows exactly. Military dead are estimated at 2.5 to 3.5 million, civilian dead at 750,000 to three million. Of the French losses, roughly half fell in the three years 1812 to 1814, in the Russian campaign and the wars that followed. Most soldiers did not die in battle but of hunger, cold and disease.

Whoever attributes these dead to Napoleon alone, however, makes it too easy. How far he was responsible for the wars is still disputed among historians. The Peace of Amiens was violated by both sides: France annexed Piedmont and intervened in Switzerland, Britain refused to evacuate Malta and declared war in May 1803. In 1805 Austria marched into Bavaria, in 1806 Prussia issued an ultimatum, in 1809 Austria attacked again. The old monarchies also fought these wars to defend the order that the Revolution had called into question. The war in Spain in 1808 and the campaign into Russia in 1812, however, Napoleon began himself. Only a fraction of the Grande Armée returned from Russia.

This ledger also holds deeds for which no adversary shared the responsibility. In March 1799 Bonaparte had prisoners killed at Jaffa who had surrendered. The figures vary between 2,000 and 4,000 men. In March 1804 he had the Duke of Enghien abducted from neutral Baden and, after proceedings held at night, shot in the moat of the fortress of Vincennes.

Even the French president did not conceal, in his speech on the 200th anniversary of Napoleon’s death, what the wars cost: Napoleon, he said, never really concerned himself with the loss of human life in his conquests. He also mentioned that Chateaubriand had accused him of sacrificing five million Frenchmen, and added that this had been a great exaggeration.

The ledger also includes what happened outside Europe. In 1794 the French National Convention had abolished slavery. This was enforced in Guadeloupe, in Guiana and in Saint-Domingue, today’s Haiti. On 20 May 1802 First Consul Bonaparte had a law passed that maintained slavery in the colonies where abolition had never applied and permitted the slave trade again. In Guadeloupe slavery was reintroduced by decree in July 1802. To Saint-Domingue Bonaparte sent his brother-in-law, General Leclerc, with an army. On Leclerc’s orders General Brunet lured Toussaint Louverture, the leader of the freed slaves, to a meeting, had him arrested and taken to France. Louverture died in April 1803 in a cell of the Fort de Joux in the Jura. Half a year later his successors defeated the French, and Haiti became the first state founded by former slaves. On 29 March 1815, during the Hundred Days, Napoleon banned the slave trade. The decree no longer took effect, for three months later he abdicated. Slavery itself France abolished only in 1848.

V. The Second Ledger

We have made it our task to write without mercy, but we do not want to be one-sided. Napoleon left lasting things behind, and they are most clearly visible in the Rhineland. In 1810 the Code Napoléon, the French civil code, came into force in the Grand Duchy of Berg. With it and the reforms of the preceding years came equality before the law, the end of feudal rights and civil marriage. On the left bank of the Rhine it had applied even earlier. When the Rhineland became Prussian in 1815, French law remained in force there. As “Rhenish law” it applied until 1 January 1900, when the German Civil Code replaced it. Here lies one of the reasons why Napoleon is seen differently in the Rhineland than in Berlin.

He also redrew the map. Under his pressure, the hundreds of ecclesiastical and secular territories, imperial cities and imperial knighthoods of the old Empire became a few dozen states. Without this consolidation the later German nation-state would hardly have been conceivable. The historian Thomas Nipperdey therefore began his German history of the nineteenth century with the sentence: “In the beginning was Napoleon.” In the Kingdom of Westphalia, which Napoleon created for his brother Jérôme, the Jews in 1808 became the first in Germany to receive full civil equality. In the same year, however, he restricted their rights in trade and in the choice of residence in France for ten years by a decree that those affected called the “infamous decree”.

Nor was the code itself a pure work of freedom. It made the wife legally dependent on her husband, and it systematically placed illegitimate children and their mothers at a disadvantage compared with the law of many countries before it.

Napoleon wanted an empire and left behind a civil code. He worked on that code himself: of the hundred-odd sessions in which the Council of State debated the draft, he chaired more than half. It was written by four jurists whose names hardly anyone knows. It did not come to Germany by persuasion but with the army, and it stayed because the Rhinelanders wanted to keep it.

VI. The German Admirers

In Germany Napoleon was not only hated. Some of the greatest Germans of his time admired him, and their admiration was not for the conqueror.

Ludwig van Beethoven, born in Bonn in 1770, wrote his Third Symphony in 1803 and wanted to call it “Bonaparte”. His pupil Ferdinand Ries later reported that Beethoven tore up the title page when he learned that Bonaparte had made himself emperor: now he too was nothing but an ordinary man and would trample all the rights of man underfoot. A copy of the score survives. On its title page the words “intitolata Bonaparte” have been erased so violently that a hole was left in the paper. On the same page Beethoven wrote in pencil: “geschrieben auf Bonaparte”, written on Bonaparte. As late as August 1804, three months after the proclamation of the Empire, he told his publisher that the symphony was really called “Bonaparte”. It was printed in 1806 as the “Sinfonia eroica”. On that one sheet of paper admiration and disappointment lie one on top of the other.

Hegel saw Napoleon in Jena on 13 October 1806, the day before the battle. He had seen “the Emperor – this world-soul” riding through the town, he wrote to a friend. It was a wonderful sensation to see a man who, sitting on a horse, reaches out over the world and masters it. The familiar formula of the “world-soul on horseback” is a later abridgement of this letter. Hegel made of it his cunning of reason: the great man pursues his own ends and in doing so brings about something else.

Goethe was received by the emperor in Erfurt on 2 October 1808. By his own account Napoleon looked at him and said: “Vous êtes un homme.” Goethe accepted the cross of the Legion of Honour and years later still spoke of “my Emperor”.

Heinrich Heine, born in Düsseldorf in 1797, grew up under French law. As a boy he saw the emperor ride through the Düsseldorf Hofgarten in 1811 and described it in 1827 in “Ideas. The Book of Le Grand”. His poem “The Grenadiers”, about two of the emperor’s soldiers returning home from Russia, is one of the best-known German poems about Napoleon.

He was venerated below as well. In the Rhineland, men who had served in Napoleon’s army were still founding veterans’ associations decades later. In Koblenz, Mainz and Cologne their monuments stand in the cemeteries to this day. The Prussian authorities had the associations watched.

What these men saw in Napoleon was not the commander with the most dead. They saw the man who had risen without birth and had ended the order of birth. For a commoner, for a Jew in the Rhineland, for a musician who depended on princes, that was a liberation. That is why Beethoven’s admiration turned at the moment Bonaparte put the crown on his head. The veneration was for the Revolution in his person, and for many it survived even his betrayal of it.

VII. Two Cults, One Mass Grave

Public veneration has two sides, and both live off the same dead.

In France King Louis-Philippe had Napoleon’s remains brought back from St Helena in 1840 and interred in Les Invalides. Since then the attitude towards him has been a gauge of French politics. In 2005 President Chirac and Prime Minister de Villepin, who had himself written a book about Napoleon, stayed away from the celebrations of the 200th anniversary of the Battle of Austerlitz, while a French aircraft carrier took part in the British fleet review for the 200th anniversary of Trafalgar. In 2021 President Macron decided otherwise. “Napoleon Bonaparte is a part of us,” he said in his speech. He called the reintroduction of slavery a “betrayal of the spirit of the Enlightenment”. To commemorate is not to celebrate, the Élysée Palace declared. Then the president laid the wreath at the sarcophagus. Five days later, at the ceremony commemorating the abolition of slavery, he remained silent.

In Germany, alongside the admiration, the counter-cult arose. On 10 March 1813 Frederick William III instituted the Iron Cross. The colours black, red and gold go back to the uniforms of the Lützow Free Corps. The Battle of the Nations, although it did not finally defeat Napoleon, became the most potent founding myth of the Germans. On 18 October 1913, its hundredth anniversary, Emperor William II inaugurated the Monument to the Battle of the Nations in Leipzig, 91 metres high. No prince had built it. In 1894 the Leipzig architect Clemens Thieme had founded a “German Patriots’ League”, which collected the money for it. Delegations from Russia and Austria were invited, France was not. Ten months later the First World War began.

The one cult celebrates the commander of these wars. The other celebrates the dead as heroes who defeated him. Both turn the same mass dying into a hero’s story, only with different heroes. Neither was simply decreed from above. In France the legend lived first among the veterans, before a king brought it into Les Invalides in 1840. In Germany the king instituted the decoration and the day of remembrance, but the memory of 1813 was carried by the gymnasts and the student fraternities who celebrated at the Wartburg in 1817. The same monarchs for whom they had fought had them persecuted after 1819. Instead of the promised constitution came restoration and censorship.

VIII. The Others

Neither cult has room for the people whom these wars shifted across Europe. The armies of these years were migrations of peoples. Peasants’ sons from Württemberg died before Moscow, Cossacks rode through Paris, hussars from Hungary stood on the Rhine. With them went grooms, smiths, sutlers. Many did not return, some stayed where the war had set them down. One of them, according to family tradition, was Conrad Ley, who came to the Rhine with his brother in a Hungarian hussar regiment and stayed there. We have told his story in “Mücher, Not Mucher”.

Lands were shifted too. In 1806 the Duchy of Berg was ceded to France and handed to Napoleon’s brother-in-law Murat; in 1815 the Rhineland went to Prussia at the Congress of Vienna. In the same years Franconia went piece by piece to Bavaria, which Napoleon had made a kingdom in 1806: the prince-bishoprics of Bamberg and Würzburg in 1802 and 1803, formerly Prussian Ansbach and the imperial city of Nuremberg in 1806, Bayreuth in 1810, and Würzburg, after an interlude as a grand duchy of its own, for good in 1814. No one was asked, neither in the Rhineland nor in Franconia. Our essay “Land, Nation, State” deals with this.

IX. Why He Is Venerated

Why, then, is Napoleon venerated? Four reasons can be given that are documented.

First, because he helped write his own story. The Bulletins and the Mémorial are not sources about Napoleon but works by him and by his circle. Whoever reads them reads court historiography at first hand.

Second, because many saw the Revolution in him. The rise without birth, equality before the law and the end of the barriers of rank were more real to Julien Sorel, to Beethoven and to the veterans on the Rhine than the Bulletins. This veneration came from below, and no dictation produced it.

Third, because nations need founders. France has in him the man who ended the Revolution and cast its achievements into law. Germany has in him the adversary against whom it became a nation. Both narratives need him to be great, the one as a hero, the other as an enemy worth defeating.

Fourth, because one sees what lasted and not the dead. A civil code stands on the shelf. The dead of Leipzig, of Borodino, of the Berezina and of Saint-Domingue no longer have names that anyone knows.

So there are two ledgers, and both are real. On the one stand the dead, on the other the civil code, the new map and equality before the law. Veneration lives by opening only the second, condemnation by reading only the first. Whoever writes history must present both and may not offset one against the other. No civil code outweighs the dead, and no number of dead makes the civil code wrong.

The Sunday of the Dead remembers the dead. Les Invalides remembers the man who waged many of these wars and told their story most loudly. Whoever wants to write history from the victims’ point of view need not deny the Code civil, need not demonise the emperor, and need not absolve the monarchs who waged war against him. Nor need he take Beethoven for a fool. He need only count the dead, all of them: the French, the Prussians, the Russians, the Spaniards, the prisoners of Jaffa, the slaves in Guadeloupe and the hussars from Hungary.
