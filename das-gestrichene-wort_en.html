The Struck-Out Word

Canada, associate membership and the German red pen

In September the European Commission offers Canada the chance to become the Union’s first associate member. In October Germany strikes the word from the summit declaration. At the summit, Canada’s prime minister says it anyway. An addendum to “The General Standard”.

I. The offer

Canada has a neighbour that has called it the “51st state” since late 2024 and puts it under pressure with tariffs. In February 2026 Canada became the first country outside Europe to join the European defence programme SAFE, a loan facility of 150 billion euros with which Europe wants to build its own weapons again. The agreement was signed on 14 February and has applied provisionally since April.

On 16 September 2026 Ursula von der Leyen gave her State of the Union address in Strasbourg. Mark Carney sat in the front row, the first foreign head of government to attend this speech. Von der Leyen turned to him directly: “I would like to work with you on opening the door for Canada to be the first associate member of the EU.” The sentence was not in the advance text of the speech. The MEPs rose and applauded. Donald Trump called the idea a possible “hostile act” the same day and threatened the EU with further tariffs. A week later, in a Leger poll, 70 per cent of Canadians supported associate membership and 13 per cent opposed it. Support became shakier, however, once Canada would have to change its own laws for it.

II. The red pen

Six weeks later, on 29 and 30 October, the EU and Canada met for a summit in Montreal. On the Wednesday before, a few hours before von der Leyen flew to Montreal, the word had disappeared from the draft of the joint declaration. Instead of “associate membership” it now spoke of “ever closer association”, expressly short of accession. Several member states had raised objections, led by Germany. According to people involved, Berlin called the term a red line.

The reasoning passed on by diplomats was that one could not endorse a concept without legal status or a sufficiently developed framework. The content of the partnership had to be settled first, before attaching a label with potential legal implications. Other diplomats feared the word might raise expectations among further countries outside the EU.

That is not wrong. Associate membership does not exist in the treaties. Nobody knows who would have to decide on it or what it would include. Whoever hands out a word before knowing what it means creates a claim that may later prove impossible to honour.

At the end of the summit Carney nonetheless stood on stage and said Canada was working towards the deepest possible partnership short of accession, “with Canada becoming the European Union’s first associate member”. Von der Leyen kept using the term too. By the next summit in 2027, what the partnership is to contain is to be worked out.

III. Who coined the word

The term comes from Berlin. On 18 May 2026 Friedrich Merz wrote a confidential letter to von der Leyen, Council President Costa and the Cypriot Council presidency. In it he proposed an “associate membership” for Ukraine: representation in the European Council, the Council of the EU and the Parliament, an associate commissioner, but without voting rights, as an intermediate stage until full accession. Five days later Zelensky rejected it in a letter to the EU’s leaders. The model was unfair, he wrote, because Ukraine would have no voice in the Union.

Whether that was the real reason for the red pen is not established. Berlin denies it. Only the sequence is certain: in May the Chancellor coins a word for Ukraine, Ukraine rejects it, in September the Commission offers it to Canada, and in October it is Germany that strikes it out.

IV. The prehistory in gas

It is not the first time Germany and Canada have missed each other at the decisive moment. In August 2022, half a year after Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, Olaf Scholz travelled to Canada to find liquefied natural gas for Europe. At the joint press conference Justin Trudeau replied that for an export terminal on the Atlantic coast “there has never been a strong business case because of the distance from the gas fields”. Scholz left without a commitment.

In July 2026 Uniper signed a contract for Canadian liquefied gas for up to twenty years, two million tonnes a year. The gas will come from a terminal that has yet to be built on the Pacific coast of British Columbia. The first delivery is expected in 2032. Ten years after Scholz asked, the gas will travel halfway around the globe.

In 2022 Canada asked about the business case and said no. In 2026 Germany asked about legal status and said no. Both times the question was legitimate, and both times the answer came from the logic of one’s own department, not from the situation one was in.

V. The version that works

In “The General Standard” we described three versions of a standard that displaces the purpose. The second, the version that works, reads: whatever is right for my task is right. The lawyer in the Chancellery who insists that the status does not exist in the treaties is right. So is the diplomat who warns of precedents. Neither does anything wrong. The result is nonetheless that Europe answers a partner in distress who asks for closeness by pointing to missing definitions, while the American president is already threatening tariffs because the closeness goes too far for him.

Picture a firm that does not hire the best applicant it has had in years because the post does not yet have a name in the organisation chart. The personnel department works correctly. The applicant leaves anyway, or stays because he has no choice, and remembers how he was received.

Canada asked whether it could join the table. Germany replied that the chair was not yet listed in the inventory.
