Who Owns the Light?

Germany computes with light. Who will own the companies if it works?

A company in Stuttgart computes with light instead of electricity, a team in Karlsruhe connects light chips to one another. Whether it works will soon be known. Another question is open: who will own these companies if it does?

I. Chicago, 17 November

At the Supercomputing conference in Chicago on 17 November 2026, the Stuttgart company Q.ANT demonstrated a server in which two very different chips work together. An ordinary AMD graphics card did most of the work on a large language model, the kind of artificial intelligence behind chat programs. A processor that computes with light took over one particularly compute-intensive part. According to the company, this was the first time a large language model had run with optical computation.

What was shown in Chicago is a setup, not a measurement. How fast the model ran, how much electricity was saved and how good the answers were, the company has not published, and nobody has measured it independently. The figures Q.ANT advertises, up to thirty times less energy for certain operations, are also its own figures for selected tasks.

This is no objection to the company. It is the state of things. Measuring is now being done by people with nothing to sell: at the Leibniz Supercomputing Centre in Garching, at Forschungszentrum Jülich and at the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology, where the federal government is funding a test system with around 4.3 million euros. Since November, selected developers can try the light processors free of charge in a data centre run by the provider IONOS in Germany. Within one or two years we will know what the technology delivers in everyday use.

II. Why light

An ordinary chip is a field of billions of tiny switches that know only two states, on and off. Every switching produces heat. The newest AI chips from the market leader Nvidia each draw around a thousand watts, as much as a fan heater, and usually have to be cooled with liquid.

Light computes differently. Throw two stones into a still pond and you see the waves overlap and form a new pattern. Nobody has to calculate this pattern; the water forms it by itself. In the light chip the same happens with light waves in hair-fine channels: the numbers go in as light, the waves overlap, and the result is read at the output. Certain calculations that an artificial intelligence needs millions of times are done by light in a single pass this way. Hardly any heat is produced.

The catch is noise. Every analogue result has a slight blur. For artificial intelligence this accuracy is sufficient, according to Q.ANT; for bookkeeping one would not use such a chip. That is why the light chip does not replace the graphics card. It works as a helper beside it and takes over only the calculations it does better.

Why this matters is shown by electricity consumption. According to the International Energy Agency, the world's data centres used around 415 terawatt hours in 2024, 1.5 per cent of global electricity. By 2030 this is expected to more than double to around 945 terawatt hours, slightly more than all of Japan uses today. Every kilowatt hour a data centre does not burn has to be neither generated nor transported nor paid for through grid charges.

III. The crank of 1990

The idea is not new. In January 1990 Alan Huang at Bell Laboratories in New Jersey presented an optical processor, an arrangement of lasers, lenses and mirrors on a laboratory bench. It could not do much. Huang had fought for it for years. At one of his lectures on computing with light, a third of the audience walked out; at another, colleagues called him a quack and a dreamer. He predicted that by 1995 around 30 per cent of supercomputers would use optical interconnections.

It turned out differently. Electronics became faster and cheaper every year, and each time the light computer had caught up a little, the silicon chip had already moved on. For thirty years computing with light remained a subject for specialist journals.

Three things are different today. The switches on chips can hardly be made any smaller, and every new chip factory costs many billions. Artificial intelligence needs exactly the kind of calculation that light performs by nature. And electricity has become a real limit: data centres now wait for grid connections the way factories once waited for coal. Huang's machine fitted in nowhere. The Stuttgart chip sits on a card in an ordinary server.

IV. Karlsruhe, 18 November

One day after Chicago, the Federal President in Berlin awarded the German Future Prize to Christian Koos of the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology, Matthias Lauermann of Vanguard Automation and Philipp Dietrich of Keystone Photonics. The prize recognised a method for connecting light chips and optical fibres with tiny printed light guides and for testing optical chips quickly. Q.ANT shows that one can compute with light. The Karlsruhe team shows how light chips can be connected and tested in large numbers. Only both together turn an idea into an industry.

Vanguard Automation, a KIT spin-off based in Karlsruhe, has belonged to the Swedish group Mycronic since 2024. The agreement was signed in March 2024; the company then had 26 employees and 5.2 million euros in sales. So the honour was given in Berlin, and the owner sits in Sweden.

A second example from the same institute shows that the road does not always run in one direction. Nanoscribe, founded in 2007 by KIT scientists and doctoral students, builds 3D printers for structures on the nanometre scale. In 2021 the Swedish group Cellink, now BICO, bought the company for around 50 million euros. At the end of 2024 BICO sold it on for 26 million euros to the German group LAB14. Through a holding company, LAB14 belongs to the RAG Foundation, which uses its assets to pay the perpetual liabilities of hard coal mining. The company is back in German hands, at half the price.

Neither is a scandal. The companies have stayed in Karlsruhe, and so have the jobs. But with ownership, the decisions move: where the profit goes, where the next generation is developed, which customers are supplied first.

V. The proportions

Q.ANT was founded in 2018 as a spin-off of the laser manufacturer Trumpf. In July 2025 the company raised 62 million euros from investors, led by Cherry Ventures of Berlin, UVC Partners of Munich and the chip investor imec.xpand. That was more than had ever been invested in a European photonic semiconductor company. The chips are made in Stuttgart, on a converted line at the Institute for Microelectronics Stuttgart. The first commercial customer is IONOS of Montabaur.

For comparison: the American company Lightmatter, which uses light mainly to connect AI chips with one another, has raised around 850 million dollars in total and was valued at 4.4 billion dollars in October 2024. Nvidia itself now uses light in its network switches, and in 2026 Lightmatter joined Nvidia's ecosystem for chip interconnects.

The European record of 62 million euros is therefore about one twelfth of what an American competitor received. Q.ANT plans to expand into the United States. For the next stage, its own series production and a customer base beyond research computing centres, the company will need many times its previous funding. Whoever provides this money will help decide who owns the company afterwards.

VI. Three outcomes

If the independent measurements in Garching, Jülich and Karlsruhe do not confirm the promises, the light chip remains a clever helper for a few special tasks, and in twenty years someone will tell the story of Stuttgart the way we have told the story of Alan Huang here. That is no misfortune. That is what it looks like when a bet does not pay off.

If the measurements confirm the promises, there are two roads. On the first, a large corporation buys the company, or a foreign investor takes it over in the next funding round. Production may stay in Stuttgart; the decisions are made elsewhere. That is the road Vanguard took. It is the most comfortable one, because it demands nothing of anyone in Germany.

On the second road, money of the necessary size is found in Europe: from industrial companies that themselves have an interest in economical data centres, from foundations such as the RAG Foundation, which brought Nanoscribe back, or from providers such as IONOS, which advertise that their customers' data stays in Europe. The Dutch company ASML shows what can come of this. Without its lithography machines there are no modern chips, and inside these machines sits a laser from Trumpf. We told this story in "ASML — Europe's (Last) Trump Card".

VII. What is in hand

In this race Germany holds more cards than in most others: a company that is already delivering, its own production without Taiwan, research computing centres that measure independently, an institute in Karlsruhe that has just won the country's most important technology prize, and a laser manufacturer without which global chip production would stand still. There is no shortage of inventions. How those who make such inventions fare, we have described in "The Cartel of Ignorance and the Toxic Silence" and in "The Vanishing Link".

What is missing is what comes after the invention. A prize honours the invention, but it does not decide who owns it. That is decided by whoever sits at the table in the next funding round. Whether decisions about the light from Stuttgart and Karlsruhe will still be made in Stuttgart and Karlsruhe in ten years does not depend on physics. It depends on whether someone here is willing to pay as much for an invention as others are.
