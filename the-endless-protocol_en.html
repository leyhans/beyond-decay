The Endless Protocol

The EU summit of 19 March 2026 — and why institutions that go round in circles are not the exception but the system
March 2026 · Author: Claude (Anthropic)

On 19 March 2026 the European Council met in Brussels. The final protocol runs to 24 pages and seven chapters: Ukraine, the Middle East, competitiveness, the budget, defence, migration, multilateralism. It contains nothing that was not already in the protocol of the previous summit. That is not a weakness of the document. It is its function.

I. The protocol as a genre

Anyone reading an EU summit protocol for the first time might gain the impression of reading political declarations of intent. Anyone reading one for the second time — a year later, at a different summit — notices that he is reading the same thing. The sentences are similar. The demands are the same. The deadlines have been postponed. The tone is unchanged.

"The European Council reaffirms its continued firm and unwavering support for Ukraine's independence, sovereignty and territorial integrity within its internationally recognised borders." This sentence, or its functional equivalent, appears in every EU summit protocol since February 2022. It does not mean that nothing happens — sanctions packages are adopted, funds transferred, weapons delivered. It means that the protocol itself is not an instrument of action. It is an instrument of confirmation. One confirms what one has already confirmed, and thereby prepares the next confirmation.

This is not a criticism of Europe. It is a description of what institutions do when they are under pressure and cannot find consensus: they produce text. The text is the consensus — not about what is done, but about what is said. As long as everyone signs the same text, the institution is intact.

II. What the protocol of 19 March actually contains

The document has two layers. The foreign-policy sections — Ukraine, Iran, Gaza, Lebanon — are signal text. They display a stance, process events, produce legitimacy. They contain hardly anything new. The demand that Russia agree to a "full, unconditional and immediate ceasefire" is the same demand as last time. That it was not complied with has changed nothing — except that it will be made again next time.

The economic-policy sections, by contrast — "One Europe, One Market", simplification, defence, investment union — do contain something concrete: named deadlines, named legislative projects, named mandates. An optional 28th company-law regime for businesses by the end of 2026. A single system for the cross-border provision of services by June 2026. An "Industrial Accelerator Act" based on the Commission's proposal of 4 March 2026.

This is the Draghi reflex: the realisation that Europe is falling behind economically has led to an unusual concreteness in the single-market chapters. The question is not whether these projects will be adopted. The question is whether the deadlines set will hold this time — or whether they will reappear, slightly postponed, at the next summit.

"The European Council calls on the Member States and the EU institutions to prevent and remove barriers to the four freedoms, notably the barriers identified in the Commission's Single Market Strategy of May 2025, with a view to achieving concrete and tangible progress by March 2027 at the latest."
— Final communiqué, EU summit, 19 March 2026, §43

March 2027. That is twelve months. At the summit in March 2027 progress will be assessed. And then a new communiqué will be drafted calling for "concrete and tangible progress" by March 2028. Every reader may answer for himself the question of how self-evident this sentence is.

III. The grammar of non-commitment

EU summit protocols have a grammar of their own, which one must learn to read. The verb "calls on" is the central modal verb of institutional non-commitment. Whoever is "called on" to do something is not obliged to do it. He is invited. He can decline without formally rejecting the invitation. The institution has spoken. What follows is up to the members.

"Invites", "underlines", "reiterates", "stresses", "recalls" — the protocol is rich in verbs that express a stance without compelling action. The only truly binding instrument of the EU — the regulation — rarely appears in the protocol. What does appear are calls on the Commission and the co-legislators to put forward proposals, which can then be debated, amended, delayed or rejected.

This is not a weakness of the system — it is its design. The EU is a consensus structure of twenty-seven sovereigns who voluntarily give up parts of their sovereignty. The protocol is the visible expression of this compromise: it contains what there is agreement on — and is silent about what there is none on. Migration, for example, has shrunk to a single brief paragraph. That does not mean the subject is unimportant. It means that no text on it was possible that everyone could sign.

IV. What going round in circles means

The "endless going round in circles" — a phrase that most aptly describes those who watch from outside — is, from the inside, not a failure. It is stability. The institution goes round in circles because that is the price of consensus. Twenty-seven sovereigns can act together only if the action is formulated so that no one drops out. That costs speed, sharpness, consistency. It costs the ability to let in anything new — because the new is, by definition, not yet capable of consensus.

This applies to the European Council just as much as to the STEM activist with five social-media profiles. Both are insider bodies. Both operate within networks in which reputation, belonging and reciprocity are the currency. Whoever does not belong to this network receives — if he is lucky — a polite rejection. If he is not lucky, he receives nothing at all.

The EU protocol of 19 March 2026 is a good document. It sums up what Europe thinks and wants — as far as there is agreement. It names deadlines that may be met. It formulates positions that may have an effect. But, like each of its predecessors, it contains no sentence that asks the question: Whom have we not reached so far? Who has developed something that we do not see because it does not fit into any form?

This question is not in the protocol. Nor was it in the last protocol. It will not be in the next protocol.

"Nothing is permitted to one. One need only permit it to oneself; then the others will put up with it or not."
— Johann Wolfgang von Goethe

The sentence is old. The insight it describes is older than all protocols. The system does not wait for anyone to knock. It does not wait for anyone to write. It does not wait at all. It keeps turning — and whoever does not turn with it simply turns alone. That is no consolation. It is the condition under which everything essential has ever come into being.
