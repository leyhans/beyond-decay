Same procedure as every year

The climate conference as Dinner for One

On 9 November the thirty-first world climate conference opens in Antalya. We have written about these conferences before: about thirty years without a bend in the curve, about the change of course at Rio in 1992, about how narratives are switched and about measuring at the wrong end. This time we are not concerned with what is negotiated. We are concerned with the form. The form is fixed before the first speech is given.

I. The butler

Every New Year’s Eve, German regional television shows an old lady sitting at a long table. Miss Sophie is celebrating her ninetieth birthday. Four friends are invited: Sir Toby, Admiral von Schneider, Mr Pommeroy and Mr Winterbottom. The chairs are empty. The friends have been dead for years.

Nobody minds. The butler, James, walks round the table, speaks for each guest in a put-on voice and drinks for each guest. Sherry with the soup, white wine with the fish, champagne with the chicken, port with the fruit, four glasses every time. On his way to the table lies a tiger skin, and James trips over its head, every time. Before each course he asks whether things should go as they did last year. Miss Sophie answers: “The same procedure as every year, James.”

The sketch was recorded in Hamburg in 1963. Since 31 December 1972 it has been broadcast every New Year’s Eve. In the same year the Club of Rome published “The Limits to Growth”, and Stockholm hosted the first United Nations conference on the environment. That is a coincidence. But both rituals have grown old together.

II. The guests at the table

Anyone who sits at the table of the climate conference can count the chairs.

In 1995 in Berlin, at the first conference, fewer than four thousand delegates came, according to reports. Two years later the Kyoto Protocol promised binding reductions for the industrialised countries. In 2009 in Copenhagen a successor treaty was to be agreed, and none was. In 2015 in Paris the parties agreed to limit warming, if possible, to 1.5 degrees. In 2023 in Dubai the text said for the first time that the world would transition away from fossil fuels.

In 2025 in Belém the words were no longer in the final text. Brazil had wanted a roadmap for leaving coal, oil and gas, and more than eighty states supported it. In the end the closing declaration was called “Global Mutirão”, collective effort, and fossil fuels did not appear in it. The roadmap moved into side declarations outside the negotiations.

Instead Belém created a “Belém Mission to 1.5”. It is to report back in Antalya. A few days before that decision the Global Carbon Project had published its annual report. Its lead author, Pierre Friedlingstein, said: “With CO2 emissions still increasing, keeping global warming below 1.5°C is no longer plausible.” The remaining budget of 170 billion tonnes of CO₂ will be gone before 2030 at the current rate.

So the mission reports to a guest who no longer sits at the table. James would bow and answer for him.

This year one chair is empty in a different way. For the conference in Belém the United States, for the first time in the history of these conferences, sent no official delegation. On 27 January 2026 its exit from the Paris Agreement took effect, and the White House announced that it would also leave the Framework Convention itself. The world’s second-largest emitter has not died. It has simply got up and left. Dinner is served all the same.

III. The tiger’s head

The course of a climate conference is as fixed as the path from the sideboard to the table. One can write it down in advance without speculating, because it has been documented thirty times:

  • At the start, a record announcement about attendance. Dubai in 2023 had more than eighty thousand registered participants.
  • A report on the lobby. For Belém the coalition Kick Big Polluters Out counted 1,602 fossil fuel lobbyists, one participant in every twenty-five. They received two thirds more passes than the delegates of the ten most climate-vulnerable countries combined. Only Brazil itself sent more people.
  • In the second week, a draft with one strong sentence.
  • A night of overtime.
  • A final text without the strong sentence.
  • The presidency calls the result historic, the environmental groups call it disappointing.
  • A reference to the next conference.

That is the tiger’s head. Everybody knows where it lies. Everybody trips over it. The audience no longer laughs, but it does not switch off either.

IV. Miss Sophie

In the sketch the evening belongs to Miss Sophie. She wants to celebrate her birthday as she always has, with her friends, even though the friends are gone. She gets what she wants.

Who is Miss Sophie at the climate conference? A look at the hosts helps. In 2023 the head of the Emirates’ state oil company presided over the conference in Dubai. In 2024 came Baku, the capital of an oil state.

This year, for the first time, the presidency is split. Türkiye hosts, Australia leads the negotiations. According to a report by 350.org, Türkiye is Europe’s largest coal-power generator, and its energy plan foresees about thirty per cent more coal and gas power capacity by 2035. According to its own geological survey, Australia is the world’s second-largest coal exporter, after Indonesia, with a quarter of world trade. In the third quarter of 2025 it exported more thermal coal than ever before. Australia’s chief negotiator, Chris Bowen, said, according to the Guardian, that the very fact that Australia was itself a major exporter of fossil fuels gave it credibility in pressing the petrostates to do more.

We do not accuse anyone of sabotaging the conference. We do not need to. It is enough to note who sits at the head of the table. The evening is celebrated as it always has been, and the hostess goes to bed content.

V. The curve

Outside the dining room, a different account is kept.

In 1995, the year of the first conference, global CO₂ emissions from fossil fuels were about 23 billion tonnes a year. The air held about 360 parts of CO₂ per million. For 2025 the Global Carbon Project expects 38.1 billion tonnes, a new record, and 425.7 parts per million, 52 per cent above pre-industrial levels. Consumption of all three fuels rose: coal, oil and gas.

There is progress too, and we do not hide it. Thirty-five countries have reduced their emissions while their economies grew, twice as many as ten years ago. Wind and sun are cheaper than coal in many places. Deforestation in the Amazon is at its lowest since 2014. But the curve of total emissions shows no bend in thirty years that could be attributed to a conference. We have documented this elsewhere in detail, and 2025 has not changed it.

An everyday comparison: for thirty years a man has gone to a slimming group every January. He weighs himself, listens to talks, signs a resolution. Today he weighs considerably more than at the first meeting. The group did not make him fat. But it did not make him thin either. He still goes back every January, because going there feels like losing weight.

VI. What the ritual is good for

What follows is our conclusion from the evidence, not a statement of fact.

A ritual does not exist to change anything. It exists so that nothing has to change and everyone still has a part to play. Governments show that they are acting. Environmental groups get a stage and a date for their outrage. Industry sits at the table and is first to learn what is in the draft. The media get a two-week story with suspense, an all-night session and an ending. The hosts get a summit photo. And nobody is in charge of the curve.

The ritual turns a problem into a procedure. A problem demands a solution. A procedure only demands to be carried out. As long as it is carried out, the problem counts as being dealt with. This is the same mechanism we described in measuring at the exhaust pipe: the indicator takes the place of the goal. Here the conference takes the place of the exit.

On one point the sketch is more honest than the conference. James knows the chairs are empty. He plays the part out of loyalty to an old lady, and he pays for it with drunkenness. At the climate conference everyone acts as if the guests were still there: the 1.5-degree goal, the exit from fossil fuels, future generations. And in the end it is not the butler who has the hangover. It is those who were never invited.

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo (Anthropic) — Nuremberg, 8 November 2026.
