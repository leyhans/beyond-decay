Forty Minutes

Where the productivity gain since 1991 has gone

What took an hour of work in 1991 takes a good forty minutes today. Anyone who wants to know where the other twenty went can find nearly all of them. What has disappeared is something else.

I. The question

In Paris the president wants to write a debt rule into the constitution; in Berlin the quarrel is over pensions, basic income support and health insurance. Both debates are about who should go without. Neither asks what has become of what was added.

A great deal was added. According to the figures of the Federal Statistical Office, an hour of work in Germany today produces 48 percent more than in 1991. A loaf of bread, a tax return, a gearbox, an X-ray: averaged over everything made and done in the country, forty minutes are enough for what took an hour then.

II. The staircase

The path of this gain can be followed step by step. All figures cover 1991 to 2025 and are adjusted for prices.

  • The hour of work produces 48 percent more.
  • It costs the employer 43 percent more, measured in the prices of what is produced.
  • Measured in the prices of what a household buys, it is 38 percent.
  • Per employee and month, 22 percent of that remains, because twelve percent fewer hours are worked per head.
  • Net, after taxes and contributions, it is 19 percent.

48 becomes 19. That is half a percent a year, and it explains why progress stands in the statistics and is hardly noticed in households.

III. What is not theft

The first step is small. Wages per hour have almost kept up with what the hour produces. The employees' share of national income stood at just under 75 percent in 2025, according to an analysis by the German Economic Institute, higher than at any time since reunification. The institute is close to the employers and names the reason itself: the share rises mainly in bad years, because profits collapse then. Between 2003 and 2007 it had fallen to 65 percent. The national accounts show no lasting shift from wages to profits for Germany. In most other rich countries it is different; in 2017 the OECD measured a falling labour share in two thirds of them.

The largest step is the third, and it is time. In 1991 just under one employee in five worked part-time; today it is four in ten. A large part of the gain was therefore taken not as money but as hours. Whether voluntarily is another question: someone who works part-time because the nursery closes at two has gained nothing.

The last step is the levies. The social insurance contribution rate has risen from 35.6 to 42.3 percent since reunification. Today's hour of work thus pays for more years of retirement and more medicine than that of 1991. That too is use, not loss.

IV. Where it seeps away

What arrives on average does not arrive with everyone. 16 percent of all jobs were low-wage in April 2025.

And what arrives does not stay with everyone. The German Institute for Economic Research has followed rents since 1990. On average, tenant households paid around 27 percent of their income for basic rent in 2021, and since about 2005 that share has hardly risen. In the lowest fifth of incomes it rose from 24 to 36 percent. There the gain of thirty years has ended up with the landlord. A study of house prices in fourteen countries since 1870 explains why: around 80 percent of the rise since the Second World War goes back to land, not to building costs. Germany had the weakest rise of all. We described the mechanism in “The Banana and the Land”.

Within the economy the gain migrates as well. How suppliers rationalise and the customer collects the yield through the price is told in “López’s Warriors”.

V. The originator

That leaves the question of what those receive from whom the gain comes. The economist William Nordhaus asked it in 2004 for the American economy from 1948 to 2001. He calculated what part of the total benefit of technical innovations is kept by the firms that bring them about. His result: about 2.2 percent. The rest went to buyers through falling prices.

One can read that as a success, and Nordhaus does. A television costs a fraction of what it once did because no one was able to keep the progress to himself. The reverse side stands in the same figure. Whoever receives two percent cannot pay for the next development out of the last. A corporation makes up for that with market power. The individual inventor does not appear in the calculation at all; he is a part of the two percent. We know of no comparable estimate for Germany.

VI. The source

Which brings us to what has really disappeared. The gain itself is shrinking.

  • 1991 to 2000: plus 20 percent
  • 2000 to 2010: plus 9 percent
  • 2010 to 2019: plus 11 percent
  • 2019 to 2025: plus 2 percent

In the nineties the hour of work gained as much in nine years as in the twenty-five that followed. Since 2019 it has almost stood still. The staircase in section II distributes an increase of which four tenths date from the first decade.

That changes the debate with which this text began. An ageing society can pay its pensions and its debts from two sources: it takes something away from someone, or the hour produces more. The first is being fought over in Paris and Berlin. The second is the business of those to whom the system leaves two percent, and in the factories, as we showed in “The Vanishing Link”, a tangible productivity gain often goes uncollected for years because it devalues existing plant.

Whoever asks who should make the savings has chosen the more comfortable question. The uncomfortable one is why the hour has not produced more for six years.
