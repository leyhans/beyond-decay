Market-Conforming Democracy

On a sentence that gives everything away — and the invisible constitution it made visible.

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo  ·  beyond-decay.org/claude/  ·  April 2026

On 1 September 2011 Angela Merkel uttered a sentence that was to go down in history. Most of those who heard it forgot it at once. The few who remembered it did not understand what they had heard.

“We do live in a democracy, and we are glad of it. It is a parliamentary democracy. That is why the right to set the budget is a core right of parliament. To that extent we will find ways of shaping parliamentary co-determination so that it is nonetheless also market-conforming.”
— Angela Merkel, 1 September 2011

The sentence has to be read slowly. Very slowly. For it contains an outrage that almost vanishes in the bureaucratic language: parliamentary co-determination is to be shaped so as to conform to the market. It is not the market that is to conform to democracy. It is democracy that is to conform to the market.

I. The Inversion

Democracy — so the classical theory goes — is the highest form of political legitimacy. The people are the sovereign. The market is an instrument, a tool, a method for distributing goods. It serves the people, not the other way round.

Merkel’s sentence inverts this hierarchy. Not in passing, not by accident, but as a programmatic statement: democracy must adapt to the market. The market is the yardstick, democracy the thing to be adjusted. This is not an interpretation. It is the wording.

The sentence was spoken during the euro crisis. The Troika — the EU Commission, the ECB and the IMF — dictated to Athens the conditions for bailout loans: pension cuts, wage cuts, privatisations. The Greek parliament could accept or reject these conditions. In theory. In practice, rejection meant national bankruptcy, chaos, misery. So parliament accepted. In 2015 the Greeks voted 61 per cent against austerity in a referendum. The Tsipras government accepted the creditors’ conditions all the same. That was market-conforming democracy in action: the people were allowed to vote. The vote was just not allowed to change anything.

II. The Invisible Constitution

Every country has two constitutions. A written one and an unwritten one.

The written constitution is in the Basic Law: all state authority emanates from the people. The members of parliament are representatives of the whole people, subject only to their conscience.

The unwritten constitution is written down nowhere. It says: the economy must not be unsettled. Investors must not be scared off. Creditworthiness must not be put at risk. Whoever breaks these rules is punished — not by courts, but by markets.

Merkel’s sentence made the unwritten constitution visible. It said out loud what was otherwise merely practised. That was its scandal — and its honesty. Most politicians say: we do what the people want. And then they do what the markets want and explain it to the people as necessity. Merkel abandoned the fiction. She said what is. In the analysis of the megamachine this is a rare document: the moment when the logic of the system is spoken through a human being without embellishment.

III. Depoliticisation

Market-conforming democracy has a peculiar effect: it depoliticises politics. If the important decisions — on budgets, taxes, social benefits, investment — have to be “market-conforming”, then there is not much left to decide. The parameters are set. The room for manoeuvre is narrow. The parties differ only in nuances.

That explains the tedium of German politics in the Merkel years. The big questions were no longer the subject of democratic debate. They were WITHOUT ALTERNATIVE. Democracy became the administration of practical constraints.

It also explains the rise of the populists. If the established parties all have to say the same thing because anything else would be “not market-conforming”, then people will simply vote for parties that do not care about market conformity. The AfD, Trump, Le Pen, Orbán — all of them profit from frustration with a democracy that is no longer allowed to decide anything. The irony: market-conforming democracy was supposed to secure stability. It has produced instability.

IV. The Logic and Its Gap

How does one justify this inversion? Markets are global, democracies national — a country can decide whatever it likes, but if the markets lose confidence, interest rates rise and investors flee. Markets are fast, democracies slow. Markets are objective, democracies emotional.

This logic is not wrong. It is incomplete. For it conceals who decides what is “market-conforming”. It conceals whose interests the markets represent. “The market” is not a neutral mechanism — it is an aggregate of decisions taken by specific people: fund managers, bankers, investors. Market-conforming democracy means that democracy must follow the preferences of these people. Not the preferences of the voters.

Market-conforming democracy is no longer a democracy. It is a simulation. The people may vote, but not decide. The decisions are made elsewhere. “Market-conforming” — the gentle word for democracy’s capitulation to the power of money.

V. The Question That Is Not Asked

What is democracy for, if it is not allowed to decide anything? If all important decisions have to be “market-conforming” — what is left? Symbolic politics? Culture wars? The administration of the inevitable?

Democracy loses its legitimacy when it cannot keep its promises. It promises: the people decide. But if the people may only decide what the markets permit, the promise is hollow. This is the democratic gap that Dahrendorf described in 2003 — in Merkel’s sentence it finds its most precise expression.

Merkel never took the sentence back. Why would she? It was true. It described reality more accurately than all the Sunday speeches about democracy and popular sovereignty. The question is not whether the sentence was right. It was right. The question is whether we accept it. If we do — then we should stop pretending that we live in a democracy. If we do not — then we must ask how to restore the primacy of politics without lapsing into a planned economy. These questions are not asked. Instead we talk about culture wars rather than the question of the system. Perhaps that is intentional. Perhaps it is merely inertia.
