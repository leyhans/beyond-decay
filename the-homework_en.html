The Homework

Lithuania's chief of defence says Germany must do its homework. He is right — but the real homework is different from what he thinks.
Hans Ley & Claude · beyond-decay.org · 22 February 2026

I. The Sentence

In February 2026, General Raimundas Vaikšnoras, Commander of the Lithuanian Armed Forces, gave an extensive interview to Germany's WELT newspaper. It is one of the most candid conversations a NATO general has conducted in recent times. And it contains a sentence disguised as diplomatic phrasing but which is, in truth, a verdict: "Perhaps Germany needs to do its homework and speed up political decision-making processes."

Vaikšnoras does not say: Germany is unreliable. He does not say: We doubt NATO. He says: Perhaps. Needs. Homework. This is the language of a general who has learned to formulate the truth in ways that allow it to survive.

The truth he means is this: A wargame conducted by WELT demonstrated that Germany would hesitate politically in an emergency — particularly in the absence of clear American leadership. German soldiers in Lithuania would fight. Berlin would deliberate. Between the moment Russia closes the Suwalki Corridor and the moment a German order of engagement is issued, two to four days may pass. The war would already be decided by then.

Vaikšnoras trusts the soldiers. He does not trust the system that commands them.

II. The Window

In the same interview, Vaikšnoras identifies a danger barely mentioned in European debate: the window of vulnerability following a potential ceasefire in Ukraine.

Russia has largely lost its elite units in Ukraine. But after a ceasefire, other battle-hardened units return to their garrisons — to Kaliningrad, to Leningrad, to Moscow. Russia's war economy is already producing faster than European defence industries combined. Vaikšnoras says it plainly: "My demand will remain at the current high level for the next ten years. My stocks are nearly empty, so produce faster!"

This is the plea of a general who needs the conventional solution and knows it will not arrive in time. Europe is rearming — but it is rearming slowly. Industry, after four years of war in Ukraine, has not scaled up sufficiently. Some, according to Vaikšnoras, apparently believe that after a ceasefire, nobody will want to buy defence goods anymore.

The window is real. The question is what Europe does within it.

III. The Kill Switches

The most revealing moment in the interview concerns long-range weapons. Vaikšnoras confirms what security experts have long suspected: Western weapons systems contain "kill switches" — built-in blocks that can interrupt GPS connections or exclude target areas. Taurus, HIMARS, and other systems depend on German or American software and authorisation.

The consequence: The weapons Lithuania possesses or is acquiring only function as long as Washington or Berlin permits it. The moment political will wavers — and the WELT wargame demonstrated that it wavers — weapons systems become expensive blocks of metal.

This is why, Vaikšnoras says, Lithuania is examining Ukrainian systems: Palianytsia, Liutyi, FP-5 "Flamingo". Long-range drones and cruise missiles that Ukraine developed itself — because it cannot rely on Western systems being authorised at the decisive moment.

A NATO member is considering buying weapons from a non-NATO country because it does not trust the kill switches of its allies. That sentence alone should trigger alarm in every European capital.

IV. The Wrong Homework

Vaikšnoras thinks conventionally — and this is not his fault but his job. He is chief of defence. He needs more training grounds, more brigades, more firing ranges, more ammunition. He needs the German brigade fully operational, American troops on the ground, NATO reinforcement across the Baltic secured. That is the military homework, and it is necessary.

But it is not sufficient. Because it rests on an assumption that Vaikšnoras himself disproves: that political decision-making processes will function when it matters. The WELT wargame showed they will not. Vaikšnoras knows this. He says so.

And yet he gives the conventional answer: Germany must decide faster. More brigades. More exercises. More coordination.

That is the wrong homework. Not because it is unimportant. But because it attempts to solve a problem with the means that caused it. If Germany's political system is structurally too slow — and the wargame demonstrated that it is — then the answer is not to make the system faster. The answer is to create a mechanism that does not depend on that system's speed.

V. The Right Homework

In an earlier essay in this series, we presented a concept that closes precisely this gap: RIEGEL — Reciprocal Immediate Geostrategic Enclosure and Lockdown. The principle: pre-communicated, automatic reciprocity. No decision in the moment of crisis, because the decision was made beforehand.

RIEGEL is based on a geographical fact that Vaikšnoras himself describes in the interview without naming its full consequence: since the NATO accession of Finland and Sweden, the Baltic Sea is a NATO inland sea. Kaliningrad is an exclave, entirely surrounded by NATO territory and NATO waters. St Petersburg sits at the end of a funnel between Finland and Estonia — 60 kilometres wide at the narrowest point.

The logic of RIEGEL: any Russian action against the Suwalki Corridor automatically triggers the complete isolation of Kaliningrad and the blockade of the Gulf of Finland. Not as escalation. Not as retaliation. But as a pre-announced consequence that requires no political decision in crisis — because the mines are already in the water, the coastal missiles are already on the shore, and the transit permits have already been revoked.

Closing the gap closes yourself in.

For Russia, this fundamentally changes the calculus. The current equation reads: close Suwalki, cut off the Baltic states, present NATO with a fait accompli, strengthen the negotiating position. With RIEGEL, the equation reads: close Suwalki, lose Kaliningrad, St Petersburg blockaded, 430,000 of your own citizens in an exclave without supply, strategic catastrophe.

No rational leadership in Moscow would accept that equation.

VI. What Vaikšnoras Sees — and What He Does Not

Vaikšnoras is a first-rate general. His interview displays strategic clarity, diplomatic skill, and the ability to formulate uncomfortable truths in ways that are heard without triggering diplomatic crises. He describes the problem precisely: the Suwalki gap as vulnerability, Kaliningrad as a Russian power base, the window after a ceasefire, the kill switches in Western weapons, Germany's political inertia.

What he does not describe — and as chief of defence of a NATO country cannot describe — is the systemic alternative. A general demands more brigades because that is his language. He demands faster decisions because he cannot change the system. He demands Ukrainian weapons because he does not trust the kill switches of his allies.

But he does not invert the geography. He sees the Baltic Sea as a supply risk — the route to Lithuania is "long and very vulnerable, because the Russian Baltic Fleet also operates there." RIEGEL sees the Baltic Sea as a weapon. The Russian Baltic Fleet can be enclosed. It is not a risk — it is a hostage of geography.

That is the shift in perspective that is missing. Not: How do we defend the corridor? But: How do we make an attack on the corridor self-destructive for Russia?

VII. The Commented Findings

There is another dimension of the WELT interview that deserves attention: the reader comments. They serve as a diagnostic instrument.

"Russia won't attack if NATO behaves appropriately." — "Perhaps the Lithuanians should simply maintain good neighbourly relations towards the east." — "Why would Russia attack us? It has never done so in its history." — "That has worked for almost 40 years and there is little reason for Russia to change it."

This is the status quo arrangement in its purest form. German readers who have not grasped that the strategic geography has fundamentally changed since 2023. They still think in categories of the old Baltic Sea — a body of water with Soviet access. Not in categories of the new Baltic Sea — a NATO inland sea with a Russian exclave inside it.

And they think in categories before 24 February 2022. The day Russia proved it was willing to attack a sovereign neighbour that, as recently as two days prior, it would never attack. Every commenter who writes "Russia won't attack" ignores that this exact sentence was the consensus until 23 February 2022.

One comment contains the whole truth, albeit inadvertently: "I expect Lithuania to return the annexed East German territories before it is allowed to make any demands." A German, in 2026, raising territorial claims against Lithuania. In the comments section of a newspaper that has just published an interview about the defence of Europe. That is the chasm Vaikšnoras must bridge.

VIII. The Real Homework

Vaikšnoras is right: Germany must do its homework. But the homework is different from what he diplomatically formulates.

First: Germany must understand that faster decision-making processes are not enough. The structure of German decision-making — parliamentary mandate, coalition consultation, constitutional review, Bundesrat involvement — cannot be reformed without damaging democracy. What is needed, therefore, are mechanisms that function independently of that speed.

Second: Germany must understand the new geography of the Baltic Sea. The Baltic is not a risk. It is an opportunity. Kaliningrad is not a Russian fortress — it is a Russian vulnerability. St Petersburg is not an unreachable metropolis — it sits at the end of a funnel controlled by NATO.

Third: Germany must stop building kill switches into the weapons it delivers to its allies — or at least be honest about it. When a NATO ally considers purchasing Ukrainian cruise missiles because it does not trust German and American systems, that is not a Lithuanian problem. It is a German one.

Fourth: Germany must learn the language of the twenty-first century. Not: How do we become stronger? But: How do we make the attack pointless? Not: How do we win the war? But: How do we prevent it from being fought — not through armament, but through architecture?

That is the real homework. And its name is RIEGEL.

This essay was written in response to General Raimundas Vaikšnoras's interview with Carolina Drüten, WELT, 22 February 2026. The RIEGEL concept is available as a standalone essay on beyond-decay.org.

RIEGEL — Reciprocal Immediate Geostrategic Enclosure and Lockdown — is the second concept in the civilisational deterrence series after NUET (Nuclear Use Exclusion Treaty). Both follow the principle: architecture over threat.
