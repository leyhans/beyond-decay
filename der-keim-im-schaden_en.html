The Seed in the Damage

Can Trump, without meaning to, move democracy forward?

Can a president who damages democracy do more for it than many before him? It is conceivable. But only if others draw the consequences from the damage.

I. The Question

In everything negative there seems to lie the seed of a change for the better. A forest fire makes room for young trees, an illness teaches the body to defend itself, a bankruptcy forces a new start. From this follows an uncomfortable question: could Donald Trump, without meaning to, do more for the further development of democracy than many presidents before him?

The question is meant seriously. It excuses nothing and plays nothing down. It only asks what can become of a damage.

II. The Precedent of Watergate

American history knows the pattern. Richard Nixon abused the power of his office as no president before him had: a break-in at his political opponents' headquarters, wiretapping, hush money, misuse of the tax authority and the intelligence services. His fall was followed within a few years by a whole series of laws. In 1973 Congress limited the president's power to wage war without its consent. In 1974 the Federal Election Commission was created to supervise campaign finance. In 1978 came the ethics laws for officeholders, independent inspectors general in the departments, and a court that must approve surveillance by the intelligence services.

Nixon involuntarily did more for the control of power than many a president who wanted it. Germany knows the pattern too, only in a harsher form: the Basic Law is the answer to the failure of Weimar and to everything that came after.

III. What Trump Makes Visible

In 2018 the political scientists Steven Levitsky and Daniel Ziblatt showed that democracies live less by their constitutions than by two unwritten rules: that one recognises the opponent as legitimate, and that one does not exhaust one's own power to the limit even where the law would allow it. Such rules are like trust within a family. One notices them only when someone breaks them.

Trump breaks them openly. In doing so he shows how much of what was thought secure was only habit. That the president does not turn the justice system against his opponents, that he does not send the National Guard into a governor's cities against the governor's will, that he does not put pressure on election authorities: much of this stood in no law. Every reform begins with this insight. What is not written down cannot be defended.

IV. What Is Stirring

Countervailing forces are stirring too, and some of them are new. The states are organising. A network of Democratic state legislators says it has around 1,800 members in all 50 states, and it has explicitly taken as its model its opponents, who have drafted legislation for the states together for decades. Governors and state attorneys general have taken the administration to court, over the National Guard and over voter data, and have often won. Individual states have better protected their elections: voters can track their ballot through the count, and voter registration records are better secured.

On 3 November voters cast their ballots on Congress for the first time in Trump's second term. What a Congress can and cannot do against a president we have described in "Negative Feedback Returns".

V. What Speaks Against It

None of this is yet a better democracy. Five objections weigh heavily.

First, the damage is real. In its study "Systemsprenger" of January 2026, the German Institute for International and Security Affairs (SWP) describes how Trump uses the government apparatus against his opponents and violates fundamental and civil rights. A whole chapter bears the heading "Causes of the Total Failure of Congress". What has been destroyed in a ministry in knowledge and independence does not return with the next election result.

Second, Watergate worked because Nixon fell. The reforms came when his own party dropped him. This time it has not done so. A damage that nobody acknowledges as damage teaches nothing.

Third, the counterattack too is not waged with democratic means alone. Both parties now redraw electoral districts as it suits them. The civil rights organisation Common Cause speaks of an accelerating arms race. A crisis can also brutalise those who resist it.

Fourth, the voter measures something other than one hopes. According to AP's large voter survey, prices in the shops were decisive above all, less the state of democracy. A sensor that measures the supermarket would have reacted just the same against a president who strengthened democracy and let prices rise.

Fifth, Weimar shows the price. The lessons drawn from it came after the catastrophe, not in its place.

VI. More Than Many Before Him?

That is conceivable, for many presidents neither damaged democracy nor moved it forward. But an unintended push hardly reaches the best of them. Abraham Lincoln waged a civil war at whose end slavery was abolished. In 1965 Lyndon B. Johnson signed the Voting Rights Act, which gave millions of Black Americans in the South the vote in the first place. Both expanded democracy deliberately, against great resistance. An unintended push would have to set a great deal in motion to come close to that.

His supporters, incidentally, see it the other way round. For them Trump strengthens democracy by taking power back for the people from an apparatus nobody elected. One has to know this reading, even if the evidence is against it. That America did not fall ill only with Trump, and that he is the symptom of an older illness, we have shown in "Is Trump an Accident?".

VII. The Seed and the Soil

The thought of the seed in the negative is right if one grasps it precisely. Trump destroys procedures that people relied on. In doing so he opens processes that were not possible before, because nobody saw a reason to touch the procedures. We have described the difference between the two in "Process and Procedure".

What becomes of these processes is not for him to decide. It is decided by those who are now writing new rules: in the states, in the new Congress, perhaps only after him. The reforms after Watergate had names and dates. As long as there are no such names and dates for the time after Trump, he is not an involuntary reformer but merely a damage.

The seed is there. Whether it sprouts depends on the soil.
