The Price of Competence

What Singapore Can Do, What It Costs and What Can Be Transferred

Addendum to: Are Democracy and Competence Incompatible?

Singapore is the country people point to when they want to prove that good government does not need the possibility of being voted out. Looking more closely, one finds both: an achievement that must be admired, and a bill no one should want to pay.

I. A Sentence That Was Too Short

In our essay on democracy and competence we devoted a single sentence to Singapore: it was “governed competently, but by the same party since 1959. Whether it would also survive a bad successor, it has never had to show.” In “Zona Nova”, Singapore stood beside Hong Kong, Shenzhen and Dubai as an example of what a territory with its own rules can achieve. Both times we left open what this success costs. We make up for that here.

II. What Works

In May 1961 a district of wooden huts burned down in Bukit Ho Swee. 16,000 people lost their homes. The state built housing blocks on the same site, and the emergency programme became the housing policy of the whole country. Today 77 per cent of residents live in flats built by the state Housing and Development Board. 91 per cent of households live in homes they own, mostly on 99-year leases, paid for from a compulsory savings account into which employees and employers pay. In Germany just under half of households live in their own homes.

The second example is water. Singapore has no rivers or lakes that could supply a country. For a long time its water came from Malaysia, through pipes across the Johor causeway. An agreement from 1962 secures it until 2061. Malaysian politicians have repeatedly reminded the island that they could turn off the tap. Singapore did not answer with threats but with technology: rainwater collected from two thirds of its land area, seawater desalination, and purified wastewater which, under the name NEWater, now covers around 40 per cent of demand. A vulnerability to blackmail became an industry that today sells its plants to other countries.

Compare this with Germany, which until 2022 obtained more than half of its natural gas from a single country and refused to see any risk in that.

III. What It Costs

The price is not in the glossy reports, but it is documented.

It begins with the opposition. The Internal Security Act permits detention without trial. For decades, government opponents were hit with defamation suits by heads of government until they were insolvent and lost their seats. Whoever resists does not end up in prison but in bankruptcy. That is quieter, but no less effective.

It continues with the question of who manages the country’s wealth. From 2004 to 2021 Ho Ching ran the sovereign fund Temasek, which today manages hundreds of billions. She is the wife of Lee Hsien Loong, who was prime minister during the same period, and the daughter-in-law of the state’s founder. The government always stated that she was appointed on merit and did not report to her husband. No one outside could check this. Her salary was never published.

It is clearest among those who build the city. At the end of 2024 around 1.17 million people worked in Singapore on a basic work permit, among them just over 300,000 domestic workers and more than 450,000 workers on construction sites, in shipyards and in industry. There is no minimum wage for them. They may not bring their families, and they have no path to permanent residence. The permit is tied to the employer. When the pandemic came in 2020, the dormitories were sealed off. The great majority of that year’s infections occurred in these dormitories, while the numbers among citizens outside stayed low.

And it shows where the state intervened in life itself. In 1983 Lee Kuan Yew complained that educated women had too few children. From 1984, the children of university graduates received priority in school admission, and women without school qualifications received 10,000 Singapore dollars if they were sterilised after their first or second child. Today the fertility rate is 0.87 children per woman, lower than ever. The government itself calls this a challenge to the country’s survival.

IV. The Vote That Did Happen

One detail is missing from most accounts, and for our question it is the most important. The graduate mothers’ programme caused outrage across all classes in 1984. In the election in December of that year the ruling party suffered clear losses, and the programme was withdrawn. Even in a country where the opposition hardly stands a chance, the ballot once corrected a mistake that no ministry would have corrected on its own.

This is exactly the mechanism we defended in “Are Democracy and Competence Incompatible?”: democracy does not choose the best, but it can stop the mistakes of the powerful. Singapore has not abolished this mechanism. It has made it so small that it only takes effect in the case of gross errors.

V. What Can Be Transferred

From this we draw conclusions, which we mark as such.

What can be transferred is what has to do with technology and administration. A state can create home ownership for the majority if it provides land, builds in large series and organises the saving for it. A country can turn a dependency into a capability if it treats it as a task and not as a law of nature. Neither requires caning or detention without trial. It requires persistence over decades, and Singapore had that because one party could pursue the same goals for sixty years.

What cannot be transferred is order through fear, calm through a silenced opposition, and prosperity that rests on an underclass without rights. Whoever cites Singapore as a model should say which part he means.

That leaves the question our first sentence left open. Singapore has never had to survive a bad successor. Its government today worries about a people that no longer has children, and that is not due to a bad successor but to what the good founder built. Competence protects against many mistakes. It protects least against its own.
