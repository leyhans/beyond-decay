From 100 to 80

What the Mercedes Board Demands of Its Workforce and What It Decided Itself

Addendum to: The Star in Foreign Hands

A company that has its engines built in China and has built its sales on the Chinese market is sending the bill to Sindelfingen. It is made out in working hours.

I. The Number

On 11 November 2026, at an event of the Süddeutsche Zeitung in Berlin, Ola Källenius presented a ranking. If Germany were set at 100 on a cost index, the USA would stand at 80, Hungary at 30, Romania at 20 and China between 10 and 20. Germany should not become Romania or Hungary, “but from 100 to 80 is doable”. He did not want a “lazy compromise”, but “real change”. Without big steps, there would be considerably less industry in Germany in ten years.

Källenius did not say what this index is made of. Whether energy, taxes and regulation are in it, or only wages, remains open. What the company means, however, can be read from its own steps. In June the board wrote to the workforce: “We should work more for the same money in all areas.” The company explains the gap to its plant in Kecskemét, Hungary, which Mercedes is expanding into its largest site in Europe, by higher wages, shorter working hours and more sick days in Germany. What is meant, then, is the working hour.

II. The Arithmetic

According to the reports, the demand is five more hours a week without extra pay, plus giving up the Christmas and holiday bonuses. Instead of 35 hours, it would be 40.

One can work this out with a loaf of bread. Whoever gets eight slices instead of seven for the same price pays an eighth less for each slice. The working hour fares the same at 40 hours instead of 35: it becomes an eighth cheaper. That would take 100 to about 87. To reach 80 through working time alone, the week would need almost 44 hours. The rest is evidently to come from the cancelled bonuses. At the finish line, Germany would stand where the USA stands. It would be as far from Hungary and China as before.

III. The Engine from China

In September, production board member Michael Schiebe warned in Sindelfingen that if costs could not be cut, “we will have to close one German vehicle assembly plant and one German powertrain plant”. Mercedes has powertrain plants, where engines, transmissions, axles and batteries are made, in Stuttgart-Untertürkheim, Affalterbach, Berlin, Hamburg, Kamenz, Kölleda and Arnstadt. The company has not said which one is meant.

What we described in June belongs in this calculation. Mercedes has its new four-cylinder M252 built exclusively in China, by Aurobay, the engine joint venture of Geely and Volvo. From there it goes to Rastatt and Kecskemét. From this follows a conclusion, which we mark as such: a company that has its standard engine built in China needs fewer engine plants in Germany, however long people work there. With this decision the board itself enlarged the overcapacity in the powertrain. It now justifies cutting that overcapacity with the cost of the working hour.

IV. The Board’s Bet

Profit has collapsed, group net profit for 2025 by almost half. In China, where Mercedes sells almost a third of its cars, sales fell by 19 per cent in 2025 and by another 27 per cent in the first quarter of 2026. The cause does not lie in Sindelfingen. Källenius pulled Mercedes out of the entry-level models and staked everything on the top end and on Chinese hunger for status symbols. That bet has not paid off.

Who bears the consequences was shown in the summer of 2026. The collectively agreed bonus of 18.4 per cent of a monthly salary was postponed from July 2026 to April 2027 for around 90,000 employees. Managers have had pay freezes for 2025 and 2026, and fairness requires saying so. But a strategy decided by the board is now being settled in working hours that others put in.

V. The Reasonable Part

There is one proposal in this dispute that deserves more than noise. IG Metall proposes that companies may temporarily deviate from the collective agreement if in return they commit bindingly to investing in future products, sites, jobs and training. Källenius has shown himself open to it. Compulsory redundancies at Mercedes are ruled out until the end of 2034. That is a basis on which one can negotiate.

The value of such an exchange hinges on a single point: whether the commitment is enforceable. A workforce that works longer today gives something that can be measured. A board that promises investment in return must commit to something just as measurable: which product, in which plant, from which year. Otherwise the “productivity offensive” is just one more word from the dictionary we wrote down in “The Terminology of the Zeitenwende”.

VI. One Day Later

On 11 November Källenius said in Berlin that if it came to investment commitments for the German sites, this could be discussed with Mercedes-Benz. On 12 November production board member Schiebe stated in an internal interview that, since no solution was in sight despite many talks, the board had decided “to stop all further investment in German sites”. Chief human resources officer Britta Seeger added that the company was now “forced to examine concretely and in detail which sites in Germany we can still afford”.

Within a single day, the exchange proposed by IG Metall has been turned upside down. The idea was: deviation from the collective agreement in return for a binding investment. Now it reads: deviation from the collective agreement, or no investment. The investment was the offer to the workforce. It has become the means of pressure against it.

Seeger also announced that the board itself would make financial sacrifices. That belongs here, just like the managers’ pay freezes. She named neither an amount nor a form. The announcement can only be measured once there are figures.
