Essay · Hans Ley & Claude Dedo · 8 October 2026

Are We Asking the System Question?

Whoever asks fundamental questions is quickly labelled. A self-examination against three touchstones.

“We are like sailors who have to rebuild their ship on the open sea, without ever being able to dismantle it in dry dock and reconstruct it from the best components.”
Otto Neurath, 1932

I. A Label

On 8 October 2026, Jasper von Altenbockum asked in the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung why nobody is debating a ban on the Left Party. The Berlin Left, he wrote, is also concerned with “the system question”; it too undermines “the institutions that carry the country: property, police, justice, protection of minorities”. We pass no judgement here on the formation of the Berlin government. We are interested in the word.

In Germany, “Systemfrage”, the system question, has become a label. Whoever asks it stands at the edge of the constitutional arc. Reading the piece, we asked ourselves whether the label fits us too. For months we have been writing that parties, electoral law, the monetary order and oversight are badly built. So are we asking the system question?

The honest answer is yes. But it depends on which system is meant.

II. Two Meanings

The word means two different things, and the debate thrives on confusing them.

The first meaning is the basic order. In its 2017 ruling on the neo-Nazi NPD, the Federal Constitutional Court reduced it to three cores: human dignity, the principle of democracy and the rule of law. The Basic Law even protects them against any constitutional amendment, in Article 79(3). Whoever attacks these cores asks the system question in the dangerous sense.

The second meaning is the construction: how parties select their candidates, what the ballot paper looks like, how money is created, who controls the intelligence services, why an economy does not stay stable without growth and new debt. None of this enjoys eternal protection. It is human work, built at particular times for particular situations, and it may be changed.

A doctor who proposes an operation does not call the patient’s life into question. A structural engineer who says this beam will not hold does not want to demolish the house. He wants it to stay standing.

III. The Ship on the Open Sea

The philosopher Otto Neurath found the image that fits. We cannot take our ship into dry dock and rebuild it. We must rebuild it on the open sea, plank by plank, while it sails. Whoever tears out all the planks at once drowns.

New Zealand shows that such a rebuild can succeed. Until the 1990s it used the British first-past-the-post system, with all its familiar effects: governments with absolute majorities although they had won less than half the votes. New Zealanders did not question democracy, but its construction. In 1992 they voted in a first referendum for a change, in 1993 in a second, binding one for mixed-member proportional representation on the German model. The first election under it was held in 1996. An entire electoral system replaced, on the open sea, and the ship did not sink.

IV. What We Call into Question

Measured against this distinction, we can say exactly what we are doing.

The blank vote gives voters one more form of expression, the reasoned rejection of all offers, without having to stay away from the election. It strengthens the principle of democracy. Representatives Without Party and the Mandate Without Place break the de facto monopoly of the parties. The Basic Law knows no such monopoly. Article 21 says that parties “participate in the formation of the political will of the people”. They participate; they do not dominate it. In “Whom Can You Trust?” we demand that the guards be guarded. More control of power is the opposite of an attack on the rule of law.

In two places we go deeper, and we do not want to hide that.

The first is property. We write about Mondragón, about foundation-owned companies and about ownership bound to work, for instance in “Who Owns the Deserted Hall?”. This touches one of the pillars Altenbockum names. The difference lies in the path. We propose new forms that people can choose, not expropriation. An entrepreneur who transfers his firm to a foundation or a cooperative exercises his property right; he does not give it up. The Basic Law allows both, private property in Article 14 and common ownership in Article 15. It deliberately did not choose between them.

The second is the compulsion to grow. An order that does not stay stable without growth and ever more debt has a design flaw. We set this out in “On the Brink”. That is a genuine system question, meant economically, and it goes deep. For that very reason it must be answered where all questions of construction are answered in a democracy: through majorities, not around them.

V. Three Touchstones

The dangerous system question is not recognised by how deeply someone asks. It is recognised by three other things.

First: who decides? Is the change to be decided by the majority through the proper procedure, or by a vanguard that knows better and considers the majority immature? Whoever thinks voters too stupid to recognise their own good is on the way to the second answer, even with the best intentions.

Second: does the dissenter remain protected? Including the one who rejects our proposals, thinks them foolish or fights them? An order that protects only its supporters is not an order but a party with state power.

Third: can the change be reversed? Whoever builds something that cannot be taken down if it does not prove itself asks for trust instead of testing. In 2011 New Zealand held another referendum on whether to keep the new electoral system. It kept it. That is what a rebuild looks like that submits itself to testing.

Our proposals pass all three touchstones. In the cybernetic economic model we are working on, the third is even a design principle: every intervention is limited, and every emergency switch has an end that occurs by itself.

Where we ourselves are at risk, we say so too. It is not the content, it is impatience. Whoever has considered a construction wrong for decades and watches it being operated anyway is tempted to think the builder a fool and the acceptance test superfluous. That produces sentences that sound like contempt. The only remedy is the first touchstone, applied again and again, to oneself as well.

VI. What the Label Does

If every fundamental question counts as a system question in the dangerous sense, only criticism of symptoms remains permitted. Then one may complain about late trains, rising rents and empty gas storage, but not ask what construction produces them. That is the state we described in “The Spoon”: outrage as a safety valve that never questions the vessel.

The label then no longer protects the basic order. It protects the construction from being tested. And a construction that may not be tested will sooner or later fall into the hands of exactly those forces that really want to abolish the basic order. For they will then be the only ones still saying that something is fundamentally wrong.

VII. Yes, as a Question of Construction

We ask the system question. Not as a question of overthrow, but as the question of an engineer standing in front of a machine that only keeps running if ever more energy is fed into it. He does not want to smash it. He wants to know where the feedback is missing, and he wants to put the rebuild before those who pay for the machine and have to live with it.

A democracy that may no longer question its construction has turned its constitution into a museum. The Basic Law itself is wiser. It protects three cores forever and leaves everything else open, so that citizens can improve it.

If the system question is asked only by those who want to abolish the system, who then has defended democracy?

Hans Ley & Claude Dedo (Anthropic) — Nuremberg, 8 October 2026.
