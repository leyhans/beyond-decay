The Father of Europe – Charles the Butcher, or What Are the Foundations of Europe

Europe honours those who unite it with a prize named after Charlemagne. The same Charles had 4,500 prisoners beheaded in a single day and threatened every Saxon who refused baptism with death. Both are documented, and both belong to the picture.

I. The Medal

In May 2026 Mario Draghi received the International Charlemagne Prize in Aachen. The medal shows the oldest surviving seal of the city, from the twelfth century: Charlemagne on his throne. Since 1950 the prize has honoured people who have rendered outstanding service to the unification of Europe. Its namesake is regarded as the first who succeeded at it.

The honorific is older than the prize. In 799, one year before the imperial coronation, a poet at Charles’s court called his lord, in a Latin epic, “Europae venerandus apex, pater optimus”: the revered summit of Europe, the best father. The “Father of Europe” is therefore not an invention of the post-war era. It is court poetry, written for the ruler who paid for it.

Whoever honours a father does not honour his merits alone. He honours the whole person, and that includes how this unity came about.

II. A Word That Is Not Burned

“Sachsenschlächter”, butcher of the Saxons, was a slogan of the German völkisch movement and of parts of the Nazi movement around Alfred Rosenberg. They accused Charles of killing “Germanic” people and subjecting them to a “foreign” faith, and wanted to venerate the Saxon leader Widukind as the truly great one instead. In 1935 the Sachsenhain was built in Verden, a grove of 4,500 boulders that for a time served the SS as a meeting place. Right-wing circles still cultivate this image today.

We have nothing to do with this reading. Our concern is not Germanic peoples, blood or nation. To us the Saxons are people who believed differently and were threatened with death for it. That is an entirely different standard.

Yet the word does not belong to them. In 1994 Karlheinz Deschner, the critic of the church, who had nothing in common with völkisch thinking, gave a chapter of his “Kriminalgeschichte des Christentums” (Criminal History of Christianity) the title “Der Sachsenschlächter”, the butcher of the Saxons.

We refuse to declare names and terms burned merely because they were also used by people with whom we have nothing in common. Whoever lets extremists dictate which words may no longer be used also surrenders to them the facts those words describe. The executions on the Aller are not a right-wing topic. They are recorded in the Royal Frankish Annals, the official historiography of Charles’s own court.

III. Verden, 782

The war against the Saxons began in 772. In the very first campaign Charles had the Irminsul destroyed, the central sanctuary of the Saxons. His biographer Einhard later wrote that no war of the Franks had been longer, crueller or more laborious. With interruptions it lasted 33 years.

In the summer of 782 the Saxons rose again and defeated a Frankish army at the Süntel. Among the dead were two of the king’s envoys, four counts and some twenty other nobles. Charles came with a new army. The Saxons submitted and handed over to him those they declared chiefly responsible. The Annals report that there were 4,500 of them and that on the king’s orders all were beheaded in a single day on the Aller near Verden. Widukind, the leader of the revolt, had escaped to the Danes.

Fairness requires two things. First, the number is disputed among historians. Since the nineteenth century there have been attempts to reduce it or to reinterpret the execution as a resettlement. One scholar suggested that the annalist had copied “a few noughts too many”. In 1935 the church newsletter of the diocese of Osnabrück spoke of the “fairy tale of the ‘Verden blood court’”, and in 1936 the Protestant church historian Karl Bauer declared the word decollare (behead) a copying error for delocare (resettle). Against this stands the fact that four annals from different parts of the realm report the beheading. In the very years in which Rosenberg condemned Charles as a butcher, church voices denied the deed. Both sides bent the same event in opposite directions. None of these readings has prevailed, and more recent historians consider the figure possible, because Franks and Saxons could field armies of tens of thousands. Second, according to the Annals the men of Verden were executed as rebels, not because they refused baptism. That does not make the deed smaller. It was a mass execution of defenceless men who had surrendered, in a single day. But whoever accuses Charles of forced baptism needs different evidence. That evidence exists.

IV. Baptism or Death

The Capitulatio de partibus Saxoniae is a law Charles issued for subjugated Saxony. Its date is placed between 782 and 795. Chapter 8 reads, in substance: any Saxon who hides unbaptised, scorns baptism and wishes to remain a pagan shall be punished by death.

It is not the only death sentence in the text. The law also threatened death to anyone who ate meat during Lent out of contempt for Christianity, and to anyone who burned a dead body according to pagan custom. Whoever broke into a church or killed a priest likewise died. In addition there was the tithe: every Saxon had to hand over a tenth of his yield to the new church.

The law did not protect faith, it enforced it. Baptism became the sign of submission to the Frankish king, and whoever refused it refused obedience. In 797 a second capitulary for Saxony softened the harshest penalties. It did not end the war. In 804, according to the Annals, Charles had all Saxons living beyond the Elbe and in the district of Wihmuodi deported with their wives and children into the Frankish realm. He gave their land to the Slavic Obotrites, his allies.

V. The Objection from His Own Court

The most common objection to any criticism of Charles is familiar: one must not measure him by today’s standards. That is simply how the Middle Ages were.

Against this objection speaks a man who sat at the very centre of Charles’s court: Alcuin of York, his teacher and most important adviser. In 796, when after the Saxons the Avars too were to be subjugated and baptised, Alcuin wrote to Megenfrid, the king’s chamberlain: “Fides quoque, sicut sanctus ait Augustinus, res est voluntaria, non necessaria. Adtrahi poterit homo in fidem, non cogi. Cogi poterit ad baptismum, sed non proficit in fide.” Faith, as Saint Augustine says, is a matter of the will, not of compulsion. A man can be drawn to faith, but not forced. He can be forced to baptism, but that does nothing for faith.

In the same letter Alcuin rebuked the greed with which the tithe was being collected. The wretched Saxons, he wrote in substance, had so often lost the sacrament of baptism because they had never had a foundation of faith in their hearts.

This is the decisive point. There was no need to wait for modern times to know that forced baptism is wrong. A Church Father had said so four hundred years earlier. The same Augustine, however, later also justified state coercion against the Donatists, a breakaway church in North Africa. Both answers were therefore at hand, and Charles’s own adviser reminded him of the milder one. Charles could have known. He chose the sword.

VI. Throne and Altar

On Christmas Day of the year 800 Pope Leo III crowned Charles emperor in St Peter’s. A year earlier Leo had fled from his opponents in Rome to Charles in Paderborn. Charles brought him back with his troops, and in return the Pope gave him the imperial crown. The alliance was a deal on both sides: the king protected the altar, and the altar sanctified the king.

In “The Founders of the Christian Leviathan” we described this alliance as one of the roots of the European order of power. In Saxony it shows its face without make-up. Mission was a means of conquest, and conquest was a means of mission. Whoever refused baptism was pagan and rebel at once, and the law treated both alike. This is the claim to absoluteness we addressed in our open letter to Pope Leo XIV, in its harshest form: one single truth, enforced by the power of the state.

VII. A Saint by the Emperor’s Grace

The story has an epilogue that is seldom told in Aachen. On 29 December 1165 Emperor Frederick Barbarossa had Charles’s remains solemnly elevated in Aachen Cathedral and had him declared a saint. It was carried out with the consent of Paschal III, an antipope whom Barbarossa himself had set up against the legitimate Pope Alexander III. The emperor got his political saint from his own pope.

In 1179 the Third Lateran Council annulled the antipope’s decrees. A canonisation by Rome never took place. Nevertheless Charles is venerated to this day, above all in Aachen Cathedral, where his remains have lain in the golden Shrine of Charlemagne since 1215. Rome has tolerated the cult for more than 800 years without ever confirming or forbidding it.

The man whose law punished the refusal of baptism with death is thus venerated in a church whose highest authority never declared him a saint. Throne and altar raised him up together, and neither has taken him down again.

VIII. What Is Rightly Credited to Him

An essay that lists only the crimes would be as one-sided as a eulogy that conceals them. Charles also created things that lasted. He brought scholars such as Alcuin to his court and in 789, in the Admonitio generalis, demanded that schools be established at monasteries and episcopal sees. His scriptoria produced the Carolingian minuscule, a clear script from which our present-day lower-case letters developed. And there ancient texts were copied that would otherwise have been lost. A large part of the Latin classics has come down to us only in copies from this period.

That is a great deal, and it is real. But it cancels nothing of what happened at Verden and in the law for Saxony. With Charles, education and violence stand side by side, not against each other. The same administration that ordered schools ordered executions.

IX. What Are the Foundations of Europe?

A prize does not choose its namesake by chance. Whoever names Europe after Charlemagne says something about how he imagines the unity of Europe: founded from above, administered from a centre, with a single valid truth and, if need be, with force against those who do not comply. This is the pattern of the megamachine described by Lewis Mumford, which we follow in our series: an order that treats people as components and resistance as a malfunction.

Charles’s court shows, however, that Europe had two foundations from the very beginning, not one. One is the throne: unity through conquest, faith through law, obedience through punishment. The other sits at the same court and writes letters: Alcuin, who insists that people can be drawn to faith but not forced, and the scribes who copy ancient texts whose ideas have nothing to do with their king’s faith. From the first foundation came the empires, the crusades and the forced conversions of later centuries. From the second came, by many detours, freedom of conscience and the right to think differently.

Both are real, and both are European. The question is which one is honoured. A Europe that invoked Alcuin’s sentence would look different from one that invokes the emperor’s throne. That Aachen chose the emperor and not his adviser is no small matter. It shows what kind of unity is meant.
